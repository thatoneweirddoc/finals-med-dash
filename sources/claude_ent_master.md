# ENT Notes — Section 1: The Ear and Hearing Loss
*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic), Otitis Media Guidelines for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Children 2020 (Menzies/CDU), RCH CPGs, Queensland Health/CHQ otitis externa guidelines, Australian Prescriber, AAO-HNS Sudden Hearing Loss guideline, Hearing Australia. Scope: ear anatomy and assessment, infective ear disease, the hearing-loss differential, sudden SNHL, tinnitus and trauma.*

## 1.1 Applied anatomy and the examination

**The short version**
- Outer ear (pinna → canal → drum) and middle ear (drum, ossicles, ET) disease produces **conductive** loss; anything medial to the stapes footplate (cochlea, CN VIII, central) produces **sensorineural** loss.
- Weber lateralises **to** a conductive ear and **away from** a sensorineural ear. Rinne is "negative" (BC > AC) only in conductive loss.
- The **air–bone gap** is the whole game: gap = conductive component; both curves down with no gap = SNHL; both down *and* a gap = mixed.
- Tympanometry: **A** normal; **B** flat = effusion (normal canal volume) or perforation/grommet (large volume); **C** = negative middle-ear pressure from ET dysfunction.
- Describe a drum systematically: colour, translucency, position, light reflex, mobility, perforation (site/size), attic.

**Anatomy that earns its keep.** Outer third of canal cartilaginous; inner two-thirds bony and thin-skinned. The **bony–cartilaginous junction** collects debris and is where necrotising otitis externa granulation appears. The middle ear cleft connects forward to the nasopharynx via the Eustachian tube (shorter, wider, more horizontal in infants — hence paediatric otitis media) and backward to the mastoid air cells (hence mastoiditis). The facial nerve runs along the medial middle-ear wall over the oval window; dehiscent bone here explains facial palsy in middle-ear disease. Chorda tympani crosses the drum medially — ear surgery risks altered taste.

**The normal drum.** Pearly grey, translucent, slightly concave; handle of malleus down and back to the umbo, lateral process bulging anterosuperiorly, cone of light forward and down. Taut **pars tensa** (four quadrants) plus the small **pars flaccida** (attic) above the lateral process — where retraction pockets and cholesteatoma hide.

**Otoscopy.** Largest speculum that fits; pinna up-and-back in adults, down-and-back in infants; good ear first. Pneumatic otoscopy adds most — an immobile drum suggests effusion or perforation.

**Tuning fork tests.** Use **512 Hz** (256 Hz is felt as vibration; 1024 Hz decays too fast). **Weber**: fork on vertex/mid-forehead — ask *where* it is heard. **Rinne**: AC (beside meatus) vs BC (mastoid); AC > BC = "positive" (normal or SNHL); BC > AC = "negative" = conductive loss of roughly ≥20–25 dB.

| Pattern | Weber (forehead) | Rinne left | Rinne right |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Central, heard midline | Positive (AC > BC) | Positive (AC > BC) |
| **Left conductive** | Lateralises to **left** (the deaf ear) | **Negative (BC > AC)** | Positive |
| **Right conductive** | Lateralises to **right** (the deaf ear) | Positive | **Negative (BC > AC)** |
| **Left sensorineural** | Lateralises to **right** (the better ear) | Positive (both reduced) | Positive |
| **Right sensorineural** | Lateralises to **left** (the better ear) | Positive | Positive (both reduced) |

⚑ **The false-negative Rinne trap.** In severe/profound unilateral SNHL ("dead ear") the mastoid fork is heard by the *opposite* cochlea through the skull, so the patient reports BC > AC on the affected side and looks conductive. The giveaway: Weber lateralises to the *opposite* (good) ear. Mask the good ear if the tests disagree.

**Reading a pure tone audiogram.** Frequency (250–8000 Hz) left to right; threshold dB HL top (−10, best) to bottom (120, worst) — **lower on the page = worse hearing**. AC: **O = right, X = left**. BC: **< = right, > = left**. Normal adult ≤20–25 dB HL. Grading: mild 26–40, moderate 41–60, severe 61–90, profound >90 dB.

| Audiogram | Air conduction | Bone conduction | Air–bone gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | ≤20–25 dB | Same | Nil (<10 dB) |
| Conductive | Reduced | **Normal** | **Present (>10–15 dB)** |
| Sensorineural | Reduced | Reduced, tracks AC | **Nil** |
| Mixed | Reduced most | Reduced, but better than AC | Present, on a depressed BC line |

Shape cues: bilateral symmetrical **high-frequency downsloping** = presbycusis; **4 kHz notch with recovery at 8 kHz** = noise-induced; **fluctuating low-frequency SNHL** = Ménière's; **Carhart notch** (BC dip ~2 kHz) with a conductive gap = otosclerosis; **asymmetry >15 dB at two adjacent frequencies**, or speech discrimination worse than expected, = image the internal auditory meatus.

**Tympanometry** measures compliance against applied canal pressure. **Type A**: peak near 0 daPa, normal (*As* shallow = stiff — otosclerosis, tympanosclerosis; *Ad* tall = hypermobile — ossicular discontinuity, flaccid drum). **Type B**: flat — *normal* canal volume = middle ear effusion; *large* volume = perforation or patent grommet. **Type C**: peak shifted negative (beyond about −100 daPa) — Eustachian tube dysfunction.

## 1.2 Otitis externa

**The short version**
- **Pain on tragal pressure or on pulling the pinna is the discriminator** from otitis media — the canal, not the middle ear, is inflamed.
- *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *S. aureus* dominate; **fungal** (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*) typically follows prolonged drop use.
- Treatment is **topical + aural toilet**. ⚑ Oral antibiotics are *not* indicated for uncomplicated diffuse otitis externa.
- ⚑ If the drum is perforated or a grommet is in situ, use **non-ototoxic ciprofloxacin drops**, never aminoglycoside-containing preparations.
- Diabetic/immunosuppressed + severe pain + **granulation at the bony–cartilaginous junction** = necrotising otitis externa until proven otherwise.

**Presentation.** Rapid onset (<48 h) otalgia — often out of proportion to the otoscopic picture — with itch, fullness, scant discharge, and conductive loss if the canal occludes. Signs: **tragal and/or pinna tenderness**, diffuse canal oedema and erythema, debris. Predisposing: swimming, humidity, cotton-bud trauma, hearing aids, eczema/psoriasis. If the drum cannot be seen, a discharging middle ear is not excluded.

**Organisms.** *P. aeruginosa* and *S. aureus*; polymicrobial common. Fungal disease is the classic **treatment-failure** picture after antibacterial drops — fluffy white hyphae with black spores (*Aspergillus niger*) or creamy curd (*Candida*), itch over pain.

**Management (Australian practice).**

| Situation | Topical agent | Regimen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uncomplicated, **intact drum** | Dexamethasone + framycetin + gramicidin | 3 drops tds, 7 days |
| **Perforation or grommet** ⚑ | Ciprofloxacin 0.3% (non-ototoxic) | 5 drops bd, 7 days |
| Severe, marked canal oedema, **intact drum** | Ciprofloxacin + hydrocortisone | 3 drops bd until 2 days after symptoms clear; max 2 weeks. ⚑ **Contraindicated with a perforated drum or a grommet** — use ciprofloxacin 0.3% alone (or cipro+dexamethasone) there |
| **Fungal** | Triamcinolone + neomycin + gramicidin + nystatin; alternatives flumetasone + clioquinol, or clotrimazole 1% cream | 3 drops tds, 7 days |

- **Aural toilet is therapeutic, not cosmetic** — drops cannot work through debris. Dry mopping or microsuction; microsuction preferred, effectively mandatory for fungal disease.
- **Wick** (ribbon gauze or sponge) if canal oedema prevents drop penetration; drops onto the wick, review at 48–72 h.
- Analgesia, keep the ear dry, stop cotton buds, treat any dermatitis.
- **Systemic antibiotics only** if infection extends beyond the canal (pinna/facial cellulitis), the patient is systemically unwell, or significant immunocompromise/diabetes — discuss with ENT.
- No response at 48–72 h: retained debris, occluded canal, fungal overgrowth, contact allergy (neomycin sensitises), or wrong diagnosis.

**Necrotising (malignant) otitis externa.** Skull-base osteomyelitis spreading from the canal — a *Pseudomonas* disease of the **elderly diabetic** or immunosuppressed patient. Suspect when pain is deep, nocturnal and disproportionate, and fails to settle on standard topicals.

- **Sign to quote: granulation tissue at the bony–cartilaginous junction of the canal floor.**
- Progressive **cranial nerve palsies** mark spread: **CN VII first** (poor prognostic sign), then jugular foramen nerves IX/X/XI, then XII; V and VI imply extensive disease.
- Investigate: swab/biopsy of granulation tissue (also excludes canal SCC), **ESR/CRP** to track response, **HRCT** for bony erosion, **MRI** for soft-tissue/intracranial extent; nuclear medicine (Tc-99m to diagnose, Ga-67 to monitor) where available.
- Treat: admit, ENT + infectious diseases, **prolonged systemic antipseudomonal therapy over weeks to months** (ciprofloxacin for bone penetration, or IV antipseudomonal beta-lactam, culture-guided), strict glycaemic control, repeated aural toilet. Duration follows clinical, inflammatory-marker and imaging response, not a fixed course; surgery is limited to debridement/abscess drainage.

## 1.3 Acute otitis media and otitis media with effusion

**The short version**
- Peak 6–24 months; *S. pneumoniae*, non-typeable *H. influenzae*, *M. catarrhalis*, plus viruses.
- Diagnosis needs a **bulging, opaque, immobile drum** with acute symptoms — a red drum in a crying febrile child is not enough.
- ⚑ Any systemically well child **≥6 months** without risk features gets **analgesia and observation** (review/delayed script at 48–72 h), not immediate antibiotics. The under-2 rule below bites only for **bilateral** disease.
- ⚑ **Always give antibiotics**: <6 months; systemically unwell; **bilateral in <2 years**; **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children**; immunocompromised; cochlear implant; only-hearing ear; AOM with discharge.
- First-line **amoxicillin 15 mg/kg (max 500 mg) 8-hourly for 5 days**, or 30 mg/kg (max 1 g) 12-hourly for 5 days.

**Clinical picture.** Otalgia (ear-tugging or unexplained irritability in infants), fever, coryzal prodrome, conductive loss; sudden pain relief with purulent discharge = perforation. Distinguish from otitis externa (tragal tenderness, canal oedema) and referred pain (teeth, TMJ, tonsil, cervical spine).

**Antibiotics.**
- **Analgesia is the priority** — regular paracetamol ± ibuprofen. ⚑ Decongestants, antihistamines and corticosteroids do **not** help.
- Penicillin allergy: cefuroxime (non-severe) or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole/macrolide (severe immediate hypersensitivity) — check current TG.
- Failure at 48–72 h, or recent amoxicillin: amoxicillin–clavulanate, covering beta-lactamase-producing *H. influenzae*/*M. catarrhalis*.
- ⚑ **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children** (Menzies 2020): treat *all* episodes — **amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day for 7 days**; **azithromycin** where adherence is difficult or refrigeration unavailable; escalate to 90 mg/kg/day or amoxicillin–clavulanate if unresolved. AOM **with perforation**: antibiotics until discharge resolves, adding topical ciprofloxacin if larger than pinhole.

**Complications.** **Acute mastoiditis** is the commonest suppurative complication — postauricular swelling, erythema, tenderness, loss of the postauricular crease, pinna pushed **down and out**; admit for IV antibiotics (e.g. third-generation cephalosporin), ENT review, CT if abscess suspected, myringotomy ± cortical mastoidectomy. Also perforation, facial palsy, labyrinthitis, and intracranial spread (meningitis, extradural/brain abscess, lateral sinus thrombosis, Gradenigo syndrome — petrous apicitis with CN VI palsy and retro-orbital pain).

**Otitis media with effusion ("glue ear").** Non-infected middle-ear fluid after AOM or with ET dysfunction. Painless; presents as hearing loss, inattention, loud television, speech/language delay or behavioural concern. Otoscopy: dull, retracted or amber drum, air-fluid level or bubbles, reduced mobility; **Type B tympanogram, normal canal volume**; conductive loss typically 20–30 dB.

- **Natural history is the examinable point** — most resolve spontaneously within about 3 months, so the default is watchful waiting with hearing and language review.
- Consider **grommets** when effusion persists **>3 months** with documented bilateral conductive loss, or at any stage with speech/language delay, developmental concern or retraction pocket. ⚑ Australian prioritisation ranks **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children**, significant bilateral loss and confirmed speech delay as higher-urgency; Menzies sets the hearing-impairment threshold here at an average **>20 dB**.
- Autoinflation (Otovent) may help cooperative children; antibiotics, antihistamines and steroids are not routine.
- Grommets extrude over ~6–12 months; counsel about otorrhoea (topical ciprofloxacin, ⚑ not aminoglycoside). Routine water precautions are no longer mandated for most.

## 1.4 Chronic suppurative otitis media and cholesteatoma

**The short version**
- CSOM = persistent otorrhoea through a perforation with conductive loss — ⚑ **≥6 weeks in the Australian OMA/Menzies definition** (typically with a perforation >2% of pars tensa). The **≥2-week** figure is WHO, not Menzies.
- ⚑ Treat with **aural toilet plus topical ciprofloxacin 5 drops bd** — topical quinolone beats systemic therapy and avoids ototoxicity.
- **Painless, foul-smelling discharge with hearing loss = cholesteatoma until excluded.**
- The dangerous perforations are **attic (pars flaccida)** and **marginal/posterosuperior**; central pars tensa perforations are "safe".
- Cholesteatoma is a **surgical** disease — drops may dry the ear but do not treat it.

**CSOM.** Recurrent or continuous mucopurulent discharge through a non-healing perforation; hearing loss proportional to perforation size and ossicular status. Aural toilet then topical ciprofloxacin; add oral trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole if topical therapy fails. Systemic antibiotics alone are inferior. The long-term cost is educational and developmental — hence audiology follow-up, particularly for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, in whom prevalence is high.

**Cholesteatoma.** Keratinising squamous epithelium trapped medial to the drum — usually from a retraction pocket (acquired), rarely congenital (white mass behind an intact drum in a child). Not a neoplasm, but locally destructive via pressure and osteolytic enzymes.

- **Cues:** scanty, offensive, *painless* discharge unresponsive to drops; progressive conductive loss; crust or granulation in the **attic**; a retraction pocket whose base you cannot see; drum polyp.
- **Why it matters:** ossicular erosion (usually long process of incus); **facial nerve palsy**; **labyrinthine fistula** (lateral SCC) — vertigo on tragal pressure or loud sound (fistula sign/Tullio); SNHL; intracranial sepsis (meningitis, extradural/temporal lobe/cerebellar abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis).
- **Imaging:** **HRCT temporal bones** for extent and bony erosion (scutum, ossicles, tegmen, fallopian canal, lateral SCC); MRI non-EPI DWI separates residual/recurrent cholesteatoma from granulation or fluid.
- **Management:** ENT surgery — atticotomy, canal-wall-up or -down mastoidectomy with tympanoplasty/ossiculoplasty, plus second-look or DWI surveillance. Drops are pre-operative adjuncts only.

## 1.5 Hearing loss by category

**The short version**
- Sort the stem: **conductive vs sensorineural** (Weber/Rinne, air–bone gap), then unilateral vs bilateral, then sudden vs gradual.
- Conductive: wax, foreign body, otitis externa, effusion, perforation, ossicular discontinuity, **otosclerosis**.
- Sensorineural: presbycusis, noise, **ototoxicity**, Ménière's, **vestibular schwannoma**, congenital/infective.
- **Unilateral or asymmetric SNHL demands imaging of the internal auditory meatus.**

| Cause | Discriminating features |
| --- | --- |
| **Conductive** — wax / foreign body | Sudden, after cotton bud or water; obvious on otoscopy. **⚑ Do not irrigate** with perforation, grommet, previous ear surgery or only-hearing ear — cerumenolytic then microsuction |
| Otitis media with effusion | Painless, dull/retracted drum, **Type B tympanogram**, inattentive child |
| Perforation; ossicular discontinuity | Visible defect; or post-traumatic/post-infective large gap with **Ad tympanogram** |
| **Otosclerosis** | Young–middle-aged adult, often female, worse in pregnancy, family history, **paracusis Willisii**, normal drum ± Schwartze sign, **Carhart notch**; aid or stapedotomy |
| **Sensorineural** — presbycusis / noise | Bilateral, gradual; high-frequency slope vs **4 kHz notch** with exposure history |
| **Ototoxicity** | Aminoglycosides (gentamicin vestibulotoxic; amikacin/neomycin cochleotoxic), platinum chemotherapy (cisplatin > carboplatin), loop diuretics (often reversible), high-dose salicylates/quinine (reversible tinnitus), high-dose macrolides. ⚑ Also **aminoglycoside drops through a perforation or grommet** |
| **Ménière's disease** | Recurrent spontaneous vertigo ~20 min–12 h, **fluctuating low/mid-frequency SNHL**, tinnitus and fullness, same ear |
| **Vestibular schwannoma** | **Unilateral** SNHL/tinnitus, discrimination worse than the PTA predicts, unsteadiness; **trigeminal involvement only with larger tumours — but when it comes, a reduced corneal reflex is its earliest sign** (§2.8), facial nerve signs later still; **MRI IAM with gadolinium** |
| Congenital / infective | Congenital CMV (commonest non-genetic), GJB2/connexin-26, rubella; acquired — bacterial meningitis (⚑ urgent implant referral before cochlear ossification), mumps, measles. Universal Australian newborn screening detects most congenital cases |

## 1.6 Sudden sensorineural hearing loss

**The short version**
- Definition: SNHL of **≥30 dB across ≥3 consecutive frequencies within 72 hours**, usually unilateral.
- ⚑ **Weber lateralises AWAY from the affected ear; Rinne stays positive bilaterally** — and the ear looks entirely normal.
- **An otological emergency**, not wait-and-see: audiology same/next day, ENT referral, **high-dose corticosteroids as soon as possible, ideally within 14 days** of onset.
- **MRI of the internal auditory meatus** in all cases to exclude vestibular schwannoma.
- Recovery falls sharply with delay; salvage **intratympanic steroids** if systemic therapy fails or beyond ~2 weeks.

**Why it is missed.** The ear looks normal, so the loss is mislabelled as wax, "fluid" or infection. Most notice it on waking or on lifting the phone to that ear; many report fullness and tinnitus, and up to a third have vertigo (worse prognosis). **Confirm with tuning forks and an urgent audiogram** — do not reassure and review in a fortnight.

**Management.**
- **Urgent PTA** to confirm and quantify; do not delay steroids awaiting imaging.
- **Systemic corticosteroids**: prednisolone approximately **1 mg/kg/day (max ~60 mg) for 7–14 days, then taper**. Benefit is greatest within days, falls markedly after ~2 weeks, occasionally reported to 6 weeks. Counsel re diabetes, dyspepsia, mood, sleep.
- **Intratympanic steroid** as salvage after failed systemic therapy, or first-line if systemic steroids are contraindicated.
- **MRI IAM with contrast** (or ABR/audiometric follow-up if MRI not possible) for retrocochlear pathology. ⚑ **CT head is not the test**; routine screening bloods are not recommended.
- ⚑ **Do not** routinely prescribe antivirals, vasodilators, thrombolytics or antioxidants.
- Repeat audiometry at end of treatment and within ~6 months; refer for rehabilitation if loss persists.

## 1.7 Presbycusis, noise-induced loss, hearing aids and implants

**The short version**
- Presbycusis: bilateral, symmetrical, gradual, high-frequency-first SNHL of ageing; *"I hear but can't understand,"* worst in background noise.
- Noise-induced loss: cumulative, permanent, **preventable** — 4 kHz notch; protection and exposure limits are the intervention.
- Untreated hearing loss is linked with isolation, depression and cognitive decline — a reason to treat, not just document.
- Hearing aids first; **bone-conduction devices** for conductive/mixed loss or chronically discharging ears.
- ⚑ **60/60 rule** for adult cochlear implant referral: better-ear unaided PTA **≥60 dB HL** and best-ear monosyllabic word score **≤60%** — a far lower bar than the old "profound deafness" mindset.

Management = amplification plus communication strategy (face the person, reduce background noise, assistive listening devices). Australian access: the Hearing Services Program funds devices for pensioner concession card holders and DVA clients; Hearing Australia covers under-26s, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people **aged 50+** (or any age if in a Community Development Program). Profound bilateral SNHL found on newborn screening is implanted in infancy, and ⚑ **post-meningitic deafness needs immediate referral**, since cochlear ossification can preclude electrode insertion within weeks.

## 1.8 Tinnitus

**The short version**
- Most tinnitus is **bilateral, non-pulsatile, subjective** and accompanies presbycusis or noise damage — explain, treat the hearing loss, address sleep and anxiety.
- ⚑ **Investigate: unilateral tinnitus; pulsatile tinnitus; tinnitus with asymmetric hearing loss; tinnitus with neurological signs; tinnitus with sudden hearing loss.**
- Unilateral/asymmetric → **audiogram + MRI IAM** (vestibular schwannoma).
- Pulsatile → vascular: glomus tumour (red retrotympanic mass), dural AV fistula, carotid stenosis/dissection, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, high-output states (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy). Examine the drum, auscultate neck and mastoid, check BP and fundi; CT/MR angiography.
- Benign subjective tinnitus: audiology, hearing aids (amplification alone often reduces awareness), sound enrichment, CBT for distress, sleep hygiene, review ototoxic and stimulant drugs. No drug reliably abolishes tinnitus — do not start benzodiazepines.

## 1.9 Perforation, barotrauma, temporal bone fracture and otorrhoea

**The short version**
- Traumatic perforations (slap, blast, cotton bud, water) mostly **heal spontaneously** within weeks to ~3 months.
- ⚑ **Keep the ear dry**; no drops unless infected, and then non-ototoxic ones.
- **Myringoplasty** if the perforation persists beyond ~3 months, causes recurrent infection or significant hearing loss, or the patient needs water exposure.
- Barotrauma: descent/flying with a blocked ET → retraction, haemotympanum, rarely perforation; treat the nasal cause, analgesia, autoinflation.
- Post-trauma **clear otorrhoea = CSF until proven otherwise** — base-of-skull fracture, not an ear infection.

**Perforation.** Record site, size and hearing; larger and posterior perforations lose more. New **vertigo, marked SNHL or facial weakness** after ear trauma suggests ossicular disruption into the oval window or perilymph fistula — urgent ENT.

**Temporal bone fracture.** Suspect with head injury plus **Battle's sign**, haemotympanum, canal bleeding, CSF otorrhoea, facial palsy or hearing loss.

| Feature | Longitudinal (~80%) | Transverse (~20%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Lateral blow | Frontal/occipital blow |
| Hearing loss | **Conductive** (ossicular, haemotympanum) | **Sensorineural** (otic capsule) |
| Vertigo | Uncommon | Common, severe |
| Facial palsy | ~20%, often delayed | ~50%, often immediate |
| Otorrhagia | Common, canal laceration | Less common; drum often intact |

Management: do not syringe, pack or instil drops; head-injury care first; HRCT temporal bone; audiometry once stable. **Immediate complete facial palsy** needs urgent ENT (± decompression); delayed or incomplete palsy is managed conservatively with corticosteroids and eye protection. Most traumatic CSF leaks settle conservatively; persistent leaks need repair. ⚑ Prophylactic antibiotics are not routine.

# ENT Notes — Section 2: Vertigo and the Dizzy Patient
*Sources: Bárány Society diagnostic criteria (BPPV 2015, Ménière's 2015, vestibular migraine 2012/2022, PPPD 2017), ICHD-3, Therapeutic Guidelines (Australia), Australian Prescriber/RACGP AJGP dizziness articles, RACGP HANDI (Epley), AAO-HNSF Ménière's CPG 2020, Cochrane reviews (HINTS 2023, Ménière's pharmacotherapy 2023), Stroke Foundation Australia Living Clinical Guidelines. Scope: adult dizziness for written finals — timing/trigger classification, peripheral causes, and the central mimics you cannot afford to miss.*

## 2.1 The approach to the dizzy patient

**The short version**
- "Dizzy" is a symptom word, not a diagnosis. Old teaching split it four ways: **vertigo** (illusion of movement), **presyncope** (about to faint), **disequilibrium** (unsteady on the feet, symptom lives in the legs not the head), **non-specific lightheadedness**.
- ⚑ That symptom-quality approach is now known to perform badly — patients describe the same pathology in different words on the same day, and symptom quality does not reliably separate peripheral from central.
- The examinable replacement is **TiTrATE**: **Ti**ming, **Tr**iggers, **A**nd **T**argeted **E**xamination. Classify into four vestibular syndromes, then apply the exam that belongs to that syndrome.
- Written stems are built out of timing and triggers because those are the discriminating variables. **Duration of a single attack is the single highest-yield question** — it usually narrows the answer to one or two options before you read the exam findings.

**The four syndromes**

| Syndrome | Defining pattern | Typical causes | Targeted exam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triggered episodic vestibular syndrome (t-EVS) | Brief attacks, always provoked by a specific head/body movement or position; asymptomatic between | • BPPV (commonest)<br>• Orthostatic hypotension<br>• SCDS (sound/pressure-triggered) | Dix–Hallpike, supine roll test, lying-to-standing BP |
| Spontaneous episodic vestibular syndrome (s-EVS) | Attacks arise out of the blue, minutes–hours, well between | • Vestibular migraine<br>• Ménière's<br>• Vertebrobasilar TIA<br>• Panic/arrhythmia | Interictal exam usually normal — diagnosis is historical; audiogram, ECG |
| Acute vestibular syndrome (AVS) | Continuous vertigo, nausea, gait unsteadiness and nystagmus for **days**, worse on movement but not caused by it | • Vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis<br>• Posterior circulation stroke<br>• Demyelination, Wernicke's | **HINTS** (+ hearing), gait, full neuro |
| Chronic vestibular syndrome | Months of dizziness/unsteadiness | • PPPD<br>• Vestibular schwannoma<br>• Bilateral vestibular failure<br>• Cerebellar degeneration, polypharmacy | Romberg, gait, dynamic visual acuity, audiogram, MRI |

**Why duration is so discriminating.** Each disorder has a physiologically constrained attack length: otoconia settle within seconds; hydrops distends and equilibrates over tens of minutes to hours; an infarcted or inflamed vestibular nerve gives a fixed tone imbalance resolving only as central compensation occurs over days. Duration maps almost one-to-one onto mechanism.

**Two traps.** (1) *Positional worsening ≠ positional triggering* — every vestibular disorder feels worse on head movement; BPPV differs because between provocations the patient is symptom-free. Constant vertigo "worse on turning the head" is AVS. (2) *Frequency ≠ duration* — "vertigo for two weeks" may be daily 10-second attacks (BPPV) or 14 continuous days (AVS). Ask the length of one attack.

## 2.2 The duration table

**The short version**
- **Seconds (<1 min), positionally triggered** → BPPV.
- **Minutes, spontaneous** → vertebrobasilar TIA; vestibular migraine can also sit here.
- **20 minutes to 12 hours, with aural symptoms** → Ménière's disease.
- **Days, continuous** → vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis, or posterior circulation stroke.
- **Constant / chronic (weeks–months)** → vestibular schwannoma, bilateral vestibular failure, PPPD, cerebellar disease.

| Attack duration | Most likely | Key discriminators in the stem |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds to <1 min | **BPPV** | • Triggered by rolling over, lying down, looking up, bending<br>• Well between attacks<br>• **No hearing loss, no tinnitus, no neurology**<br>• Dix–Hallpike positive |
| Seconds | Orthostatic hypotension; SCDS | • On standing (with BP drop) or on loud noise/straining |
| Minutes (2–30) | **Vertebrobasilar TIA** | • Vascular risk factors, sudden onset<br>• Diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, visual field loss<br>• Isolated vertigo can still be TIA — recurrent stereotyped attacks are a stroke warning |
| Minutes to hours (5 min – 72 h, variable) | **Vestibular migraine** | • Migraine history, photophobia/phonophobia during attack, head-motion intolerance<br>• Duration deliberately variable |
| **20 min – 12 h** | **Ménière's disease** | • Fluctuating low-frequency SNHL, tinnitus, aural fullness, unilateral |
| Days (24 h – weeks, continuous) | **Vestibular neuritis** (no hearing loss) / **labyrinthitis** (hearing loss) | • Preceding viral illness<br>• Unidirectional horizontal nystagmus<br>• **Abnormal** head impulse to the affected side |
| Days (continuous) | **Posterior circulation stroke** | • **Normal** head impulse, direction-changing or vertical nystagmus, skew<br>• Cannot walk unaided; other posterior fossa signs |
| Constant / chronic | Vestibular schwannoma; bilateral vestibular failure (aminoglycoside); PPPD; cerebellar degeneration | • Schwannoma: asymmetric SNHL + mild imbalance<br>• Bilateral failure: oscillopsia when walking, worse in the dark<br>• PPPD: worse upright and in visually busy environments |

**The past-paper stem.** *"Episodes of severe spinning vertigo lasting under a minute, brought on by rolling over in bed."* Sub-minute + positional trigger + otherwise well = **BPPV**, and the next step is **Dix–Hallpike**, not imaging and not a vestibular sedative.

## 2.3 Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

**The short version**
- Otoconia dislodged from the utricular macula float free in a semicircular canal (**canalithiasis**); ~85–90% posterior canal, usually the dependent ear.
- History: brief spinning triggered by **rolling over in bed, lying down, sitting up, looking up, bending forward**; each attack <1 min; residual "off" feeling between; **no** cochlear or neurological symptoms.
- **Dix–Hallpike** is diagnostic for posterior canal; positive = **latency, upbeat-torsional (geotropic) nystagmus, <1 min duration, fatigability**, with matching vertigo.
- Treatment is **canalith repositioning — Epley**. ⚑ **Vestibular sedatives are not the treatment**; they suppress symptoms without moving the otoconia and impede central compensation.
- Horizontal canal variant (~5–15%): triggered by rolling supine; diagnosed on **supine roll test**; treated with **BBQ/Lempert roll**.

**Pathophysiology.** Free otoconia move under gravity when the canal plane is reoriented, dragging endolymph and deflecting the cupula — hence the latency (particles must start moving), the crescendo–decrescendo course, and reversal of nystagmus on sitting up. Less commonly otoconia adhere to the cupula (**cupulolithiasis**), giving nystagmus with little latency that persists while the position is held.

**Dix–Hallpike technique.** Patient seated so the head will overhang. Turn the head 45° toward the test ear, then lie them rapidly supine with the neck extended ~20–30° below horizontal. Eyes open, watch ≥30 s. Positive for **posterior canal BPPV** of the dependent ear:

| Feature | Expected finding |
|---|---|
| Latency | ~1–5 s (up to ~20 s) before nystagmus starts |
| Direction | **Upbeating with torsional component, top pole beating toward the dependent (affected) ear** |
| Duration | Builds then fades, typically <30 s, always <1 min |
| On sitting up | Nystagmus reverses direction, milder |
| Repetition | **Fatigues** on repeated testing |
| Symptoms | Reproduces the patient's vertigo, often with marked nausea |

Cautions: cervical instability or significant neck disease, severe carotid disease, recent neck/spinal surgery — use a side-lying (Semont-type) positional test instead.

**Epley (canalith repositioning).** Sequential 90° rotations moving the otoconia round the posterior canal into the utricle, holding each position until symptoms settle (~30 s, or 1–2 min in HANDI-style protocols): (1) Dix–Hallpike position on the affected side → (2) rotate head 90° to the opposite side, still extended → (3) roll body onto that shoulder, head facing ~45° floorward → (4) sit up slowly, chin tucked. Repeat if still positive. Most are clear after one or two manoeuvres versus roughly half resolving spontaneously over weeks. Post-procedure positional restrictions add little; Brandt-Daroff exercises are second-line self-treatment.

**Red flags against BPPV.** Hearing loss, headache, focal neurology, non-fatiguing downbeat or purely torsional nystagmus, or positional nystagmus without vertigo → central positional vertigo (posterior fossa/cerebellar lesion); image.

## 2.4 Vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis

**The short version**
- AVS: sudden continuous vertigo, nausea/vomiting, gait instability and nystagmus lasting **days**, peaking within hours, then slowly settling over 1–3 weeks with residual motion sensitivity for longer.
- **Neuritis = vestibular nerve only** (no cochlear symptoms). **Labyrinthitis = neuritis + hearing loss ± tinnitus** (cochlea involved).
- Nystagmus is **unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, fast phase beating away from the affected ear, suppressed by visual fixation** and worse with fixation removed (Frenzel lenses/ophthalmoscope).
- Management: symptomatic vestibular sedative for the **first 24–72 hours only**, antiemetic, fluids, then **early vestibular rehabilitation**.
- ⚑ Corticosteroids improve caloric test recovery but evidence for better patient-centred outcomes is weak — not routine.

**Assessment.** Head impulse is **abnormal (corrective saccade) toward the affected ear** — the reassuring finding. Patients can usually walk, veering to the affected side. Sudden SNHL here demands audiometry and urgent ENT input (and consider AICA infarct — see 2.5).

**Pharmacology.**

| Drug class | Example | Role | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antihistamine (H1) | Promethazine, cinnarizine | Acute vertigo/nausea, **≤72 h** | Sedation, anticholinergic effects; impairs central compensation if continued |
| **Dopamine D2 antagonist** ⚑ (not an antihistamine) | Prochlorperazine | Acute vertigo/nausea, **≤72 h** | Sedation; **dystonia and other extrapyramidal effects, especially in the young and the elderly** — this is why its profile differs from promethazine's |
| Antiemetic | Metoclopramide, ondansetron | Vomiting control | Dystonia (metoclopramide) |
| Benzodiazepine | Diazepam | Severe distress, very short term | Dependence; strongly impairs compensation |

⚑ The single most examinable pharmacological point: **prolonged vestibular suppressants delay central compensation and worsen long-term outcome**. Stop them and start exercises.

**Vestibular rehabilitation.** Gaze-stabilisation and habituation exercises as soon as vomiting settles — good evidence, and the definitive treatment for persisting symptoms. No improvement by 4–6 weeks → reassess for a central cause or evolving PPPD.

## 2.5 Ruling out central causes — the high-stakes bit

**The short version**
- Around 3–5% of ED dizziness presentations turn out to be stroke, and dizziness/vertigo features in a large proportion of posterior circulation strokes, and a minority present with **isolated** vertigo — indistinguishable from neuritis without a structured exam.
- **HINTS** applies **only to AVS** (continuous, ongoing vertigo with nystagmus) — never to BPPV, never to a well patient between episodes.
- ⚑ Counter-intuitive but examinable: a **NORMAL head impulse test in AVS points to a CENTRAL lesion**. The peripheral case is the abnormal one.
- Peripheral pattern = **HI abnormal, unidirectional horizontal nystagmus, no skew**. Any deviation from all three → central.
- **CT is inadequate for the posterior fossa**; **MRI with DWI** is required and can be falsely negative in the first 24–48 h, particularly for small brainstem infarcts.

**HINTS**

| Component | Peripheral (reassuring) | Central (worrying) |
|---|---|---|
| **H**ead **I**mpulse | **Abnormal** — catch-up saccade on rapid head thrust toward affected ear (VOR failed) | **Normal** — eyes stay locked on target (VOR intact, so the lesion is above the labyrinth) |
| **N**ystagmus | Unidirectional, horizontal(-torsional), fast phase always the same way, **suppressed by fixation** | **Direction-changing** on gaze to either side, **vertical** or **pure torsional**, not fixation-suppressed |
| **T**est of **S**kew | Absent — no vertical refixation on alternate cover test | **Present** — vertical corrective movement (skew deviation, brainstem) |

**HINTS-plus** adds bedside hearing: **new unilateral hearing loss in AVS is a central red flag**, because AICA territory infarction takes out the labyrinth (via the internal auditory/labyrinthine artery) and the brainstem together. ⚑ Do not assume hearing loss = benign labyrinthitis.

Performance: with trained examiners HINTS is more sensitive for stroke than early MRI-DWI. ⚑ Accuracy falls substantially in non-specialist hands — a "normal HINTS" from an untrained examiner does not exclude stroke.

**Other central red flags (any one → image and admit)**
- Sudden onset, severe headache or **neck pain** (vertebral dissection — young patient, neck trauma/manipulation).
- **Inability to stand or walk unaided** — strongest single bedside discriminator; neuritis patients veer, cerebellar stroke patients fall.
- Other neurology: diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, hemisensory change, limb ataxia, Horner's.
- Vascular risk factors, AF, age >50 with new isolated vertigo.
- Recurrent stereotyped attacks in a vasculopath (vertebrobasilar TIA — a stroke warning).

**Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome — PICA/vertebral territory**

| Structure infarcted | Sign |
|---|---|
| Vestibular nuclei | Vertigo, nystagmus, vomiting |
| Inferior cerebellar peduncle | Ipsilateral limb ataxia, fall toward the lesion |
| Spinal trigeminal nucleus | **Ipsilateral** facial pain/temperature loss |
| Spinothalamic tract | **Contralateral** body pain/temperature loss (crossed sensory pattern) |
| Nucleus ambiguus | Dysphagia, hoarseness, absent gag |
| Descending sympathetics | Ipsilateral **Horner's** (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) |

Note motor power and dorsal column sensation are typically spared. **AICA** infarction gives the same vertigo plus **ipsilateral SNHL and facial weakness** — the "vertigo with deafness" stroke.

**Imaging.** CT detects haemorrhage but misses most acute posterior fossa infarcts (beam-hardening artefact, small lesions). **MRI-DWI** is the investigation of choice; false-negative rates in the first 24–48 h for small brainstem infarcts are appreciable, so a negative early scan with a central exam does not exclude stroke — repeat imaging or admit. Add CTA/MRA if dissection or vertebrobasilar stenosis is suspected. Manage on the standard Australian acute stroke pathway — time-critical reperfusion assessment applies posteriorly too.

## 2.6 Ménière's disease

**The short version**
- Quartet: **episodic vertigo 20 min – 12 h**, **fluctuating low-frequency SNHL**, **tinnitus**, **aural fullness** — unilateral at onset.
- **Definite** MD requires ≥2 spontaneous vertigo episodes of 20 min–12 h **plus audiometrically documented** low-to-mid frequency SNHL in the affected ear before/during/after an episode, plus fluctuating aural symptoms, not better explained otherwise.
- **Probable** MD: ≥2 episodes of vertigo/dizziness lasting **20 min – 24 h** with fluctuating aural symptoms — audiometric documentation not required.
- Pathology: endolymphatic hydrops. Hearing loss starts low-frequency and fluctuating, becomes fixed and pan-frequency over years; vertigo attacks eventually burn out.
- **Tumarkin otolithic crises**: sudden drop attacks without warning or LOC — late feature, major injury/driving risk.

**Documented hearing loss.** The Bárány audiometric definition requires bone-conduction thresholds worse in the affected ear by ≥30 dB at **two contiguous frequencies below 2000 Hz**. Practical point: **you cannot diagnose definite Ménière's without an audiogram** — the correct "next investigation" in a stem.

**Management ladder**

| Step | Intervention | Evidence position |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reduce dietary salt; limit caffeine, alcohol, nicotine; sleep and stress hygiene | Widely recommended, low-quality evidence; harmless and first-line in Australian practice |
| 2 | Vestibular sedative/antiemetic **for the acute attack only** | Symptomatic; not prophylaxis |
| 3 | **Betahistine** | ⚑ Long-standing first-line, but the BEMED RCT found no difference vs placebo in attack rate and Cochrane rates the evidence very low certainty. Still commonly prescribed in Australia; be able to say the evidence is weak |
| 4 | Thiazide ± K-sparing diuretic (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide–amiloride) | Low-certainty evidence; still used |
| 5 | **Intratympanic corticosteroid** | Non-ablative, hearing-sparing; reasonable before ablation |
| 6 | **Intratympanic gentamicin** (chemical labyrinthectomy) | Effective for intractable vertigo; **risk of hearing loss** — not initial therapy |
| 7 | Surgery: endolymphatic sac decompression, vestibular neurectomy, labyrinthectomy | Refractory disease; labyrinthectomy destroys hearing |
| Adjunct | Vestibular rehab, hearing aids, tinnitus support, **driving advice** | Rehab helps interictal imbalance, not the attacks |

Driving: notify the licensing authority per Austroads *Assessing Fitness to Drive* where attacks are unpredictable or Tumarkin crises occur.

## 2.7 Vestibular migraine

**The short version**
- ⚑ Now recognised as one of the **commonest causes of recurrent spontaneous vertigo**, plausibly more common than Ménière's, and consistently under-diagnosed.
- Criteria: ≥5 episodes of moderate/severe vestibular symptoms lasting **5 minutes to 72 hours**, a current or past history of migraine (with or without aura), and **≥50% of episodes accompanied by ≥1 migrainous feature**.
- Migrainous features: migrainous headache (≥2 of unilateral, pulsating, moderate/severe, aggravated by routine activity); **photophobia and phonophobia**; **visual aura**.
- Vertigo need **not** occur with headache — that is precisely why it is missed.

Clues: lifelong motion sickness, head-motion intolerance, visual-motion sensitivity (supermarket aisles), recognised migraine triggers, normal audiogram. Overlap with Ménière's is real; the discriminator is **documented fluctuating low-frequency SNHL** (Ménière's) versus **normal hearing plus migrainous features** (VM). Manage on migraine principles — triggers, acute treatment, prophylaxis (propranolol, amitriptyline, candesartan, topiramate) if frequent, plus vestibular rehabilitation.

## 2.8 Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)

**The short version**
- Benign Schwann cell tumour of the vestibular division of CN VIII, in the internal auditory meatus/cerebellopontine angle.
- **Asymmetric/unilateral progressive SNHL** ± **unilateral tinnitus** with **disproportionately mild imbalance** — slow growth allows central compensation, so true rotatory vertigo is uncommon.
- **Absent/reduced corneal reflex** (CN V) is a classic early CPA sign as the tumour enlarges; later facial numbness, then facial weakness (CN VII is relatively resistant).
- Investigation of choice: **MRI internal auditory meati with gadolinium**. Audiogram first, MRI regardless if asymmetry is unexplained.
- **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas = neurofibromatosis type 2** until proven otherwise.

Any **unexplained asymmetric SNHL, unilateral tinnitus or sudden SNHL** warrants MRI to exclude a schwannoma — a high-yield "next investigation" answer. Large tumours give cerebellar signs, raised ICP and hydrocephalus. Management: serial MRI surveillance for small non-growing tumours, stereotactic radiosurgery, or microsurgery.

## 2.9 Other causes worth a line

| Condition | Recognition cue | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Orthostatic hypotension / drugs** | Lightheaded **on standing**, seconds, resolves on sitting | Drop ≥20 systolic / ≥10 diastolic mmHg within 3 min. Culprits: antihypertensives, α-blockers, nitrates, diuretics, TCAs, antipsychotics, dopaminergics, alcohol. Review meds in every older dizzy patient |
| **Bilateral vestibular failure** | Gentamicin exposure; **oscillopsia** when walking, worse in the dark or on uneven ground | Bilaterally abnormal head impulse; Romberg positive eyes closed. Rehab, not drugs |
| **Cervicogenic dizziness** | Non-spinning unsteadiness with neck pain and restricted range | Diagnosis of exclusion — exclude dissection and central causes first |
| **Perilymph fistula** | Vertigo ± hearing loss after barotrauma, straining, head injury or ear surgery | Symptoms on Valsalva/pressure; conservative, then surgical repair |
| **Superior semicircular canal dehiscence** | ⚑ **Tullio phenomenon** (loud sound → vertigo/oscillopsia), pressure-induced vertigo (Hennebert), **autophony**, hearing own footsteps/eye movements | Low-frequency **conductive** loss on audiogram but **normal tympanometry and intact acoustic reflexes** (a "conductive" loss with a normal middle ear). Diagnose on high-resolution temporal bone CT with reformats; VEMP thresholds low |
| **PPPD** | Non-spinning dizziness/unsteadiness **most days for ≥3 months**, worse **upright**, with **motion**, and in **complex visual environments** | Precipitated by a preceding vestibular/medical/psychological event. Treat with vestibular rehabilitation, SSRI/SNRI and CBT — **not** vestibular sedatives |

# ENT Notes — Section 3: The Nose and Paranasal Sinuses
*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic) and Australian Prescriber 2023; ASCIA Allergic Rhinitis Clinical Update 2024; RACGP "An update on epistaxis" 2015; SA Health ENT Clinical Prioritisation Criteria; RCH Melbourne CPG Periorbital/Orbital Cellulitis; EPOS 2020; TGA montelukast advisory. Scope: written-finals rhinology — anatomy, epistaxis, acute and chronic rhinosinusitis and complications, allergic rhinitis, nasal obstruction and trauma, sinonasal and nasopharyngeal malignancy, anosmia.*

## 3.1 Applied anatomy

**The short version**
- Anterior septum carries a confluence of vessels (**Little's area / Kiesselbach's plexus**) — site of >90% of nosebleeds.
- Posterior bleeding is **sphenopalatine artery** territory (terminal maxillary a., ECA), entering behind the middle turbinate.
- Dual carotid supply: ECA (sphenopalatine, greater palatine, superior labial) + ICA (ethmoidals via ophthalmic a.) — hence ECA embolisation cannot address an ethmoidal bleed.
- **Ostiomeatal complex (OMC)** in the middle meatus is the common drainage path for frontal, maxillary and anterior ethmoid sinuses — block it and all three obstruct.
- **Olfactory cleft** sits high between superior septum and middle turbinate; obstructive anosmia follows polyp or oedema there.

**Little's area.** Anteroinferior septal mucosa: thin, adherent to cartilage, dried by airflow, reachable by a finger. Contributors: anterior ethmoidal (ophthalmic, i.e. **ICA**), sphenopalatine septal and greater palatine branches (maxillary/**ECA**), superior labial (facial/ECA). The RACGP review attributes ~80% of nasal vault supply to the sphenopalatine and greater palatine arteries.

**Posterior supply.** The sphenopalatine artery emerges at the posterior end of the middle turbinate. Bleeding is brisk, poorly visualised with a headlight, and drains into the pharynx — the target of **endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation**, controlling ~98% of posterior epistaxis.

**Ostiomeatal complex.** Maxillary ostium, ethmoidal infundibulum, uncinate process, ethmoid bulla, hiatus semilunaris. Mucociliary clearance beats *towards the natural ostium* regardless of gravity — an inferior accessory ostium does not drain the maxillary sinus. Obstruction → stasis, falling intrasinus O₂, ciliary dysfunction → bacterial colonisation; hence the rationale for FESS (restore ostial patency, don't strip mucosa). ⚑ Posterior ethmoid and sphenoid drain **superiorly** (superior meatus, sphenoethmoidal recess) and are *not* OMC — sphenoid disease can present with a normal middle meatus and isolated vertex/retro-orbital headache.

**Olfactory cleft.** Fibres pierce the cribriform plate — fracture or FESS injury risks CSF rhinorrhoea and meningitis. Conductive loss (polyps, CRS) often responds to steroid; sensorineural loss (post-viral, head injury) does not.

## 3.2 Epistaxis

**The short version**
- >90% anterior, from Little's area; posterior bleeds affect older patients, are brisker, look bilateral and run down the throat.
- Hypertension is **rarely the direct cause** — treat the bleed, don't blame the BP.
- Anticoagulants/antiplatelets raise severity and recurrence, not usually causation.
- **Unilateral epistaxis + progressive unilateral obstruction in an adolescent male = JNA until excluded — image, refer, NEVER biopsy.**
- First aid: sit up, lean forward, pinch the **soft cartilaginous** part continuously ≥10 min.

### Anterior vs posterior

| Feature | Anterior | Posterior |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | >90% | ~10% |
| Typical patient | • Children, young adults • Digital trauma, dry air | • Older, arteriopathic • Anticoagulant/antiplatelet |
| Source | Little's area / Kiesselbach's | Sphenopalatine branches, posterolateral wall |
| Presentation | • Unilateral drip • Visible bleeding point | • Blood from both nares and down the oropharynx • Haematemesis, melaena |
| Control | Pressure ± cautery | Fails first aid; needs posterior tamponade, SPAL or embolisation |
| Risk | Nuisance | Aspiration, hypovolaemia, admission |

### Causes
- **Local**: digital trauma (commonest in children), nose blowing, dry/heated or air-conditioned air, fracture, foreign body, steroid spray aimed at the septum, cocaine.
- **Mucosal/structural**: septal deviation with turbulent airflow over the spur; **septal perforation** (margins crust and rebleed — prior cautery/surgery, cocaine, trauma, GPA); rhinitis, rhinosinusitis.
- **Systemic**: anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, thrombocytopenia, liver disease, haemophilia/von Willebrand, **hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia** (recurrent bleeds, lip/tongue/finger telangiectasia, family history, iron deficiency).
- **Neoplastic**: JNA, sinonasal SCC, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, inverted papilloma.
- ⚑ **Hypertension**: associated with more severe bleeds and underlying vasculopathy but "rarely the direct cause". Don't withhold haemostasis to chase a BP number — acute readings reflect anxiety and pain.

### The must-not-miss: juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA)
Benign, locally destructive, highly vascular tumour arising near the sphenopalatine foramen/pterygopalatine fossa, almost exclusively in **adolescent males**. Triad: recurrent **unilateral** epistaxis, progressive **unilateral obstruction**, nasopharyngeal mass. Later: facial swelling, proptosis, cranial neuropathy.
- **Do not biopsy** — catastrophic haemorrhage. Diagnosis is radiological (contrast CT/MRI: avidly enhancing mass, widened sphenopalatine foramen/pterygopalatine fossa).
- Preoperative embolisation then endoscopic or open excision, tertiary centre.
- ⚑ SA Health prioritisation lists suspected JNA as **Category 2 (≤90 days)** and suspected tumour on imaging as **Category 1 (≤30 days)** — but active heavy bleeding is an ED problem.

### Stepwise management
1. **ABC and resuscitate** if brisk — IV access, FBC, group and hold, coagulation profile if anticoagulated or bleeding heavily.
2. **First aid** — sit upright, lean **forwards** (reduces aspiration), pinch the **cartilaginous** part (not the bony bridge — the RACGP review specifically criticises pinching over the nasal bones), continuously **≥10 minutes** without releasing to check. Spit blood out; ice is comfort only.
3. **Topical vasoconstrictor ± anaesthetic** — co-phenylcaine (lignocaine 5% + phenylephrine 0.5%) or oxymetazoline pledget, then re-examine with light and suction; clot must be cleared to see the point.
4. **Chemical cautery** — silver nitrate to the vessel and immediately around it, seconds only, one side. ⚑ Avoid **bilateral septal cautery at one sitting** (perforation risk). Emollient ointment after; avoid picking, hot showers, heavy lifting.
5. **Anterior packing** — tampon, inflatable pack (may stay ~3–4 days) or ribbon gauze, along the **floor** of the nose directed posteriorly, not upwards. Consider antibiotic cover if prolonged.
6. **Posterior packing / balloon** — Foley or posterior balloon plus anterior pack; needs **admission with monitoring** (vagal response, hypoxia, pressure necrosis) and ENT.
7. **Surgical/radiological** — endoscopic **SPA ligation** (~98% control, increasingly preferred over prolonged packing), anterior ethmoidal ligation for high bleeds, or **embolisation** (stroke/blindness risk up to ~4%).

⚑ **Tranexamic acid**: the **NoPAC** RCT found topical TXA did **not** reduce the need for anterior packing versus placebo (44% vs 41%). Don't cite it as a guideline-backed step; systemic TXA retains a role in coagulopathy/HHT on specialist advice.

### The anticoagulated patient
- Establish drug, indication, last dose, thrombotic risk (mechanical valve, recent VTE/stent) — reversal is a trade-off, not a reflex.
- **Warfarin**: check INR; mechanical measures first. **Vitamin K 5–10 mg IV + 4-factor PCC (Beriplex) 50 IU/kg** (capped at 100 kg; 25–50 IU/kg for lesser bleeding) only for life-threatening bleeding or markedly raised INR — ⚑ **no FFP**. **What changed:** the **3-factor Prothrombinex-VF** was discontinued around **June 2024** and replaced by Beriplex (4-factor), and FFP — which existed only to supply the missing factor VII — is no longer required; most texts still print "Prothrombinex + FFP", so recognise that as the **old** answer. Full treatment: **Critical Care §3.14**.
- **DOACs**: last dose and renal function. **Idarucizumab** (dabigatran) for life-threatening haemorrhage — unchanged. ⚑ For **factor Xa inhibitors**, Australian reversal is now **PCC-based (Beriplex)**: **andexanet alfa was removed from the ARTG on 20 May 2026**, so the older "andexanet first" answer is superseded. See **Critical Care §3.14**.
- **Antiplatelets**: no useful reversal; platelets only for critical bleeding.
- ⚑ Most epistaxis on anticoagulants is controlled by pressure, cautery or packing **without** reversal — treat the nose, don't reflexively stop the drug.

## 3.3 Acute rhinosinusitis (ARS)

**The short version**
- ≥2 symptoms, one being **nasal blockage or discharge**, ± facial pain/pressure, ± reduced smell, for <12 weeks.
- Overwhelmingly **viral**; Therapeutic Guidelines: antibiotics "required rarely".
- Bacterial suggested by ≥3 of: fever >38°C, **double sickening**, unilateral disease, severe pain, raised ESR/CRP (EPOS 2020).
- Symptomatic care is the answer: analgesia, saline irrigation, intranasal corticosteroid; topical decongestant **3–5 days max**.
- When antibiotics are justified: **amoxicillin 500 mg orally 8-hourly for 5 days** (adult, Australian).

**Viral vs bacterial.** No single feature separates them — purulence and fever alone do **not** mean bacterial infection, and green mucus is not an antibiotic indication (it reflects neutrophil myeloperoxidase). ⚑ The commonest exam trap here. Peaking then improving by day 5–7 = viral; persisting **beyond ~10 days without improvement**, **worsening after initial improvement (double sickening)**, or severe from onset with high fever and unilateral facial pain = consider bacterial.

**Symptomatic management**
| Measure | Comment |
|---|---|
| Analgesia | Paracetamol ± NSAID — most useful single intervention for facial pain |
| Saline irrigation | Low risk; evidence strongest in children |
| Intranasal corticosteroid | Small effect in post-viral ARS (EPOS 2020) — reasonable, not mandatory |
| Topical decongestant | Symptom relief only; ⚑ **3–5 days** max — beyond this, **rhinitis medicamentosa** (rebound congestion, receptor downregulation) |
| Oral decongestant, antihistamine | Little benefit unless coexisting AR; pseudoephedrine has cardiovascular and misuse concerns |
| Steam, mucolytics | Comfort only |

**When antibiotics are indicated (Australia).** Reserve for symptoms persisting **beyond 10 days** without improvement, high fever >3 days, biphasic illness, severe systemic illness, immunocompromise, or suspected complication. First line **amoxicillin 500 mg orally 8-hourly for 5 days** (children by weight); doxycycline is an adult alternative in non-immediate penicillin hypersensitivity (not children or pregnancy). ⚑ Australia does **not** default to amoxicillin–clavulanate as some US guidance does, and 5-day courses match longer ones.

## 3.4 Complications of sinusitis

**The short version**
- Complications track anatomy: through the **lamina papyracea** to the orbit, through frontal bone to Pott's puffy tumour, via valveless veins to cavernous sinus and cranium.
- **Preseptal (periorbital)** cellulitis = lid swelling and erythema with a **normal globe**.
- **Orbital (postseptal)** cellulitis = **proptosis, painful or restricted eye movements, reduced acuity, diplopia, RAPD** — these discriminators are the exam answer.
- Suspected postseptal disease → urgent **contrast CT orbits and sinuses ± brain**, IV antibiotics, same-day ENT and ophthalmology.

**Chandler classification**: I preseptal → II orbital cellulitis → III **subperiosteal abscess** → IV orbital abscess → V **cavernous sinus thrombosis**.

| Complication | Recognition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Preseptal cellulitis | Lid oedema/erythema; movements full, acuity normal, no proptosis | Oral antibiotics, review 24–48 h; admit if young, unwell or eye not assessable |
| Orbital cellulitis | Proptosis, painful/restricted movements, ↓acuity, RAPD, chemosis | Emergency: IV ceftriaxone/cefotaxime (± vancomycin if MRSA), contrast CT, ENT + ophthalmology, surgery if abscess |
| Subperiosteal abscess | Usually medial (ethmoids); globe displaced laterally/inferiorly | Drain if large, non-medial, visual compromise, or IV failure |
| Cavernous sinus thrombosis | **Bilateral** eye signs, III/IV/V₁–V₂/VI palsies, headache, swinging fever, meningism | MRI/MRV; IV antibiotics; anticoagulation on specialist advice; high mortality |
| Pott's puffy tumour | **Doughy forehead swelling** with frontal sinusitis, usually adolescent | CT + MRI (intracranial extension common); prolonged IV antibiotics + drainage |
| Intracranial extension | Meningitis, epidural/subdural empyema, frontal abscess — headache, vomiting, seizures, ↓GCS | MRI; urgent neurosurgery |

⚑ In children, unilateral eye swelling with fever after a URTI is the classic stem. If the child will not open the eye, or acuity and movements cannot be documented, treat as postseptal and image.

## 3.5 Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS)

**The short version**
- Same symptom pair as ARS but **≥12 weeks**, plus objective endoscopic or CT evidence (polyps, middle meatal mucopus/oedema, OMC or sinus mucosal change).
- Phenotype by polyps: **CRSsNP** vs **CRSwNP**; also primary vs secondary (CF, primary ciliary dyskinesia, immunodeficiency, GPA/EGPA).
- Backbone: **long-term intranasal corticosteroid + saline irrigation** — high-quality evidence, larger effect in CRSwNP.
- FESS only after failure of ≥8 weeks of adequate medical therapy with CT change.
- **Samter's triad / AERD** = nasal polyps + asthma + NSAID sensitivity; aggressive recurrent polyps.

**Medical management.** Topical corticosteroid (spray, or added to large-volume irrigation in specialist practice), daily isotonic saline irrigation, and 1–2 short oral corticosteroid courses per year for uncontrolled disease (mainly CRSwNP). Prolonged low-dose macrolide is used by some specialists in CRSsNP with normal IgE. Treat coexisting AR and asthma. Investigate secondary causes if disease is unilateral, paediatric, refractory, or systemic features are present (ANCA, eosinophilia, bronchiectasis).

**Surgery.** FESS follows documented failure of medical therapy — EPOS frames this as ≥8 weeks of adequate treatment plus CT change (Lund–Mackay ≥1) and persistent burden (SNOT-22 ≥20). It restores drainage and improves topical delivery; not curative in eosinophilic disease, and polyps recur without ongoing topical steroid. ⚑ Type 2 **biologics** (dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab) are now established for severe recurrent CRSwNP — older texts offer only revision surgery.

**Samter's triad / AERD.** Polyposis + asthma + hypersensitivity to aspirin and other COX-1 inhibitors, driven by dysregulated arachidonic acid metabolism (cysteinyl leukotriene excess), **not** IgE. Adult onset; polyps recur rapidly post-surgery; asthma often severe. Reaction = bronchospasm and profuse rhinorrhoea within an hour or two. Avoid COX-1 inhibitors (selective COX-2 usually tolerated after specialist assessment); maximal topical therapy, LTRA, surgery, and **aspirin desensitisation** in selected patients.

## 3.6 Allergic rhinitis (AR)

**The short version**
- Classify by timing (seasonal/perennial/occupational) and by ARIA (intermittent vs persistent; mild vs moderate–severe).
- Signs: pale boggy turbinates, clear rhinorrhoea, allergic shiners, transverse nasal crease, conjunctival injection.
- **Intranasal corticosteroid is first-line for persistent or moderate–severe disease** (ASCIA wording), and also for antihistamine failure. It is widely taught as the single most effective agent, but ASCIA itself does not make that superiority claim.
- Technique: **contralateral hand, aimed away from the septum**, no hard sniffing.
- "One airway": AR and asthma are one inflammatory process at two levels — always ask about wheeze.

**Classification.** Intermittent = <4 days/week **or** <4 weeks; persistent = ≥4 days/week **and** ≥4 weeks. Moderate–severe = disturbed sleep or impaired daily activity/school/work.

**Management ladder (ASCIA)**
| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| Allergen minimisation | Dust mite measures, pet exclusion, pollen strategies — modest alone |
| Saline | Safe adjunct, especially children and pregnancy |
| **Intranasal corticosteroid** | First line for persistent/moderate–severe; days to full effect, so **regular use, not PRN**; irritation and epistaxis are the main adverse effects |
| Non-sedating oral antihistamine | Mild intermittent symptoms or add-on. ⚑ **Sedating antihistamines discouraged** — poor sleep quality, next-day impairment, driving risk, paradoxical effects in children |
| Intranasal antihistamine | Onset ~30 min — useful as rescue |
| Combination INCS + antihistamine spray | Better than either alone; moderate–severe or poorly controlled disease |
| Montelukast (LTRA) | Second line; no added benefit over INCS alone. ⚑ **Boxed warning for serious neuropsychiatric effects** — this is an **FDA** action (2020); the TGA has issued safety advice rather than a boxed warning, so do not attribute a "TGA boxed warning" in an answer (mood change, sleep disturbance, suicidal ideation) |
| Decongestants | Topical ≤3–5 days only — rhinitis medicamentosa |
| Systemic corticosteroid | Short course, rarely. ⚑ Depot IM corticosteroid for hay fever is **no longer acceptable practice** in Australia |
| Immunotherapy (SCIT/SLIT) | Uncontrolled despite optimal pharmacotherapy, intolerable side effects, wish to reduce medication, unavoidable or occupational allergen. ~50% symptom/medication reduction; disease-modifying over 3–5 years |

**Spray technique** — worth a mark: shake, clear the nose, head slightly forward, insert just inside the nostril with the **opposite hand** so the tip points **laterally towards the same-side eye, away from the septum**, spray and breathe gently in — no hard sniffing. Reduces epistaxis and perforation, improves deposition.

**One airway.** 50–80% of people with asthma have AR; treating the nose improves asthma control. Conversely, poorly controlled asthma with nasal polyps should raise AERD.

## 3.7 Nasal obstruction and structural causes

**The short version**
- Bilateral, alternating, worse supine = mucosal/turbinate/rhinitic. Fixed unilateral = structural or sinister.
- ⚑ **Persistent unilateral obstruction in an adult — especially with bloody or foul discharge, facial numbness or epiphora — is a red flag for sinonasal neoplasm; nasoendoscopy required.**
- Child with **unilateral offensive purulent discharge** = nasal foreign body until proven otherwise.
- **Septal haematoma** after trauma needs urgent drainage — cartilage necrosis → saddle nose.

| Cause | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Septal deviation | Fixed unilateral obstruction, often post-traumatic | Septoplasty if symptomatic after medical therapy |
| Turbinate hypertrophy | Bilateral, fluctuating, worse supine and with rhinitis | INCS first; turbinate reduction if refractory |
| Adenoidal hypertrophy (children) | Mouth breathing, snoring, hyponasal speech, OSA, recurrent otitis media with effusion | Adenoidectomy ± tonsillectomy if OSA or morbidity |
| Nasal polyps | Bilateral, painless, insensate pale masses, hyposmia | INCS, treat as CRSwNP. ⚑ A **unilateral** polyp is not CRSwNP — image and biopsy (inverted papilloma, malignancy); in a child exclude encephalocoele/glioma first |
| Foreign body (child) | Unilateral **foul-smelling purulent/bloody** discharge over days–weeks | Removal; button battery is an emergency (perforation within hours) |
| Choanal atresia | Bilateral: neonatal cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying. Unilateral: later rhinorrhoea | Catheter fails to pass; CT; surgical repair |
| Septal haematoma | Post-trauma obstruction, boggy fluctuant cherry-red septal swelling, pain | **Urgent incision, drainage, quilting + antibiotics** — untreated → cartilage necrosis, septal abscess, **saddle nose** |

## 3.8 Nasal trauma and fracture

**The short version**
- Clinical diagnosis; **plain radiographs are not indicated** for isolated nasal fracture.
- Examine the **septum every time** for haematoma — the time-critical finding.
- Review at ~5–7 days once swelling settles to judge deformity; closed reduction usually within ~10–14 days, before the fracture sets.
- Exclude CSF rhinorrhoea, other facial fractures, orbital and cervical spine injury.

Assess mechanism, epistaxis, obstruction, prior nasal shape (photographs help) and external deviation. Clear rhinorrhoea suggests **CSF leak** (anterior skull base fracture — urgent referral, do not pack blindly); diplopia or infraorbital numbness suggests orbital/zygomatic fracture (image); check occlusion. ⚑ Referral is driven by deformity and the timing window, not imaging; refer children earlier (faster remodelling, growth-centre injury).

## 3.9 Nasopharyngeal carcinoma and sinonasal malignancy

**The short version**
- Markedly higher incidence in **southern Chinese** populations (also Southeast Asian, Inuit); strongly **EBV**-associated (undifferentiated non-keratinising type); male predominance.
- Arises in the **fossa of Rosenmüller**, obstructing the Eustachian tube.
- ⚑ **Unilateral serous otitis media in an adult mandates nasopharyngeal examination** — the classic first sign.
- Painless upper cervical **neck node** is a very common presentation; cranial neuropathies (VI most often) in ~20%.
- Diagnosis: nasoendoscopy + biopsy, MRI for local/skull-base staging, EBV serology/plasma EBV DNA. Treatment: radiotherapy ± concurrent cisplatin.

Other features: blood-stained discharge, unilateral obstruction, headache, hearing loss, aural fullness. Trotter's triad — unilateral conductive deafness, ipsilateral V₃ pain/numbness, palatal immobility. **Sinonasal malignancy** (SCC commonest; adenocarcinoma with hardwood dust; esthesioneuroblastoma; melanoma) presents late with unilateral obstruction, epistaxis, facial pain or numbness, epiphora, proptosis or a palatal mass. Inverted papilloma is benign but locally aggressive with malignant transformation risk.

## 3.10 Anosmia

**The short version**
- Split causes into **conductive** (airflow can't reach the olfactory cleft) and **sensorineural** (epithelium or central pathway).
- Commonest: sinonasal disease (CRSwNP, allergic rhinitis), **post-viral**, and head injury (shearing of fibres at the cribriform plate — often permanent).
- Post-viral anosmia recovers gradually over months in most; olfactory training is the mainstay of therapy.
- ⚑ Image (**MRI**) when loss is **unilateral**, progressive, associated with neurological signs or headache, or when there is no sinonasal or viral explanation.

Also: ageing, smoking, drugs, Parkinson and Alzheimer disease (early hyposmia is characteristic), olfactory groove meningioma, Kallmann syndrome (congenital anosmia + hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism). CT sinuses if inflammatory sinonasal disease; MRI brain/olfactory bulbs if central or neoplastic. A short corticosteroid course is diagnostically useful — improvement implies a reversible conductive component.

## 4.1 Sore throat

**The short version**

- Most acute pharyngitis is viral. Group A *Streptococcus* (GAS, *S. pyogenes*) causes ~15–30% in children, ~5–15% in adults.
- Clinical scores (Centor/McIsaac, FeverPAIN) **cannot** reliably separate GAS from viral — they shift pre-test probability only. Throat swab culture is the Australian reference standard.
- ⚑ **Australian practice diverges from the UK.** In the general low-risk population sore throat is managed **symptomatically** — antibiotics are not routinely given even with a high Centor score, because the sole justification (preventing ARF) does not apply.
- ⚑ In people at **high risk of acute rheumatic fever**, antibiotics are given for *every* sore throat **irrespective of clinical features or score** — swab, but do not wait for the result.
- ⚑ **First-line in a high-risk patient is benzathine benzylpenicillin G IM as a single dose** — RHDAustralia prefers IM because adherence is guaranteed and it continues the agent used for secondary prophylaxis. Oral **phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg (child 15 mg/kg up to 500 mg) 12-hourly for 10 days** is the alternative when IM is not possible; the full 10 days is what eradicates carriage.

**Viral vs GAS**

| | Suggests viral | Suggests GAS |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | • cough • coryza • hoarseness • conjunctivitis • oral ulcers • diarrhoea | • abrupt onset • fever • odynophagia • headache • abdominal pain/vomiting (children) |
| Signs | diffuse erythema, no exudate | tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, palatal petechiae, scarlatiniform rash |

**Scores**

*Centor (modified/McIsaac)* — 1 point each: tonsillar exudate/swelling; tender anterior cervical adenopathy; fever >38°C; **absence** of cough; plus age adjustment (+1 if 3–14 yrs, 0 if 15–44, **−1 if ≥45**). Valid only for pharyngitis ≤3 days' duration.

*FeverPAIN* — 1 point each: **F**ever in past 24 h; **P**urulence; **A**ttend rapidly (onset ≤3 days); severely **I**nflamed tonsils; **N**o cough or coryza. UK-derived, drives delayed/immediate scripts there. ⚑ Australia does not use FeverPAIN to drive prescribing.

**⚑ The Australian ARF-risk position — the examinable point**

High-risk groups (RHDAustralia, 3rd edn 2025) — ⚑ note the guideline defines risk by **setting and living circumstances**, not by ethnicity alone:

- **First Nations peoples living in rural or remote settings**; and **First Nations, Māori or Pacific Islander peoples in metropolitan households affected by crowding and/or lower socioeconomic status**. Also anyone in a community with **ARF incidence >30/100,000/year in 5–14-year-olds, or all-age RHD prevalence >2/1000**.
- Migrants and refugees from ARF-endemic settings.
- Anyone with a **personal history of ARF or RHD who is aged <40** — recurrence risk is the highest of all.

For these patients: **swab, then treat immediately** — do not withhold pending culture, do not apply Centor. ARF follows GAS pharyngitis after **~2–3 weeks** (Sydenham chorea later, typically 6–9 weeks). Partial courses can abolish symptoms while leaving the organism, so the immunological trigger persists — hence the full 10 days.

| Setting | Regimen |
|---|---|
| First-line oral | Phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg (child 15 mg/kg, max 500 mg) PO **12-hourly × 10 days** |
| **Preferred in high-risk patients (RHDAustralia); also when adherence is a concern or oral not tolerated** | **Benzathine benzylpenicillin G IM, single dose** — ≥20 kg: 1,200,000 units (900 mg); 10–<20 kg: 600,000 units; <10 kg: 450,000 units |
| Simpler oral alternative (children) | Amoxicillin **25 mg/kg (max 1 g) once daily × 10 days** |
| Non-severe (delayed, non-immediate) penicillin hypersensitivity | Cefalexin 1 g (child 25 mg/kg up to 1 g) 12-hourly × 10 days |
| Immediate/severe penicillin hypersensitivity | Azithromycin 500 mg (child 12 mg/kg up to 500 mg) daily × 5 days |

⚑ **Do not use amoxicillin/ampicillin empirically in an undifferentiated adolescent sore throat** — if it is EBV, you cause a rash (4.2).

Complications: suppurative (peritonsillar/deep neck abscess, otitis media, sinusitis) vs non-suppurative (**ARF**; post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis — antibiotics prevent ARF but **not** PSGN). Scarlet fever = GAS erythrogenic toxin: sandpaper rash, strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, Pastia's lines. Secondary prophylaxis after ARF: benzathine benzylpenicillin G IM **every 28 days** (21 days only in defined high-risk circumstances, not routinely). ⚑ Duration is by severity, and the ARF-without-RHD figure is **5 years**, not 10: **ARF, no RHD → 5 years or until age 21, whichever is longer; mild RHD → 10 years or until 25; moderate RHD → until 40; severe RHD → lifelong.**

## 4.2 Infectious mononucleosis

**The short version**

- EBV (>90%); CMV, toxoplasma and acute HIV are the mimics. Adolescents/young adults.
- Looks like severe streptococcal tonsillitis but with **prolonged fatigue, generalised/posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, palatal petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly**.
- ⚑ **Amoxicillin/ampicillin → widespread maculopapular rash** in a high proportion; *not* a true penicillin allergy and must not be recorded as one.
- Counsel two things: **avoid contact sport ~4 weeks (or until splenomegaly resolves)** because of splenic rupture, and return if airway symptoms develop.

**Diagnosis and timing**

- **Monospot/heterophile antibody**: cheap, specific, but **falsely negative in the first week** and unreliable in children <5 yrs — repeat at 1–2 weeks if negative and suspicion persists.
- **EBV-specific serology** when timing matters: **VCA IgM + VCA IgG positive with EBNA IgG negative = acute infection.** EBNA IgG appears at 6–12 weeks and persists — its presence means **past** infection.
- FBC: lymphocytosis with >10% atypical lymphocytes. LFTs mildly deranged in most.

**Management** — supportive: analgesia, hydration, rest. No antivirals. Corticosteroids **not** routine; reserve for impending airway obstruction from massive tonsillar hypertrophy, or severe haemolysis/thrombocytopenia.

**Complications**

| Complication | Note |
|---|---|
| Splenic rupture | Peak weeks 2–3. Avoid contact/collision sport and heavy lifting ≥4 weeks |
| Airway obstruction | "Kissing" tonsils, stertor, drooling, hypoxia → steroids ± nasopharyngeal airway ± ENT |
| Ampicillin/amoxicillin rash | Pruritic, morbilliform, days 5–9 of the drug |
| Others | Prolonged fatigue (weeks–months), haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, Guillain–Barré, encephalitis |

## 4.3 Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)

**The short version**

- Pus between tonsillar capsule and superior constrictor — usually complicating acute tonsillitis, sometimes de novo. Polymicrobial (GAS + anaerobes).
- **Unilateral** severe throat pain, ipsilateral referred otalgia, **trismus**, **"hot potato" muffled voice**, drooling, fetor.
- Signs: peritonsillar bulge with soft palate fullness, **uvula deviated to the contralateral side**.
- Treatment = **drainage** (needle aspiration or I&D) **plus antibiotics** — antibiotics alone rarely suffice once pus has formed.
- Trismus discriminates it from simple tonsillitis and is the practical barrier to bedside drainage.

Drain under local anaesthetic in the cooperative adult, aspirating at the point of maximal fullness superolateral to the tonsil — aim medially, the internal carotid lies laterally and posteriorly. Children and uncooperative patients need theatre. Antibiotics cover streptococci and oral anaerobes (penicillin plus metronidazole, or amoxicillin–clavulanate; clindamycin in penicillin allergy). Add IV fluids, analgesia, and a single dose of dexamethasone. Interval tonsillectomy is not automatic. Failure to improve or lateral neck swelling suggests parapharyngeal extension → contrast CT.

## 4.4 Deep neck space infection and airway emergencies

**The short version**

- Neck fascial planes communicate — infection tracks to the mediastinum. Fever + neck pain + restricted neck movement + odynophagia → **contrast CT** once the airway is safe.
- **Ludwig's angina**: bilateral submandibular/sublingual cellulitis, usually **odontogenic**; tongue elevated and pushed back — airway loss, not the abscess, is what kills.
- ⚑ **Adult epiglottitis is now commoner than paediatric** post-Hib vaccination. Severe sore throat and odynophagia **out of proportion to a near-normal oropharynx**.
- ⚑ **Do not examine the throat with a spatula, do not lie the patient flat, do not cannulate a distressed child.** Keep upright and calm; call the **senior airway team (ENT + anaesthetics)** early; secure the airway in theatre.
- **Lemierre's syndrome**: oropharyngeal infection → **IJV septic thrombophlebitis** with septic pulmonary emboli; *Fusobacterium necrophorum*.

**The spaces**

| Space | Picture | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Retropharyngeal | Mainly children <5 yrs; fever, torticollis, drooling, neck held extended, refusal to move neck | Lateral neck X-ray: widened prevertebral soft tissues. Risk of **mediastinitis** |
| Parapharyngeal | Trismus, lateral neck/parotid swelling, medial displacement of tonsil and pharyngeal wall | Abuts carotid sheath — carotid blowout, IJV thrombosis |
| Submandibular/sublingual (Ludwig's) | Bilateral firm "woody" floor-of-mouth swelling, **tongue elevated**, drooling, muffled voice, cannot swallow saliva | **Airway first.** Dental source in most; IV antibiotics + decompression + extraction |
| Supraglottis (epiglottitis) | Rapid severe sore throat, odynophagia, drooling, muffled voice, **tripod/sniffing posture**; stridor late and ominous | Adults: *H. influenzae* (non-type-b), streptococci, staphylococci. "Thumbprint sign" on lateral neck X-ray — do not delay care for it |

**Immediate approach to suspected epiglottitis/Ludwig's**: minimal handling, sit upright, oxygen, nil by mouth, IV access only if it does not distress, **senior anaesthetics + ENT, in a place where a surgical airway is possible**. Empirical IV third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone) per Therapeutic Guidelines, adding anaerobic/staphylococcal cover as the picture dictates; dexamethasone commonly given, evidence limited.

**Lemierre's** — young adult, recent sore throat, then septic days later with **rigors, unilateral tenderness along sternocleidomastoid, pleuritic chest pain and cavitating lung lesions**. Contrast CT neck (IJV thrombus) + blood cultures; prolonged IV anaerobic-active antibiotics, anticoagulation debated.

**Stridor — localise by phase**

| Phase | Site | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inspiratory** | Supraglottic / extrathoracic | Epiglottitis, laryngomalacia, angioedema, supraglottic foreign body |
| **Biphasic** | Glottic / subglottic — fixed lesion at the narrowest point | Croup, subglottic stenosis, bilateral cord palsy, tracheal foreign body |
| **Expiratory** | Intrathoracic trachea / lower airways | Tracheomalacia, bronchial foreign body, asthma |

Extrathoracic obstruction worsens on **inspiration**, intrathoracic on **expiration**. Quietening stridor with rising distress and falling air entry means the airway is closing, not improving.

## 4.5 Tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy and post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage

**The short version**

- Two dominant indications: **recurrent tonsillitis meeting frequency criteria** and **obstructive sleep-disordered breathing** (now the commonest paediatric indication).
- Frequency criteria (Paradise): **≥7 episodes in 1 year, ≥5/year for 2 years, or ≥3/year for 3 years**, each documented with fever >38.3°C, cervical adenopathy, exudate, or positive GAS culture.
- Also: suspected malignancy (**unilateral tonsillar enlargement/ulceration**), recurrent quinsy, PFAPA.
- **Primary haemorrhage <24 h** (usually within 6 h) — technical, back to theatre.
- **Secondary haemorrhage days 5–10** — slough separation ± infection, commoner overall; admit, every bleed is potentially significant.

**Adenoidectomy** — nasal obstruction and mouth breathing, hyponasal speech, sleep-disordered breathing (usually combined with tonsillectomy), recurrent otitis media with effusion (often with grommets), chronic adenoiditis.

**⚑ Post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage — treat every report of bleeding as an emergency.** Swallowed blood is concealed; children compensate then decompensate abruptly. A child repeatedly swallowing, or vomiting altered blood, is bleeding.

Sequence: sit upright, IV access, bloods including **group and hold/crossmatch and coagulation**, fluids, nil by mouth, analgesia, antiemetic, monitored area, ENT informed. Antibiotics for secondary bleeds. Adjuncts: cold saline gargle, adrenaline-soaked gauze pressure in a cooperative adult, topical tranexamic acid in some units. Active/recurrent bleeding or airway compromise → **theatre under GA** (difficult intubation: stomach full of blood, RSI, senior anaesthetist). Other morbidity: odynophagia and referred otalgia for 7–14 days, dehydration. ⚑ NSAIDs are acceptable post-tonsillectomy; **codeine is contraindicated in children** (ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolisers, deaths reported).

## 4.6 Hoarseness / dysphonia

**The short version**

- **⚑ Hoarseness >3 weeks — especially in a smoker or drinker, or with any red flag — requires laryngoscopy to see the cords. No "trial of PPI first."**
- Red flags: smoking/alcohol, neck lump, dysphagia, odynophagia, otalgia, haemoptysis, stridor, weight loss, prior head/neck radiation.
- Benign causes: viral laryngitis, vocal misuse (nodules, polyps), laryngopharyngeal reflux, inhaled corticosteroids, hypothyroidism, presbyphonia, muscle-tension dysphonia.
- **Vocal cord palsy is a sign, not a diagnosis** — find the lesion along the recurrent laryngeal nerve. Any unilateral cord lesion or immobility in an adult smoker is laryngeal SCC until excluded.

| Cause | Discriminating features |
|---|---|
| Acute laryngitis | Viral URTI, days, self-limiting; voice rest, hydration; no antibiotics |
| Vocal cord nodules | Bilateral, symmetrical, anterior/middle third junction; voice overuse (teachers, singers, children); speech therapy first |
| Vocal cord polyp | Usually unilateral, single traumatic episode or smoking; often excised |
| Reflux (LPR) | Morning hoarseness, throat clearing, globus, cough; posterior laryngeal changes |
| Laryngeal carcinoma | Progressive hoarseness, smoker, otalgia, later stridor/dysphagia. **Glottic tumours present early with hoarseness; supraglottic present late with a neck node** |
| Vocal cord palsy | Breathy voice, weak cough, aspiration; bilateral palsy → **stridor with a near-normal voice** |

**Recurrent laryngeal nerve — anatomy is the answer to the question**

Both RLNs branch from the vagus and ascend in the tracheo-oesophageal groove, supplying all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid (external branch of superior laryngeal nerve — controls pitch; injury gives a weak low-pitched voice, the classic post-thyroidectomy complication in singers).

⚑ **The left RLN loops under the aortic arch (ligamentum arteriosum); the right loops under the right subclavian artery.** The left has a far longer intrathoracic course, hence is much more often affected.

Causes along the course: **skull base/brainstem** — stroke, tumour (other CN signs); **neck** — thyroid malignancy, **thyroid/parathyroid surgery** (commonest iatrogenic cause), adenopathy, trauma; **thorax (left)** — **apical lung carcinoma**, mediastinal nodes, oesophageal carcinoma, aortic arch aneurysm, left atrial enlargement (Ortner's), cardiothoracic surgery; **idiopathic/post-viral** — exclusion only. Work-up: laryngoscopy, then **CT skull base to upper mediastinum** — CXR alone is insufficient.

## 4.7 Head and neck cancer

**The short version**

- Predominantly **squamous cell carcinoma** of oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx.
- Smoking and alcohol are **synergistic, not additive**. Also betel/areca nut (paan) in South/South-East Asian communities, UV for lip, poor dentition, prior radiation, EBV for nasopharyngeal carcinoma.
- ⚑ **HPV-16 oropharyngeal SCC** — younger, often never-smokers, tonsil/tongue-base primary, often presents as a **cystic neck node with occult primary**, and has **markedly better prognosis and radiosensitivity**. Rising incidence; HPV vaccination is preventive.
- Red flags: any symptom persisting **>3 weeks**, especially if more than one — non-healing oral ulcer or mass, dysphagia/odynophagia, hoarseness, **unilateral otalgia with a normal ear (referred pain via CN V, IX, X)**, neck lump, weight loss, cranial neuropathy, trismus.
- ⚑ **An unexplained neck lump in an adult >40 is metastatic SCC until proven otherwise**, and ⚑ **fine needle aspiration (ideally ultrasound-guided) — not excisional biopsy — is the correct first step.**

**Why FNA and not excision** — excising a metastatic node breaches fascial planes, seeds tumour into neck soft tissues, distorts later imaging and neck dissection planning, and worsens regional control. USgFNAC is safe, repeatable, and allows p16/HPV and EBV testing. If non-diagnostic, repeat or core biopsy **with specialist input**.

**Triple assessment (head and neck):**

1. **Clinical** — history (tobacco, alcohol, betel, HPV-relevant sexual history); examine oral cavity, oropharynx, neck levels, cranial nerves; **flexible nasendoscopy**.
2. **Imaging** — neck ultrasound, contrast CT neck/chest ± MRI; PET-CT for occult primary and staging.
3. **Pathology** — **USg-FNAC of the node** and/or biopsy of the visible primary; **panendoscopy + EUA with biopsy** for occult primary (with tonsillectomy and tongue-base biopsy).

Management is multidisciplinary, with early dental, speech pathology, dietetic and smoking-cessation input. The Optimal Care Pathway sets **first specialist appointment within 2 weeks of referral** and **diagnosis within 2 weeks of that appointment**.

## 4.8 The neck lump — an anatomical approach

**The short version**

- Position first: **midline vs lateral**. Then age, then duration.
- Midline: **thyroglossal duct cyst** (rises on **tongue protrusion** and on swallowing), thyroid nodule/goitre (moves on swallowing only), dermoid (neither), submental node.
- Lateral: reactive/malignant lymphadenopathy, **branchial cleft cyst** (anterior to upper third of sternocleidomastoid, young adult, may infect), salivary gland, carotid body tumour (**pulsatile, mobile side-to-side but not vertically**), lipoma, cystic hygroma (infant).
- ⚑ Age rule: **<20 yrs → congenital/inflammatory; 20–40 → inflammatory, lymphoma, thyroid; >40 → malignant until proven otherwise (metastatic SCC first, lymphoma second).**
- Investigate with **ultrasound ± FNA**; do not excise.

Persistent (>3 weeks), hard, fixed, >2 cm, supraclavicular, or accompanied by red-flag mucosal symptoms — all raise malignancy. Supraclavicular nodes point below the clavicle (lung, GI, breast, testis; left = Virchow's). B symptoms with rubbery multi-site nodes → lymphoma, where **core or excisional biopsy is needed for architecture** — the one situation FNA alone is insufficient — but that decision follows FNA and belongs to the specialist.

## 4.9 Salivary gland disease

**The short version**

- **Sialolithiasis: ~80–90% submandibular.** ⚑ Wharton's duct drains **uphill** against gravity, is long and tortuous, and submandibular saliva is more **mucinous, alkaline and calcium-rich** than parotid serous saliva.
- Classic history: **pain and swelling at mealtimes**, settling between meals; bimanual floor-of-mouth palpation may find the stone.
- **Acute bacterial sialadenitis** — usually parotid, in the dehydrated/elderly/post-operative patient; *S. aureus*; pus from the duct. Hydration, sialogogues, massage, antistaphylococcal antibiotics.
- **Mumps** — bilateral parotitis with prodrome; orchitis, meningoencephalitis, pancreatitis, SNHL; vaccine-preventable, notifiable.
- ⚑ **Facial nerve palsy with a parotid mass = malignancy until proven otherwise.** Benign tumours do not paralyse the nerve.

| Tumour | Features |
|---|---|
| **Pleomorphic adenoma** | Commonest salivary tumour; ~80% parotid superficial lobe; slow, painless, mobile. **Pseudocapsule with microscopic extensions** — enucleation recurs, so **superficial parotidectomy with nerve preservation**. Small long-term malignant transformation risk |
| **Warthin tumour** | Second commonest benign; strongly **smoking-associated**, older men, may be **bilateral/multifocal**; cystic |
| Mucoepidermoid carcinoma | Commonest salivary malignancy |
| Adenoid cystic carcinoma | **Perineural spread** → pain and nerve palsy; late lung metastases, indolent course |

⚑ **The smaller the gland, the higher the proportion of tumours that are malignant** — ~20–25% parotid, ~50% submandibular, majority of sublingual/minor gland tumours. Investigate with ultrasound + FNA, MRI for deep-lobe extension; **never incisional biopsy of the parotid** (nerve injury, seeding, fistula). Parotidectomy complications: facial nerve palsy, **Frey syndrome** (gustatory sweating, aberrant reinnervation), greater auricular numbness, salivary fistula.

## 4.10 Obstructive sleep apnoea

**The short version**

- Repetitive upper-airway collapse in sleep → snoring, witnessed apnoeas, choking arousals, unrefreshing sleep, **excessive daytime sleepiness**, morning headache, nocturia, poor concentration.
- **STOP-BANG** — 1 point each: loud **S**noring; **T**iredness; **O**bserved apnoeas; high blood **P**ressure; **B**MI >35; **A**ge >50; **N**eck circumference >40 cm; **G**ender male. **0–2 low, 3–4 intermediate, ≥5 high risk.** Epworth (≥11 abnormal) quantifies sleepiness, does not diagnose OSA.
- **Diagnosis = polysomnography** (attended lab study; level 2 home studies in uncomplicated cases). Severity by **AHI**: mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, severe >30.
- Consequences: hypertension (especially resistant), AF, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, T2DM, pulmonary hypertension, perioperative airway risk, **motor vehicle and workplace accidents**.
- Management: weight loss, alcohol/sedative avoidance, side-sleeping, treat nasal obstruction; **CPAP** first-line for moderate–severe; mandibular advancement splint for mild–moderate or CPAP-intolerant; surgery selectively (tonsillectomy — curative in most children, nasal surgery, UPPP).

⚑ **Fitness to drive (Austroads).** **Moderate–severe daytime sleepiness**, self-reported sleepiness at the wheel, or a crash attributed to inattention/sleepiness → advise **not to drive** pending specialist assessment. A **conditional licence** may follow documented effective treatment — for **private** drivers the standard is simply documented treatment compliance and satisfactory response — ⚑ **the numeric ≥4 h/night on ≥70% of nights benchmark is the COMMERCIAL standard, not the private one**. Commercial drivers additionally get: annual sleep-specialist review, objective CPAP usage download, and consideration of a Maintenance of Wakefulness Test. The doctor assesses, advises in writing, and (jurisdiction-dependent) notifies the licensing authority.

## 4.11 Dysphagia and globus

**The short version**

- **Structural until proven otherwise.** Globus is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Globus pharyngeus**: constant lump sensation **between meals**, **relieved by swallowing food**, no true dysphagia, no weight loss, normal examination. Often reflux- or anxiety-related.
- **True dysphagia**: food genuinely sticks *during* swallowing. Progressive solids→liquids with weight loss = obstructing lesion.
- Oropharyngeal (transfer) — coughing/choking at initiation, nasal regurgitation: stroke, MND, Parkinson disease, myasthenia; pharyngeal pouch (regurgitated undigested food, halitosis, gurgling neck).
- Oesophageal — carcinoma, stricture, eosinophilic oesophagitis, achalasia, extrinsic compression.

⚑ Red flags mandating **urgent endoscopy** (plus nasendoscopy if a pharyngolaryngeal cause is suspected): progressive dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, anaemia or GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, neck lump, hoarseness with dysphagia, age >50 with new symptoms, any symptom **>3 weeks**. ⚑ Barium swallow **before** endoscopy if a pharyngeal pouch is suspected (perforation risk on blind scope insertion). Involve speech pathology/videofluoroscopy for aspiration risk in neurological dysphagia.

## 5.3 Confusable pairs

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| Conductive vs sensorineural loss | Weber goes **to** the conductive ear, **away from** the sensorineural ear; Rinne negative (BC > AC) and an air–bone gap only in conductive loss |
| Otitis externa vs otitis media | Tragal/pinna tenderness with canal oedema = externa; bulging immobile drum with fever = media. Externa is treated topically, media systemically |
| Acute otitis media vs otitis media with effusion | AOM: acute pain, fever, bulging opaque drum. OME: painless, dull retracted drum, hearing loss and inattention, type B tympanogram, no antibiotics |
| Cholesteatoma vs simple chronic perforation | Cholesteatoma: attic/marginal defect, scanty foul painless discharge, granulation, drops fail, needs surgery. CSOM: central pars tensa ("safe") perforation, mucopurulent discharge drying with aural toilet plus ciprofloxacin |
| Sudden SNHL vs sudden conductive loss (wax/effusion) | Both feel blocked. Wax/effusion is visible, Weber **to** the bad ear; sudden SNHL has a normal drum, Weber to the **good** ear — steroids urgently |
| BPPV vs vestibular neuritis | BPPV: seconds, positionally triggered, symptom-free between, Dix–Hallpike positive. Neuritis: continuous for days, worse on movement but not caused by it, spontaneous nystagmus, abnormal head impulse |
| BPPV vs Ménière's | Both episodic — duration decides: seconds and positional (BPPV) versus 20 min–12 h with tinnitus, fullness and fluctuating SNHL (Ménière's) |
| Ménière's vs vestibular migraine | Ménière's needs documented low-to-mid frequency SNHL; VM has a normal audiogram, migrainous features and visual-motion sensitivity, attacks 5 min–72 h |
| Peripheral vs central acute vestibular syndrome (HINTS) | Peripheral = abnormal head impulse + unidirectional fixation-suppressed nystagmus + no skew. Any one of: normal head impulse, direction-changing/vertical nystagmus, or skew → central. HINTS applies only to ongoing AVS |
| Vestibular neuritis vs labyrinthitis | Identical, except labyrinthitis adds hearing loss/tinnitus. But new deafness in AVS is also the AICA infarct pattern — HINTS-plus; do not assume benign |
| Vestibular schwannoma vs presbycusis | Presbycusis is bilateral, symmetrical, gradual. Schwannoma is asymmetric with discrimination worse than the PTA predicts, unilateral tinnitus, mild imbalance — MRI IAM |
| Viral vs bacterial rhinosinusitis | Viral peaks then improves by day 5–7. Bacterial suggested by ≥3 of: fever >38 °C, double sickening, unilateral disease, severe pain, raised ESR/CRP. Purulent green mucus alone means nothing |
| Preseptal vs orbital cellulitis | Preseptal: lid swelling with a normal globe — full movements, normal acuity, no proptosis. Orbital: proptosis, painful/restricted movements, reduced acuity, RAPD, chemosis → CT and IV antibiotics |
| Allergic vs vasomotor rhinitis vs CRS | AR: sneezing, itch, clear rhinorrhoea, pale boggy turbinates, allergen triggers. Vasomotor: obstruction and rhinorrhoea on temperature, odours, alcohol — no itch, sneeze or allergen link. CRS: ≥12 weeks plus objective endoscopic/CT findings |
| Nasal polyps vs a unilateral nasal mass | Polyps are bilateral, pale, insensate, painless, with hyposmia. Anything unilateral — bleeding, pain, numbness, epiphora — is neoplasm until imaged and biopsied (in a child exclude encephalocoele) |
| Anterior vs posterior epistaxis | Anterior: young, unilateral drip, visible point at Little's area, controlled by pressure ± cautery. Posterior: older/arteriopathic, both nares and down the throat, fails first aid — tamponade, SPA ligation or embolisation |
| Streptococcal pharyngitis vs infectious mononucleosis | GAS: abrupt fever, exudate, tender **anterior** nodes, no cough. IM: prolonged fatigue, **posterior**/generalised nodes, palatal petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly, atypical lymphocytes, rash if given amoxicillin |
| Quinsy vs simple tonsillitis | Quinsy is unilateral with trismus, hot-potato voice, drooling, peritonsillar bulge and uvular deviation, and needs drainage plus antibiotics. Tonsillitis is symmetrical without trismus |
| Adult epiglottitis vs severe pharyngitis | Epiglottitis: symptoms out of proportion to a near-normal oropharynx, drooling, muffled voice, tripod posture, stridor late. Do not use a spatula, do not lie flat — senior airway team |
| Benign vs sinister neck lump | Benign: <20 yrs, short history, tender, mobile, resolves. Sinister: >40 yrs, >3 weeks, hard, fixed, >2 cm, supraclavicular, or mucosal red flags — USg-FNAC, never excision |
| Pleomorphic adenoma vs parotid malignancy | Adenoma: slow, painless, mobile, nerve intact. Pain, fixity, rapid growth, skin involvement or **facial nerve palsy** means malignancy |
| Thyroglossal cyst vs branchial cyst | Thyroglossal: **midline**, rises on tongue protrusion and swallowing. Branchial: **lateral**, anterior to the upper third of sternocleidomastoid, young adult, may present infected |

## 5.4 The must-not-miss list

| Emergency / red flag | Time-critical action |
|---|---|
| Sudden SNHL | Same/next-day audiogram; high-dose oral steroids ASAP, ideally within 14 days; MRI IAM |
| Central cause of acute vertigo (posterior circulation stroke) | HINTS + gait; MRI-DWI (not CT); acute stroke pathway |
| Necrotising otitis externa | Admit; biopsy granulation; prolonged systemic antipseudomonal therapy; glycaemic control |
| Mastoiditis / intracranial complications of AOM | Admit, IV antibiotics, ENT, CT if abscess; myringotomy ± mastoidectomy |
| Orbital cellulitis / cavernous sinus thrombosis | Urgent contrast CT (MRI/MRV for CST), IV ceftriaxone, ENT + ophthalmology, drain abscess |
| Adult epiglottitis / Ludwig's angina | No spatula, no lying flat; upright, oxygen; senior ENT + anaesthetics, surgical airway available |
| Post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage | Every bleed is an emergency — IV access, group and hold, NBM, ENT; theatre if active |
| Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma | Contrast imaging and tertiary referral — **never** biopsy |
| Septal haematoma | Urgent incision, drainage and quilting — otherwise cartilage necrosis and saddle nose |
| Unexplained neck lump >40 yrs | Ultrasound-guided FNA, not excisional biopsy; head and neck MDT |
| Hoarseness >3 weeks | Laryngoscopy — no PPI trial first |
| Unilateral middle ear effusion in an adult | Nasoendoscopy of the nasopharynx (NPC) |
| Airway obstruction / stridor | Minimal handling, oxygen, senior airway team; quietening stridor with rising distress = closing airway |

## ⚠ Inconsistencies found in Sections 1–4

- **CN V signs in vestibular schwannoma.** Section 1.5 calls CN V/VII signs **late**; Section 2.8 calls a reduced corneal reflex a classic **early** CPA sign. 5.1/5.3 above avoid committing to timing — decide which to memorise.
- **Topical decongestant duration.** Section 3.3's short version says **≤5 days**; its own management table, the 3.6 ladder and the Section 3 rapid-recall table all say **3–5 days**. 5.5 quotes 3–5 days.
- **Antibiotic threshold in ARS.** Section 3.3 uses "beyond ~10 days without improvement" in one paragraph and ">7–10 days" in the next.
