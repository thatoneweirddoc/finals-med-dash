# Geriatrics Notes — Section 1: Delirium, Dementia and Depression

*Sources: ACSQHC Delirium Clinical Care Standard (2021), Clinical Practice Guidelines and Principles of Care for People with Dementia (NHMRC-approved, 2016; ChEI/memantine update 2024), Therapeutic Guidelines (Psychotropic/Geriatric), Australian Prescriber, DLB Consortium 4th Consensus (2017), Dementia Australia, TGA, PBS, Austroads Assessing Fitness to Drive (2022), Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission restrictive practices rules. Scope: the three Ds — discrimination, delirium diagnosis/management, dementia subtypes/assessment/management, late-life depression — at written-finals depth for Australian practice.*

## 1.1 The three Ds — the discriminator table

**The short version**

- Delirium = **acute, fluctuating, inattentive, clouded consciousness**. Dementia = chronic, progressive, clear sensorium. Depression = subacute, mood-congruent, effort-dependent cognition.
- **Inattention is the cardinal feature of delirium** — if attention is intact, it is very unlikely to be delirium.
- Delirium is *superimposed on* dementia in a large proportion of cases; dementia is the single biggest risk factor for delirium. A stem with baseline dementia and 2 days of worsening = delirium until proven otherwise.
- **Attitude to deficits** is the fastest bedside discriminator between dementia and depression: dementia conceals/confabulates, depression complains.
- Any "acute confusion" stem in a written paper is a delirium stem unless the timeline is months.

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression (pseudodementia) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours–days, datable | Months–years, insidious (stepwise if vascular) | Weeks–months; often datable to a loss/illness |
| Course | **Fluctuates**, worse late afternoon/night (sundowning) | Slowly progressive, stable through the day | Diurnal variation — classically worse in the morning |
| Attention | **Impaired — the defining feature** | Preserved until late | Poor concentration but attention testable if engaged |
| Conscious level | **Altered** — drowsy or hypervigilant | Clear until terminal stages | Clear |
| Orientation | Impaired, fluctuating | Impaired, progressive | Usually intact |
| Memory | Registration + recent memory poor (attention-driven) | Recent >> remote; encoding failure, no benefit from cueing | Inconsistent, patchy; improves with cueing/prompting |
| Psychomotor | Hypo- or hyperactive, may alternate | Normal until late | Retardation or agitation |
| Hallucinations/delusions | Common — **visual**, transient, fleeting persecutory delusions | Less common (except **DLB — recurrent formed visual hallucinations**); delusions of theft in AD | Uncommon; if present, mood-congruent (nihilistic, guilt, poverty) |
| Sleep–wake cycle | **Disrupted/reversed** — hallmark | Fragmented late in disease | Early-morning waking; initial insomnia |
| Speech/thought | **Disorganised**, rambling, incoherent | Word-finding difficulty, empty content | Slow, monosyllabic, latency of response |
| Reversibility | Usually reversible (but ↑mortality, may not fully resolve) | Irreversible (bar the treatable mimics) | Reversible with treatment |
| Attitude to deficits | Unaware, distressed by perceptual disturbance | **Conceals, confabulates, minimises** ("my memory is fine") | **Highlights and complains**; "I don't know" answers, poor effort |
| Collateral | "He changed overnight" | "It's been about 2 years" | "He stopped enjoying things first" |

⚑ Do not treat these as mutually exclusive. The three coexist constantly; a depressed patient with dementia who develops delirium is the commonest real ward scenario, and exam stems that give a *baseline* plus an *acute change* are testing whether you separate the two layers.

## 1.2 Delirium — recognition, screening and causes

**The short version**

- Diagnosis is clinical: **acute onset + fluctuating course + inattention + (disorganised thinking or altered conscious level)**. That combination is the CAM algorithm in plain English.
- **Hypoactive delirium is the commonest subtype, is the one most often missed, and carries the worse prognosis.** The quiet, drowsy, withdrawn patient is not "settled" — they are delirious.
- Screen with the **4AT** (score **≥4 → possible delirium**; **1–3 → possible cognitive impairment**; 0 → neither). CAM is the alternative but requires training.
- Delirium is a **medical emergency and a marker of frailty**: it predicts falls, functional decline, longer stay, institutionalisation, new dementia diagnosis and death.
- It is usually **multifactorial** — find and fix *all* the contributors, not one.

**Clinical features.** Acute change in mental status over hours–days, fluctuating within the day; impaired attention (cannot sustain, focus or shift); disturbed cognition (orientation, memory, language, perception); altered arousal from hypervigilance to stupor. Perceptual disturbance is typically **visual** and frightening. Add emotional lability, reversed sleep–wake cycle and disorganised, tangential speech.

**Subtypes.**

| Subtype | Picture | Exam trap |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperactive | Agitated, restless, wandering, calling out, pulling lines, hallucinating | Over-diagnosed — this is the *minority* |
| **Hypoactive** | Withdrawn, drowsy, slow, sparse speech, poor oral intake, "pleasantly confused" | **Commonest, most missed, worst outcomes**; mislabelled as depression, fatigue or "just tired" |
| Mixed | Alternates between the two across the day | Fluctuation itself is the diagnostic clue |

**Screening tools.**

| Tool | Structure | Cut-off / use |
|---|---|---|
| **4AT** | Alertness; AMT4 (age, DOB, place, year); Attention (months backwards); Acute change/fluctuation. Score 0–12 | **≥4 possible delirium; 1–3 possible cognitive impairment**. No training, ~2 min, works in drowsy/uncooperative patients |
| **CAM** | (1) acute onset/fluctuating course **AND** (2) inattention, **PLUS** (3) disorganised thinking **OR** (4) altered conscious level | Positive = **1 + 2 + (3 or 4)**. CAM-ICU for intubated patients |
| Bedside attention | Months backwards, days backwards, serial 7s, digit span | Months backwards is the best single screen |

⚑ **Australian Delirium Clinical Care Standard (ACSQHC, revised 2021)** — 8 quality statements: (1) early identification of risk, (2) multicomponent prevention interventions, (3) patient/carer information and support, (4) prompt assessment and diagnosis with a validated tool, (5) identify and treat the underlying causes, (6) prevent complications of care (falls, pressure injury, functional decline, malnutrition), (7) **avoid antipsychotic medicines**, (8) safe transition from hospital with a documented plan. It specifies **cognitive screening within 24 hours of presentation** for patients aged **65+ — or 45+ for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people** — and for anyone with known cognitive impairment, using culturally appropriate tools.

**Causes — work the list, don't guess.** Think of predisposing (baseline vulnerability) vs precipitating (the acute hit); the frailer the brain, the smaller the insult needed.

| Domain | Specifics |
|---|---|
| Infection | UTI, pneumonia, cellulitis, biliary sepsis, meningitis/encephalitis |
| **Drugs** | Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, promethazine), **benzodiazepines**, opioids (esp. pethidine), steroids, dopaminergics, antiepileptics, digoxin, lithium; polypharmacy, any recent change |
| **Withdrawal** | **Alcohol, benzodiazepines**, nicotine — ask explicitly; often day 2–4 of admission |
| Retention/constipation | **Urinary retention and faecal impaction** — classic missed reversible causes; PR and bladder scan |
| Pain | Undertreated pain, esp. #NOF and post-op |
| Metabolic | Na⁺, glucose, Ca²⁺, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, thyroid, thiamine (Wernicke's), B12 |
| Hypoxia/perfusion | Pneumonia, PE, **ACS (often silent)**, arrhythmia, cardiac failure, anaemia, shock |
| Intracranial | Stroke, **subdural haematoma** (fall + anticoagulant), seizure/post-ictal, tumour |
| Environment | Ward transfers, ICU, sensory deprivation, sleep disruption, restraints, **IDC**, immobility, dehydration |

**Workup.** Collateral history first — baseline and timeline. Full drug review incl. recent changes, PRNs and agents stopped on admission. Exam: vitals with sats and temperature, hydration, focal neurology, chest, abdomen, **bladder scan and PR**, pressure areas. Bedside BSL, ECG, urinalysis (⚑ a positive dipstick alone does **not** diagnose UTI in an older person — asymptomatic bacteriuria is common; treat the patient, not the stick). Bloods: FBE, UEC, CMP, LFT, CRP, TFT, B12/folate, cultures if febrile. CXR. **CT brain if** focal neurology, head injury/fall, anticoagulation, reduced conscious level, or no cause found. LP/EEG only if indicated.

## 1.3 Delirium management — the highest-yield item in this section

**The short version**

- **Treat the cause + non-pharmacological management = first line, always.** Nothing else outranks this.
- **Benzodiazepines are NOT first-line and are themselves a cause of delirium.** The only routine indication is **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal delirium**.
- Antipsychotics are **not recommended** to treat delirium per the Clinical Care Standard; consider only for severe distress or immediate risk of harm when non-drug measures have failed — **lowest dose, shortest time, documented, reviewed daily**.
- Antipsychotics are **contraindicated in DLB and Parkinson's disease** (neuroleptic sensitivity) — use a benzodiazepine there instead if sedation is unavoidable.
- **Physical restraint is avoided** — it worsens agitation, prolongs delirium and causes injury.

⚑ **Past-exam trap: "an elderly patient with acute confusion is given diazepam."** This is wrong on three counts — it is not first-line, benzodiazepines *precipitate and prolong* delirium in older people, and long-acting agents accumulate. The correct answer is to identify and treat the precipitant while instituting non-pharmacological measures. Diazepam is only the right answer if the stem gives you **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal** (and even then, in hepatic impairment or marked frailty, shorter-acting **oxazepam/lorazepam** are preferred as they lack active metabolites).

**Non-pharmacological management (first-line, all patients).**

| Domain | Actions |
|---|---|
| Orientation | Clock, calendar, natural light, repeated reorientation, consistent staff, avoid ward moves |
| Sensory | **Glasses on, hearing aids in and working** — the cheapest effective intervention |
| People | Family/carers to stay; familiar objects from home; interpreter if needed |
| Sleep | Reduce night noise/light, no routine overnight obs or meds where safe, no daytime napping |
| Mobility | Early repeated mobilisation, physio; **remove IDC, lines, telemetry** ASAP |
| Nutrition | Oral intake, dentures in, feeding assistance, monitor bowels |
| Medication | Deprescribe anticholinergics/sedatives; review every drug against the delirium list |
| Basics | Analgesia (regular paracetamol), treat constipation and retention, oxygenation, glycaemic control |
| Communication | Calm, short sentences, one instruction at a time; de-escalate, don't confront |

**Pharmacological management — only if.** Severe distress, or behaviour posing immediate danger, where non-drug strategies have failed. Start low: **haloperidol 0.5 mg** (avoid in prolonged QT and parkinsonian syndromes), **risperidone 0.25–0.5 mg** or **olanzapine 2.5 mg** — single dose, reassess; no regular orders or open-ended PRNs. Review daily, cease early, document the indication, discuss with family, and **do not continue on discharge** — inadvertent continuation is a recognised medication-safety failure. Avoid phenothiazines (anticholinergic burden).

⚑ **Absolute caution: DLB and Parkinson's disease.** Around half of DLB patients show severe neuroleptic sensitivity — irreversible parkinsonism, rigidity, reduced consciousness, autonomic instability, increased mortality. Typicals are contraindicated; atypicals only with extreme caution and consent. If sedation is essential, a low-dose short-acting benzodiazepine is safer. Antipsychotics also increase stroke and death risk in dementia generally.

**Prevention.** Multicomponent (HELP-style) bundles cut incidence by roughly a third: orientation and therapeutic activity, early mobilisation, sleep protocols, vision/hearing correction, hydration, avoiding catheters and restraints, medication review. Prophylactic antipsychotics are **not** recommended.

**Follow-up.** Resolution may take weeks–months. Reassure family it is common, usually reversible, not "going mad". Re-assess cognition at 4–6 weeks — delirium markedly increases subsequent dementia risk. Document the episode and precipitant in the discharge summary.

## 1.4 Dementia — subtypes and how stems distinguish them

**The short version**

- **AD** = insidious **episodic memory** loss first. **VaD** = **stepwise**, vascular risk factors, focal signs. **DLB** = **fluctuation + visual hallucinations + parkinsonism + RBD**. **FTD** = **young, personality/language first, memory relatively spared**.
- Diagnosis needs **cognitive decline in ≥1 domain + loss of function** (that functional decline is what separates dementia from MCI).
- **DLB vs PDD = the one-year rule** — dementia before or within 1 year of parkinsonism → DLB; dementia >1 year after established motor PD → PDD.
- Mixed AD/vascular pathology is the commonest picture at autopsy — "either/or" is a simplification.
- Always screen for **reversible contributors** before labelling.

| Subtype | Proportion | Early hallmark | Stem cues | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alzheimer's disease** | ~60–70% | Anterograde **episodic memory** — repeats questions, misplaces items | Insidious over years; preserved social façade; delusions of theft; later apraxia, agnosia, language; hippocampal/medial temporal atrophy | ChEIs indicated |
| **Vascular** | ~15–20% | Executive dysfunction, slowed processing; memory may be relatively spared | **Stepwise** decline or post-stroke; HTN/DM/AF/smoking; **focal signs**, gait apraxia, early urinary symptoms, pseudobulbar affect; white-matter change/infarcts on imaging | Manage vascular risk; ChEIs not PBS-subsidised |
| **Dementia with Lewy bodies** | ~5–10% | **Fluctuating cognition** with marked variation in attention/alertness | **Recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations** (people, animals), **spontaneous parkinsonism**, **REM sleep behaviour disorder** (acts out dreams, often precedes by years), recurrent unexplained falls/syncope, autonomic dysfunction, severe **neuroleptic sensitivity** | Probable DLB = dementia + ≥2 core features (or 1 core + indicative biomarker). ChEIs often work well |
| **Parkinson's disease dementia** | — | Dementia arising in established PD | Motor PD ≥1 year before cognitive decline | Same Lewy pathology; ⚑ **one-year rule** is the separator |
| **Frontotemporal dementia** | Commonest dementia **<65 yrs** alongside AD | Behavioural variant: **disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive/ritualistic behaviour, hyperorality/dietary change**, executive dysfunction with **relatively preserved memory and visuospatial function**. Language variants: progressive non-fluent aphasia / semantic dementia | Younger patient (50s–60s), referred for "personality change", social/legal transgression, family history; frontotemporal atrophy | ⚑ **ChEIs are not indicated and may worsen behaviour**; SSRIs sometimes used for behaviour |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** | Uncommon but examinable | **Triad: gait apraxia ("magnetic", wide-based, shuffling) + urinary incontinence + cognitive impairment** | Gait disturbance usually **first and most prominent**; ventriculomegaly out of proportion to atrophy | Potentially treatable — **CSF tap test** (large-volume LP with pre/post gait timing) predicts response to **ventriculoperitoneal shunt**. "Wet, wacky, wobbly" — but wobbly comes first |

**Reversible/contributing causes to screen for every time:** hypothyroidism, **B12 and folate deficiency**, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, **depression**, **delirium**, **medications** (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids), alcohol and thiamine deficiency, **subdural haematoma**, NPH, obstructive sleep apnoea, uncorrected hearing and vision loss, neurosyphilis/HIV where risk factors exist.

## 1.5 Cognitive assessment and diagnosis

**The short version**

- **Collateral history is essential** — the diagnosis is made on trajectory and function, not on a screening score.
- **MMSE** (/30, ~<24 abnormal) is education- and language-biased and weak on executive function. **MoCA** (/30, <26 abnormal; +1 point if ≤12 years education) is more sensitive to MCI and executive dysfunction.
- ⚑ **RUDAS** (/30, **cut-off 23 — a score below 23 suggests cognitive impairment**) was developed in Australia and is the tool of choice in **culturally and linguistically diverse** patients — designed so items are minimally affected by education and culture and to survive translation intact.
- ⚑ **KICA-Cog** (/39; **≤33 remote, ≤35 urban/regional**) for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, screened from age **45**.
- **MCI = objective cognitive decline WITHOUT loss of independent function.** Once function goes, it is dementia.

| Tool | Score | Cut-off | Best for | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MMSE | /30 | <24 (approx.; adjust for education) | Familiar, tracks severity, used for PBS documentation | Education/language bias; ceiling effect; poor for executive/frontal and for MCI |
| MoCA | /30 | <26 | MCI, executive dysfunction, vascular and Lewy body patterns | Longer (~10 min); still education-sensitive; requires training/certification |
| **RUDAS** | /30 | **≤22** | ⚑ CALD populations, low literacy, interpreter-mediated assessment; 6 items — memory, body orientation, praxis, drawing, judgement, language | Less familiar to staff; not a substitute for full assessment |
| **KICA-Cog** | /39 | **≤33 remote; ≤35 urban/regional** | ⚑ Older Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people; KICA-Carer gives the informant component | Requires specific materials and cultural competence |
| Clock drawing | — | Qualitative | Rapid executive/visuospatial screen | Not standalone |
| IQCODE / informant tools | — | — | Collateral quantification of change from baseline | Depends on informant reliability |

**Diagnostic pathway.** History (onset, trajectory, domains, function — ADLs/IADLs: finances, medications, driving, cooking, appointments) → **collateral** → cognitive screen with the appropriate tool → depression screen → exam incl. gait, focal neurology, parkinsonism, cardiovascular. **Bloods:** FBE, UEC, CMP, LFT, TSH, **B12 and folate**, glucose/HbA1c, lipids; syphilis/HIV if indicated. **Imaging:** non-contrast **CT brain** minimum, to exclude SDH, tumour, NPH, infarcts; **MRI** if subtype matters or onset is young/atypical. FDG-PET, DaT SPECT (suspected DLB) and CSF/amyloid biomarkers are memory-clinic tools, not finals material.

**MCI**: measurable decline noticed by patient/informant, independence preserved. Manage with vascular risk reduction, exercise, hearing/vision correction, medication review, review at 6–12 months; some convert to dementia, some revert.

## 1.6 Dementia management

**The short version**

- Non-pharmacological, psychosocial and carer-directed care is the foundation; drugs are adjuncts with modest effect.
- **ChEIs (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine)** — PBS-subsidised for **mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease** with specialist confirmation; also helpful in **DLB/PDD** (often the best responders). **Not for FTD.** Side effects: **bradycardia/syncope/heart block, GI upset, cramps, vivid dreams, weight loss**.
- **Memantine** (NMDA antagonist) — PBS for **moderately severe AD**; reduce dose in renal impairment.
- **BPSD: look for the unmet need first — pain, infection, constipation, retention, delirium, boredom, fear, over/under-stimulation.** Drugs come after that search fails.
- ⚑ **Risperidone** is the only PBS-listed antipsychotic for BPSD (psychosis or persistent aggression in moderate–severe AD unresponsive to non-drug measures), and PBS restricts it to **up to 12 weeks** with review.

**Non-pharmacological.** Structured routine, orientation aids, meaningful activity, exercise, music/reminiscence therapy, optimised vision and hearing, environmental modification, carer education. Address vascular risk, alcohol, smoking, OSA; deprescribe anticholinergics.

**Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine.**

| Drug | Use | Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Donepezil / galantamine / rivastigmine | Mild–moderate AD (PBS); DLB and PDD (good evidence, esp. fluctuation and hallucinations); rivastigmine patch if GI intolerance | **Bradycardia, syncope, AV block**; nausea/vomiting/diarrhoea/anorexia; caution with peptic ulcer or NSAIDs; ↑falls via syncope. No efficacy difference between the three |
| Memantine | Moderately severe AD (PBS) | Better tolerated; dizziness, headache, confusion; **dose-reduce in renal impairment** |
| ⚑ Not indicated | **FTD** — ChEIs may worsen behaviour; not PBS-subsidised for vascular dementia | — |

**BPSD — the exam algorithm.** (1) **Describe** the behaviour — what, when, with whom, triggers. (2) **Exclude a medical cause**: **pain**, infection, constipation, urinary retention, dehydration, **delirium**, new drugs, sensory deprivation. (3) **Environmental/psychosocial**: overstimulation, unfamiliar staff, toileting need, hunger, boredom, fear, poor communication. (4) **Non-drug strategies**, trialled and documented. (5) Only then medication — with **informed consent**, a defined target symptom, a review date and a withdrawal plan. A trial of regular paracetamol is underused and often effective for agitation.

⚑ **Residential aged care (post-Royal Commission).** Antipsychotics used to control behaviour are **chemical restraint** — a restrictive practice. Required: **last resort** to prevent harm, alternatives trialled and **documented**; **informed consent** from the resident or a **Restrictive Practices Substitute Decision-Maker**; least restrictive form, shortest duration; recorded in the behaviour support plan; regular review. The five restrictive practices: **chemical, environmental, mechanical, physical, seclusion**. Antipsychotic use is a national aged-care quality indicator.

**Safety, driving, supports.** ⚑ Under **Austroads (2022)** a person with dementia **cannot hold an unconditional licence**; a **conditional licence** with (usually annual) review may be considered on the treating doctor's assessment of insight, judgement, attention, reaction time, visuospatial function and memory, with an **on-road OT driving assessment** in borderline cases; commercial licensing needs specialist assessment **plus** mandatory practical assessment. The **patient** must notify the licensing authority; advise and document this, and report directly if they keep driving unsafely (mandatory in NT and SA). Also: home safety (stove, wandering, falls), **advance care planning and enduring guardianship/POA while capacity remains**, financial abuse risk, **carer strain** (respite, carer payments, National Dementia Helpline), and **My Aged Care** referral for CHSP/HCP, then ACAT and residential care. Dementia is a terminal illness — take a palliative approach in advanced disease.

## 1.7 Depression in older people

**The short version**

- Older people report **less sadness** and more **somatic symptoms, anxiety, irritability, and cognitive complaints** — depression hides behind physical presentations.
- **Pseudodementia**: depression presenting as cognitive impairment. Clues — shorter history, patient *complains* of memory, "I don't know" answers, poor effort, preserved cueing benefit, mood precedes cognition.
- Use the **Geriatric Depression Scale** (GDS-15: **0–4 normal, 5–8 mild, 9–11 moderate, 12–15 severe**); it omits somatic items deliberately.
- ⚑ **Older men have the highest suicide rate of any group in Australia** — ask directly, always.
- **SSRIs first-line** (sertraline/escitalopram) — watch **hyponatraemia/SIADH, falls and fractures, bleeding with NSAIDs/antiplatelets/anticoagulants**, and **QT with citalopram**. **Avoid TCAs.**

**Presentation.** Somatic preoccupation, unexplained pain, weight loss, fatigue, early-morning waking, anxiety, agitation, withdrawal, apathy, hopelessness, self-neglect, "failure to thrive". Anhedonia is more reliable than reported sadness. Screen for bereavement, chronic pain, disability, sensory loss, isolation, alcohol and drug causes (corticosteroids, beta-blockers, opioids, benzodiazepines); exclude hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, occult malignancy.

**Pseudodementia vs dementia.**

| | Depression (pseudodementia) | Dementia |
|---|---|---|
| History length | Weeks–months, datable | Years |
| Complaint | Patient complains loudly | Family complains; patient minimises |
| Effort on testing | Poor — "I don't know" | Tries hard, gives wrong answers, confabulates |
| Cueing | Improves recall | Little benefit |
| Mood vs cognition | Mood change **precedes** cognitive change | Cognitive change first; mood follows |
| Diurnal pattern | Worse in the morning | Worse in the evening (sundowning) |
| Response to treatment | Cognition improves with antidepressant | No |

⚑ Depression and dementia frequently coexist, and depression in later life is itself a **risk factor for, and sometimes a prodrome of, dementia**. If unclear, treat the depression and **reassess cognition after remission** — don't diagnose dementia during an untreated depressive episode.

**Treatment.**

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Non-drug | Behavioural activation, CBT/IPT, exercise, social prescribing, treat pain and sensory loss, address isolation |
| First-line drug | **SSRI — sertraline or escitalopram**. **Start low, go slow** but titrate to an adequate dose; allow 4–6 weeks; continue ≥12 months after remission |
| Alternatives | Mirtazapine (insomnia + weight loss; sedating, ↑appetite); venlafaxine (monitor BP); avoid paroxetine (anticholinergic, short half-life) |
| ⚑ Avoid | **TCAs** — anticholinergic (confusion, retention, constipation), postural hypotension and falls, cardiac conduction effects, lethal in overdose |
| ⚑ Citalopram | **Dose-related QT prolongation** — max **20 mg/day if >65 years** (TGA); avoid with other QT-prolonging drugs or electrolyte disturbance |
| ⚑ SSRI harms | **Hyponatraemia/SIADH** — check Na⁺ at baseline and a few weeks after starting; worst with **thiazides/indapamide**. **Falls and fracture**. **Bleeding** with NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants (PPI or avoid). Serotonin syndrome with tramadol/triptans |
| Severe/refractory | Psychiatric referral; **ECT** effective and well tolerated, esp. psychotic depression, food refusal, high suicide risk |
| Risk | Ask about ideation, plan, means (firearms, stockpiled medicines), hopelessness. Older men, bereavement, physical illness, alcohol and isolation raise risk; fewer warnings and more lethal methods — lower threshold for admission |

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Cardinal feature of delirium | Inattention |
| Delirium subtype that is commonest, most missed, worst prognosis | Hypoactive |
| 4AT score indicating possible delirium | ≥4 (1–3 = possible cognitive impairment) |
| CAM positive requires | Acute onset/fluctuation **and** inattention, **plus** disorganised thinking **or** altered conscious level |
| First-line management of delirium | Treat the cause + non-pharmacological measures |
| Only routine indication for a benzodiazepine in delirium | Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal |
| Dementia in which antipsychotics are contraindicated | Dementia with Lewy bodies (and Parkinson's disease) — neuroleptic sensitivity |
| Fluctuating cognition + formed visual hallucinations + parkinsonism + REM sleep behaviour disorder | Dementia with Lewy bodies |
| Rule separating DLB from Parkinson's disease dementia | One-year rule — dementia before/within 1 year of parkinsonism = DLB |
| Dementia with disinhibition, apathy, hyperorality and relatively preserved memory in a 58-year-old | Behavioural-variant frontotemporal dementia |
| Stepwise decline with focal neurology and vascular risk factors | Vascular dementia |
| Gait apraxia + urinary incontinence + cognitive impairment, ventriculomegaly | Normal pressure hydrocephalus (CSF tap test → VP shunt) |
| Cognitive tool of choice in a CALD patient with low education, and its cut-off | RUDAS, ≤22/30 |
| Cognitive tool for older Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients | KICA-Cog (≤33/39 remote, ≤35 urban/regional); screen from age 45 |
| GDS-15 score suggesting moderate–severe depression | ≥10 (5–9 mild) |
| Distinguishes MCI from dementia | Preserved independent function in MCI |
| Only PBS-listed antipsychotic for BPSD, and duration | Risperidone, up to 12 weeks with review |
| Key ChEI adverse effects to screen for before prescribing | Bradycardia/AV block, syncope, GI upset |
| Antidepressant class to avoid in older people | Tricyclics |
| Maximum citalopram dose over age 65 | 20 mg/day (QT prolongation) |
| Electrolyte to check after starting an SSRI in an older patient | Sodium (hyponatraemia/SIADH) |
| Licence status permitted with a dementia diagnosis | Conditional licence only, with review — never unconditional |

# Geriatrics Notes — Section 2: Falls, Mobility and Bone Health
*Sources: ACSQHC Hip Fracture Clinical Care Standard (2023) and Preventing Falls guidelines (2025); RACGP/Healthy Bones Australia osteoporosis guideline (2024) and HBA position statement (2025); World Falls Guidelines (2022); Cochrane exercise-for-falls review (2019); MBS, PBS, Therapeutic Guidelines, AMH, ANZ Hip Fracture Registry. Scope: falls assessment and prevention, mobility, osteoporosis diagnosis and drug therapy, hip and fragility fracture care, sarcopenia.*

## 2.1 Falls — the multifactorial model

**The short version**
- A fall = unintentional coming to rest on the ground, not from overwhelming external force, syncope or seizure — but the definition is imperfect and unexplained falls often *are* syncope.
- ~30% of community-dwellers >65 and ~50% of those >80 fall each year; higher in RACFs and hospital.
- Falls are almost never mono-causal — risk rises steeply with the *number* of risk factors.
- Strongest predictors: previous fall, gait/balance impairment, muscle weakness, polypharmacy (especially psychotropics), visual and cognitive impairment.
- Consequences drive the exam answer: fracture, fear of falling, deconditioning, loss of independence, institutionalisation, excess mortality after hip fracture.

Name all three buckets:

| Bucket | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | • Gait/balance disorder, sarcopenia, lower-limb weakness<br>• Neuropathy, parkinsonism, prior CVA, vestibular disease/BPPV<br>• Visual impairment — cataract, AMD, glaucoma, multifocals<br>• Cognitive impairment, delirium<br>• Orthostatic/postprandial hypotension, arrhythmia, aortic stenosis<br>• Foot pathology, OA, chronic pain, urinary urgency<br>• Vitamin D deficiency, anaemia, hypoglycaemia, alcohol |
| **Extrinsic** | • Rugs, cords, clutter, poor lighting, no stair rails<br>• Wet floors, uneven paths, unfamiliar ward<br>• Worn/backless footwear; wrong walking aid; low toilet seat |
| **Situational** | • Rushing to phone or toilet, nocturia in the dark<br>• Carrying objects, dual-tasking, climbing on chairs<br>• Alcohol; risk-taking beyond capacity |

**Consequences worth naming**
- ~5% of falls cause fracture — hip, wrist (Colles), proximal humerus, pubic rami, vertebral. Subdural haematoma is the other serious outcome; suspect it if anticoagulated or new cognitive change.
- **Fear of falling** affects up to half of fallers, including after non-injurious falls: activity restriction → deconditioning → more falls. A treatable target.
- **Long lie** (>1 h down, typically living alone) → dehydration, hypothermia, **rhabdomyolysis with AKI**, **pressure injury**, aspiration. Independently predicts death and institutionalisation. Always ask "could you get up?" — it changes the discharge plan (alarm, home care, floor-rise training).
- Hip fracture: ~a quarter die within 12 months, many never regain pre-fracture function, and a substantial proportion admitted from home move into residential care.

## 2.2 The falls history and examination

**The short version**
- Take the history as *before / during / after* — this is what separates a mechanical fall from syncope.
- Always seek a **witness account**; the patient's own account of unwitnessed LOC is unreliable.
- Screening triad (World Falls Guidelines): any fall in the past 12 months, gait/balance difficulty, fear of falling.
- Examination minimum: **lying and standing BP**, gait and balance (**TUG**), cardiovascular, neurological, MSK/feet, vision, cognition. Investigations targeted, not routine; ECG in essentially all.

**History — the structure to write down**

| Phase | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Before** | • Doing what? Standing up, turning, reaching up, coughing, micturating?<br>• Warning — dizziness, palpitations, chest pain, aura, focal symptoms?<br>• Time of day, footwear, lighting, aid used or left behind |
| **During** | • Lost consciousness? Witness account of colour, tone, jerking, eyes open/shut<br>• Hands put out? (preserved protective reflexes → mechanical trip) |
| **After** | • Could you get up? How long down? Who found you?<br>• Immediate recovery vs prolonged confusion (post-ictal), incontinence, tongue bite, injuries |

Then the modifiable-factor sweep: **medications** (incl. OTC, PRN sedatives, recent changes), **alcohol**, **footwear**, **vision** (multifocals, cataract), **home hazards** (stairs, bathroom, night route to toilet), **continence**, **cognition and mood**, **function/ADLs**, supports, and whether this is one of a cluster.

**Examination**

- **Lying and standing BP.** ⚑ Supine after ≥5 min rest, then at **1 and 3 minutes standing** — a single immediate reading misses delayed OH. **Orthostatic hypotension** = fall in **systolic ≥20 mmHg** or **diastolic ≥10 mmHg** within **3 minutes** of standing (or head-up tilt); in known supine hypertension, **systolic fall ≥30 mmHg**. Note symptoms and pulse response — flat pulse suggests autonomic failure or beta-blockade, a rise suggests volume depletion.
- **Gait and balance.** **Timed Up and Go (TUG)**: rise from a standard armchair, walk 3 m, turn, return, sit. **≥12 s** flags impaired mobility. ⚑ TUG is a poor stand-alone predictor of future falls; use it to describe and track mobility, not to rule falls in or out. Watch *how*: pushing off the chair (proximal weakness), festination, freezing at the turn, wide base, retropulsion.
- **Five-times sit-to-stand** without arms — lower-limb power proxy. **Berg Balance Scale** (14 items) is the fuller physio measure. Gait speed <0.8 m/s marks slowness (also a sarcopenia/frailty criterion).
- **Neurological**: tone/power/reflexes, cerebellar signs, proprioception and vibration (neuropathy, B12), visual fields, Romberg, parkinsonian features, cognition.
- **Cardiovascular**: rate, rhythm, murmurs (**ESM to carotids with slow-rising pulse → aortic stenosis**).
- **MSK and feet**: joint range, leg length, quadriceps bulk — and *take the shoes and socks off*: long nails, ulcers, deformity, sensation, and the footwear itself.
- **Vision**: acuity with usual glasses; multifocals worn outdoors.

## 2.3 Drugs that cause falls

**The short version**
- Psychotropics (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, antipsychotics, antidepressants) and cardiovascular agents dominate.
- Risk is dose-related and cumulative — the **number** of falls-risk-increasing drugs matters as much as which.
- **Deprescribing is one of the few interventions with real evidence**, and it is free — always include it.
- ⚑ **Anticoagulants do not cause falls** — they change the consequences. "High falls risk" alone is not a reason to withhold anticoagulation in AF; stroke reduction almost always outweighs bleeding risk.

| Class | Mechanism of fall risk |
|---|---|
| Antihypertensives (ACEI/ARB, CCB, beta-blocker) | Hypotension, orthostatic drop, bradycardia; risk peaks after initiation/uptitration |
| Diuretics | Volume depletion, orthostasis, urgency/nocturia → rushed toilet trips, hyponatraemia |
| Alpha-blockers (prazosin, tamsulosin) and nitrates | First-dose/postural hypotension; venodilation → preload drop → syncope |
| Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs | Sedation, next-day hangover, ataxia, impaired postural reflexes; long-acting worst |
| Antipsychotics | Sedation, orthostasis (alpha-blockade), drug-induced parkinsonism, gait freezing |
| Antidepressants — TCAs *and* SSRIs/SNRIs | TCAs anticholinergic/sedating/orthostatic. ⚑ SSRIs are **not** falls-safe — ↑falls and fracture, plus hyponatraemia (SIADH) |
| Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, promethazine, benztropine) | Delirium, blurred vision, sedation; cumulative **anticholinergic burden** |
| Opioids | Sedation, delirium, hypotension, ataxia; worst at initiation and dose increase |
| Antiepileptics (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, gabapentinoids) | Sedation, ataxia, nystagmus, hyponatraemia; ↓BMD long-term |
| Hypoglycaemics (insulin, sulfonylureas) | Hypoglycaemia → collapse; over-tight HbA1c targets in the frail are a classic trap |
| Alcohol | Sedation, ataxia; interacts with all of the above, under-reported |

Use a structured tool (STOPP/START, Beers), but the exam-safe answer is: *review every drug against its current indication, stop what has none, wean sedatives, recheck postural BP after each change.*

## 2.4 Falls vs syncope vs seizure

**The short version**
- ⚑ **An unexplained fall in an older person may be syncope the patient does not recall** — retrograde amnesia for LOC is common. Treat "found on floor, no memory" as syncope until proven otherwise.
- The discriminator is the *narrative*: trigger, prodrome, duration, recovery, witness account.
- **ECG in everyone**; echocardiogram if murmur, structural disease, exertional symptoms or abnormal ECG.
- Red flags for cardiac syncope: no prodrome, exertional or supine onset, palpitations, family history of sudden death.

| Feature | Mechanical fall | Syncope | Seizure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Trip, slip, turn, reaching | Standing, pain, micturition, cough, exertion — or nil | Often nil; sleep deprivation, alcohol withdrawal |
| Prodrome | Nil ("legs gave way") | Lightheaded, greying vision, nausea, sweating; **absent in arrhythmia** | Aura — epigastric rising, déjà vu, olfactory |
| Consciousness / duration | Preserved | Lost, seconds | Lost, 1–3 min |
| Appearance | — | Pale, clammy, limp; brief myoclonic jerks possible | Cyanosed, rigid then rhythmic clonic, eyes open |
| Tongue bite / incontinence | No / no | No (rarely tip) / occasionally | **Lateral** bite is specific / common but non-specific |
| Recovery | Immediate | Oriented within seconds–minutes | **Post-ictal confusion for minutes–hours**, headache, Todd's paresis |
| Injury pattern | Hands/knees/forehead (protective) | Facial injury (no protective reflex) | Posterior shoulder dislocation, vertebral fracture |

**Cardiac causes not to miss**
- **Arrhythmia** — sick sinus, complete heart block, paroxysmal AF with pauses, VT. Abrupt, no prodrome, rapid full recovery ("Stokes–Adams"). Resting ECG may be normal → Holter, patch monitor, then **implantable loop recorder** for infrequent unexplained syncope.
- **Aortic stenosis** — exertional syncope, angina, dyspnoea; slow-rising pulse, ESM to carotids, soft A2 → **echocardiogram**. Also HOCM, severe LV dysfunction, PE, tamponade, dissection.
- **Carotid sinus hypersensitivity** — syncope/falls on head turning, tight collars, shaving. Carotid sinus massage with continuous ECG and BP: **asystole ≥3 s** (cardioinhibitory) and/or **systolic fall ≥50 mmHg** (vasodepressor); mixed forms occur. ⚑ Do **not** massage if carotid bruit, or stroke/TIA/MI within 3 months. Dual-chamber pacing only for the **cardioinhibitory** type with recurrent syncope.

Baseline workup: ECG, postural BP, FBC, U&E, Ca, glucose, TSH, 25(OH)D, B12; CT head if head strike on anticoagulant, focal signs or new cognitive change.

## 2.5 Falls prevention — what actually works

**The short version**
- **Exercise is the best-evidenced intervention** — must challenge balance, be ongoing, adequate dose (~3 h/week).
- **Multifactorial assessment + tailored intervention** in high-risk people (previous injurious fall, gait/balance problem, multiple risk factors).
- Also: medication review, first-eye cataract surgery, vitamin D **in the deficient**, podiatry/footwear, OT home hazard assessment, pacing in cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome.
- ⚑ Blanket vitamin D does **not** prevent falls; bed rails, restraints and generic risk scores do not prevent hospital falls.

**Exercise — the detail that gains marks.** Cochrane (Sherrington 2019): exercise cuts the *rate* of falls by ~**23%**; balance/functional training ~**24%**; multiple types combined (balance + resistance) ~**34%**; Tai Chi ~**19%**. Walking, dance or resistance alone — uncertain. Prescription: **progressively challenging balance work, ≥3 h/week, indefinitely** (benefit lost when it stops), via physio or exercise physiologist (e.g. Otago programme).

| Intervention | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Balance-challenging exercise, adequate dose | Strong — the cornerstone |
| Multifactorial assessment + tailored intervention in high-risk | Supported; depends on interventions actually being delivered |
| Medication review / psychotropic withdrawal | Supported; the effect is in *stopping* |
| Home hazard assessment by OT | Effective in **high-risk** only (recent fall, poor vision, recent discharge) |
| First-eye cataract surgery | Reduces falls; expedite |
| Vitamin D | Only if 25(OH)D <50 nmol/L |
| Podiatry, foot care, supportive footwear | Modest benefit, low harm |
| Pacing | Only cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome / documented bradyarrhythmia |
| Single-vision lenses outdoors | Helps active multifocal wearers |
| Hip protectors | Reduce hip fracture in high-risk RACF residents; adherence limits |
| Education alone; vitamin D in the replete or by bolus | Ineffective; bolus dosing potentially harmful |
| Bed rails, restraints, chair alarms, risk scores | Not effective; rails and restraints cause harm |

**Hospital inpatient falls.** ⚑ Australian guidance now de-emphasises numerical **falls risk scores** — treat every older inpatient as at risk and act on *modifiable* factors: delirium screening, orientation, glasses/hearing aids and call bell in reach, toileting plan, sedative review, proper footwear, correct bed height, aid at the bedside, uncluttered space, overnight lighting, supervised mobilisation. Post-fall: assess for injury (occult head injury if anticoagulated), neuro obs, identify the cause, revise the plan.

**Bed rails and restraints are not the answer** — full-length rails turn a low fall into a fall from height and cause entrapment; restraint (physical or chemical) increases delirium, deconditioning, pressure injury and mortality, and is a restricted practice under aged-care legislation. If someone is climbing out of bed, find and treat the reason (pain, retention, delirium, toileting).

## 2.6 Osteoporosis — diagnosis

**The short version**
- Osteoporosis = reduced bone mass + microarchitectural deterioration → fragility and fracture.
- **DXA T-score** (lumbar spine, femoral neck/total hip; vs young adult peak): **normal ≥ −1.0**, **osteopenia −1.0 to −2.5**, **osteoporosis ≤ −2.5**.
- ⚑ **Z-score** (age-matched) for premenopausal women, men <50, children; ≤ −2.0 = below expected range for age → hunt secondary causes.
- ⚑ **A minimal-trauma fracture in an adult >50 is diagnostic of osteoporosis regardless of T-score** — treat, don't wait for the number.
- ⚑ **Medicare funds screening DXA from age 70**; before that it is case-finding on risk factors or prior fracture.

**Minimal-trauma fracture** = fracture from a fall from standing height or less, or no identifiable trauma. Skull, face, hands, feet and pathological fractures excluded; vertebral and hip sites carry the highest subsequent risk.

**Who to assess:** all adults **≥70** (MBS items 12320/12322; rescreen ~5-yearly if T ≥ −1.5, ~2-yearly if −1.5 to −2.5); anyone **>50 with a minimal-trauma fracture**; any age with a major risk factor — **glucocorticoids ≥7.5 mg prednisolone daily for ≥3 months**, hypogonadism (incl. androgen deprivation, aromatase inhibitors), primary hyperparathyroidism, coeliac/malabsorption, CKD or liver disease, RA, thyrotoxicosis, anorexia nervosa, transplantation, long-term antiepileptics.

**Baseline bloods for secondary causes:** **FBC, ESR/CRP, U&E/eGFR, LFT, calcium, phosphate, 25(OH)D, PTH, TSH**. Then as indicated: testosterone (men), coeliac serology, SPEP and free light chains (myeloma), 24-h urinary calcium, cortisol screen. Review the drug chart (glucocorticoids, PPIs, SSRIs, antiepileptics, androgen/oestrogen deprivation).

**Risk calculators.** **FRAX** — 10-year probability of major osteoporotic and hip fracture, ± BMD (Australian version). **Garvan** — Australian, uniquely includes **number of prior falls**; 5- and 10-year risk. Use in the osteopenic range: treat if T ≤ −2.5, **or** T −1.5 to −2.5 with **FRAX major osteoporotic ≥20% or hip ≥3%**. ⚑ Neither handles glucocorticoid dose well and FRAX ignores falls — judgement overrides.

⚑ **Vertebral fractures are often asymptomatic.** Height loss >3 cm, new thoracic kyphosis, or an incidental "wedge" on CXR/CT should trigger lateral thoracolumbar imaging or DXA vertebral fracture assessment.

## 2.7 Osteoporosis management

**The short version**
- Non-drug foundation: calcium (diet first), vitamin D if deficient, weight-bearing + resistance + balance exercise, smoking cessation, alcohol limits, falls prevention.
- First line: **oral bisphosphonate**; **IV zoledronic acid** if intolerant/non-adherent; **denosumab**, especially in renal impairment.
- ⚑ **Denosumab must never simply be stopped** — cessation causes rapid bone loss and **rebound vertebral fractures**; always follow with a bisphosphonate.
- Anabolics (teriparatide, romosozumab) for very high risk — PBS-restricted, **must be followed by an antiresorptive**.
- PBS funds treatment after any **minimal-trauma fracture**, and for people **≥70 with T ≤ −2.5**.

**Non-pharmacological**

| Element | Australian recommendation |
|---|---|
| Calcium | **1000 mg/day** adults; **1300 mg/day** women >50 and men >70. **Diet first**; if inadequate, supplement **500–600 mg/day** only |
| Vitamin D | Target 25(OH)D **>50 nmol/L**; if low, **800–1000 IU/day**. ⚑ Avoid high-dose annual/bolus regimens (↑falls) |
| Exercise | **Weight-bearing impact + progressive resistance + challenging balance**, supervised and **ongoing** |
| Lifestyle | Smoking cessation; alcohol ≤2 standard drinks/day; BMI >20; protein ~1.0–1.5 g/kg/day |

**Bisphosphonates**
- **Alendronate 70 mg weekly PO**; **risedronate 35 mg weekly** (enteric-coated form may be taken with food); **zoledronic acid 5 mg IV over ≥15 min every 12 months**.
- ⚑ **Oral administration rules** — on waking, fasting, with a **full glass of plain water**, then **upright and nil by mouth ≥30 min**. Non-adherence explains lost efficacy and oesophagitis.
- Cautions: **eGFR <35 mL/min** (zoledronic acid; avoid orals below ~30), oesophageal stricture, inability to sit upright, hypocalcaemia — **replete calcium and vitamin D first**.
- Adverse effects: oesophagitis/dyspepsia; **acute-phase reaction** after the first zoledronic acid infusion (flu-like myalgia/fever 1–3 days, ~30%); hypocalcaemia; rarely uveitis.
- **Osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ)** — exposed necrotic jaw bone >8 weeks. **Rare at osteoporosis doses** (~1 in 10,000–100,000 patient-years; far commoner at oncology doses). Risk ↑ with >4 years' use, invasive dental work, smoking, diabetes, steroids. **Dental review and complete invasive work before starting**; do not interrupt therapy for a clean or filling.
- **Atypical femoral fracture** — transverse subtrochanteric/shaft fracture with cortical beaking, preceded by weeks of **prodromal thigh or groin pain**. Rare (~1 per 10,000 patient-years, rising after 5 years). New thigh pain on long-term antiresorptive → **image both femurs**.
- **Drug holiday** — ⚑ the Australian guideline (RACGP/Healthy Bones Australia 2024) says **reconsider the need to continue after 5–10 years**; the commonly taught 5 years oral / 3 years IV sits inside that. If no recent fracture and T now > −2.5, a 2–3 year break with review is reasonable; continue if T ≤ −2.5, prior hip/vertebral fracture or ongoing glucocorticoids. ⚑ Applies to **bisphosphonates only**.

**Denosumab**
- RANKL monoclonal antibody, **60 mg SC every 6 months**. No renal dose adjustment (usable when eGFR precludes bisphosphonates) but **hypocalcaemia risk is high in advanced CKD** — check calcium, replete vitamin D, recheck after dosing.
- ⚑⚑ **High-yield: denosumab is not retained in bone. Delay or cessation causes a rebound surge in resorption, rapid loss of all accrued BMD, and multiple painful vertebral fractures — <3%, concentrated in those treated >3 years.** Keep the **6-monthly interval rigorously** — the Australian guideline says flatly that denosumab **should not be interrupted**, so treat a delayed dose as urgent rather than as having a grace period. If it must stop, **transition to bisphosphonate therapy for a minimum of 12 months**, starting when the next denosumab dose was due. Never let it lapse through admission, GP change or dental work.

**Anabolic agents**
- **Teriparatide** (PTH 1-34) 20 microgram SC daily, **max 18 months lifetime**. PBS very-high-risk criterion is narrower than often quoted: **T ≤ −3.0 PLUS at least two minimal-trauma fractures, one of them after ≥12 months of continuous antiresorptive therapy**. ⚑ Must be **followed immediately by an antiresorptive**.
- **Romosozumab** 210 mg SC monthly for **12 months**, then antiresorptive. PBS-restricted to very high risk. ⚑ **Avoid within 12 months of MI or stroke** (excess CV events, ARCH).
- Also: raloxifene (vertebral only, ↑VTE), MHT (early postmenopause), testosterone if hypogonadal.

**PBS in one line:** subsidised after any established **minimal-trauma fracture**, and for people **aged ≥70 with T ≤ −2.5** (≤ −3.0 for some agents), plus corticosteroid indications — thresholds differ by drug.

**Duration and review:** generally long-term. Review adherence, calcium/vitamin D and falls annually; DXA at 1–2 years then ~2-yearly. A fracture **on** treatment means check adherence and technique, re-screen for secondary causes, then escalate — not "stop the drug".


## 2.8 Hip fracture and fragility fracture care

**The short version**
- Classic presentation: **shortened and externally rotated leg** (unopposed iliopsoas pull on the lesser trochanter — it is not characteristically abducted), groin/hip pain, unable to weight-bear.
- **Intracapsular vs extracapsular** determines the operation — the retinacular blood supply to the head runs up the neck.
- ACSQHC standard: analgesia at presentation, **surgery within 48 h**, orthogeriatric co-management, **mobilisation day 1 without weight-bearing restriction**, delirium prevention, **bone health + falls assessment before discharge**.
- ⚑ Only ~a third of Australian hip fracture patients leave hospital on bone-protection medication — say you started it.
- **Fracture liaison services** capture the fragility fracture patient; wrist and vertebral fractures are the missed sentinel events.

**Imaging.** AP pelvis + lateral hip. Normal X-ray but unable to weight-bear does **not** exclude fracture — MRI (best) or CT.

| Type | Blood supply | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| **Intracapsular** (subcapital/transcervical), undisplaced | Retinacular vessels may be intact | Internal fixation (cannulated screws/DHS) |
| **Intracapsular**, displaced | Retinacular vessels disrupted → **AVN and non-union** | **Hemiarthroplasty**; **THR** if previously independent, mobile, cognitively intact |
| **Extracapsular — intertrochanteric** | Preserved, heals well | **Dynamic hip screw** |
| **Extracapsular — subtrochanteric/reverse oblique** | Preserved but mechanically unstable | **Intramedullary nail** |

**Clinical Care Standard — the seven principles, and your management plan**
1. **Care at presentation** — prompt assessment, imaging, admission under an agreed pathway; preoperative cognitive assessment (4AT).
2. **Pain management** — assess at presentation and regularly; multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia including a **regional nerve block (fascia iliaca or femoral)**. Avoid NSAIDs. Good analgesia prevents delirium.
3. **Orthogeriatric model of care** from admission, not a rescue consult.
4. **Timely surgery** — within **48 hours** absent contraindication; delay only for reversible medical problems, not routine echocardiograms or "medical clearance".
5. **Mobilisation day 1 post-op, no weight-bearing restrictions**, daily thereafter; early nutrition, remove IDC early.
6. **Minimising further fracture** — falls **and** bone health assessment before discharge with a written plan; start calcium/vitamin D and an antiresorptive (zoledronic acid is convenient post-operatively once replete).
7. **Transition from hospital** — individualised plan, rehabilitation, communication to GP and carer.

**Delirium prevention** — the hospital's highest-risk group. Analgesia, avoid benzodiazepines and anticholinergics, oxygenation, hydration, treat constipation and retention, early catheter removal, orientation, sensory aids, early mobilisation; screen with 4AT.

**Fracture liaison services (FLS)** — a coordinator systematically identifies every patient >50 with a fragility fracture, investigates, initiates treatment and falls assessment, and hands over to the GP. ⚑ **Wrist (Colles), proximal humerus and vertebral fractures are the sentinel events routinely ignored** — a Colles fracture in a 62-year-old woman is an osteoporosis diagnosis waiting to be made.

## 2.9 Sarcopenia, deconditioning and immobility

**The short version**
- **Sarcopenia** = loss of skeletal muscle **mass plus strength/function**; low **grip strength** screens, low muscle mass confirms, **gait speed <0.8 m/s** marks severity.
- Primary (age-related) vs secondary (inactivity, low protein intake, inflammatory disease, cancer, organ failure, glucocorticoids).
- Consequences: falls, fractures, functional decline, longer admissions, delirium, institutionalisation, mortality.
- Hospital **deconditioning** is fast — measurable muscle loss within days of bed rest, worse in the old. "Bed rest" carries harms: pressure injury, VTE, constipation, atelectasis, orthostatic intolerance, delirium.
- ⚑ **Treatment is progressive resistance exercise plus adequate protein** (~1.0–1.5 g/kg/day) — no effective drug exists. Also treat vitamin D deficiency, review catabolic drugs, address dysphagia, dentition and social causes of poor intake.
- Inpatient answer: mobilise from day one, dressed and out of bed for meals, avoid tethering lines, dietitian review, physio-led resistance training continued post-discharge.

# Geriatrics Notes — Section 3: Prescribing, Polypharmacy and Deprescribing
*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Australian), Australian Medicines Handbook, Australian Prescriber, RACGP, NPS MedicineWise/Australian deprescribing literature, ACSQHC Medication Safety Standard, PSA Medicine Safety reports, AGS Beers Criteria 2023, STOPP/START v3 2023. Scope: age-related PK/PD change, polypharmacy and prescribing cascades, anticholinergic burden, prescribing-appropriateness tools and Australian medication review, structured deprescribing, high-risk drug classes, and medication-related harm — written-paper depth.*

## 3.1 Why older people are different — pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

**The short version**
- **Absorption is least affected** — slower gastric emptying and reduced splanchnic flow alter *rate* more than *extent*.
- **Distribution shifts**: total body water ↓, lean mass ↓, fat mass ↑. Water-soluble drugs (digoxin, gentamicin, lithium) have a **smaller Vd → higher peak per mg/kg → lower LOADING dose**. ⚑ Vd sets the loading dose only; the **maintenance** dose falls because **clearance** falls (see renal, below) — a distinction written papers like to test. Lipid-soluble drugs (diazepam, amiodarone) have a **larger Vd → prolonged t½ → accumulation, delayed offset**.
- **Renal clearance falls; serum creatinine does not rise to match**, because creatinine generation falls with sarcopenia. A "normal" creatinine in a 55 kg 88-year-old can conceal a CrCl of 25 mL/min.
- **Hepatic phase I (CYP) and first-pass extraction decline**; phase II conjugation is relatively preserved.
- **Pharmacodynamic sensitivity increases** — the same concentration produces a bigger effect for opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, antipsychotics and warfarin.

**Protein binding.** Serum albumin falls with age, malnutrition and acute illness. For **highly protein-bound drugs** — phenytoin, warfarin, valproate, some sulfonylureas — the *free* (active) fraction rises even when the total measured level reads "therapeutic". A total phenytoin of 40 µmol/L in a hypoalbuminaemic older patient may represent a toxic free level: interpret levels against albumin, or request free phenytoin. Displacement interactions are usually self-limiting (clearance of the freed drug also rises) — the exam point is *level interpretation*, not sustained toxicity.

**Hepatic metabolism.** Reduced hepatic blood flow lowers first-pass extraction, so **oral bioavailability rises** for high-extraction drugs (morphine, propranolol, verapamil, nitrates, TCAs). Phase I declines, which is why **oxazepam and temazepam** (phase II conjugation only) are preferred over **diazepam and nitrazepam** (phase I oxidation to long-lived active metabolites). Frailty, not chronological age, is the better predictor of impaired handling.

**Renal clearance — the practically examinable part.** ⚑ Do **not** dose renally-cleared drugs off serum creatinine alone. Two estimates exist and are not interchangeable:

| Estimate | What it is | Where it wins | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **eGFR (CKD-EPI)** | Lab auto-reported; **normalised to 1.73 m² BSA** | CKD staging; population assessment | Overestimates absolute clearance in small, frail, low-BSA patients; not the variable most dose-adjustment tables were derived from |
| **Cockcroft–Gault CrCl** | Calculated from age, weight, sex, creatinine; **absolute mL/min** | Narrow-index and renally-cleared drugs — digoxin, aminoglycosides, DOACs, metformin, allopurinol, sotalol, lithium | Needs a sensible weight (actual if normal BMI; adjusted/ideal if obese); underestimates in the very old and <60 kg |

⚑ **Australian practice point:** expert groups increasingly accept **de-indexed eGFR** (× actual BSA ÷ 1.73) for most dosing, but **approved dose bands for many high-risk drugs — notably the DOACs and metformin — are written against Cockcroft–Gault CrCl**, so use CG where the approved rule specifies it. Neither formula is valid in unstable renal function (AKI): dose conservatively and re-check. Almost 1 in 4 older Australians on renally-cleared medicines receives an excessive dose.

**Pharmacodynamics.** Age increases target-organ sensitivity independent of concentration: more CNS/respiratory depression from **opioids**; more sedation, ataxia and falls from **benzodiazepines**; more delirium from **anticholinergics**; a greater INR response per mg of **warfarin**; more orthostatic hypotension (blunted baroreflex) from antihypertensives, α-blockers and TCAs; more EPSE from dopamine antagonists. Homeostatic reserve is blunted — impaired thirst/ADH (hyponatraemia), thermoregulation, glycogen reserve (hypoglycaemia). This is the mechanistic basis of **"start low, go slow"**.

## 3.2 Polypharmacy

**The short version**
- Working definition: **≥5 regular medicines**; hyperpolypharmacy **≥10**. The number is a screening flag, not the diagnosis — **appropriateness** is the endpoint.
- ~**36%** of Australians aged ≥70 meet continuous polypharmacy criteria (~935,000 people, 2017); prevalence in **residential aged care ~90%**, with **~80%** exposed to ≥1 potentially inappropriate medicine.
- ADR risk rises **non-linearly** with drug count — pairwise interaction potential rises roughly with n².
- **Prescribing cascade** = an ADR misread as a new disease and treated with another drug. Ask "what did we start just before this symptom began?"
- Polypharmacy marks **fragmented care** — multiple prescribers, single-disease guidelines applied to one multimorbid person.

**Appropriate vs problematic.** Five drugs in a post-MI diabetic with heart failure may all be indicated; three in a frail 92-year-old with 6 months to live may all be inappropriate. Single-disease guidelines applied additively to a person with 5 conditions generate a regimen no trial ever tested. Consequences: ADRs, interactions, falls, delirium, non-adherence (complexity, cost, dexterity), anticholinergic/sedative load, cascades, and administration error at transitions of care.

**Prescribing cascades — worked examples.** Australian Prescriber defines the cascade as a new medicine prescribed to treat an adverse reaction to another drug, in the mistaken belief a new condition has appeared.

| Index drug | Adverse effect misread as disease | Cascade drug | Better move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dihydropyridine CCB** (amlodipine, felodipine) | Dependent ankle oedema — **not** fluid overload; JVP normal, no crackles/orthopnoea | **Frusemide** → volume depletion, postural drop, AKI, hyponatraemia, incontinence | Reduce dose or switch class; leg elevation. Diuretics don't fix vasodilator oedema |
| **Metoclopramide / prochlorperazine** | Drug-induced parkinsonism (symmetrical, subacute, post-initiation) | **Levodopa** → poor response + orthostasis, hallucinations | Cease dopamine antagonist; weeks–months to resolve |
| **NSAID** | ↑BP (prostaglandin-mediated Na retention) | **Antihypertensive added** → no source control, ongoing renal/GI risk | Stop the NSAID; paracetamol ± topical NSAID |
| **Donepezil / rivastigmine** | Urge incontinence (cholinergic detrusor effect) | **Oxybutynin** → **pharmacological tug-of-war**: anticholinergic directly opposes the cholinergic drug, worsening cognition | Review whether the cholinesterase inhibitor still benefits; treat bladder non-pharmacologically |
| **Thiazide** | Hyperuricaemia/gout | Allopurinol/colchicine | Reconsider the thiazide |
| Antihypertensives, sedatives, opioids | Dizziness | **Prochlorperazine** — Australian veterans data linked this to **~50% higher hip-fracture risk** | Find and stop the cause of dizziness |
| ACE inhibitor | Dry cough | Antibiotic/cough suppressant | Switch to ARB |

## 3.3 Anticholinergic burden

**The short version**
- Reduced cholinergic reserve and a leakier BBB make anticholinergics disproportionately harmful.
- **Central**: delirium, confusion, sedation, impaired attention/memory, falls. **Peripheral**: dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, tachycardia, reduced sweating.
- **Burden is cumulative**: several "weak" agents equal one strong one. Count the whole list.
- Culprits are easy to miss — many are **OTC or not thought of as anticholinergic**.
- Higher long-term exposure is associated with **increased dementia risk** (UK case-control data: adjusted OR ≈1.5 for the highest exposure category; strongest for antidepressants, bladder antimuscarinics, antipsychotics, antiepileptics) — association, not proven causation, but enough to minimise exposure.

**Easily-overlooked culprits.** Bladder antimuscarinics — **oxybutynin** (worst; lipophilic, crosses BBB) > solifenacin/darifenacin; consider mirabegron or non-drug measures. Sedating antihistamines — **promethazine**, diphenhydramine (often OTC in sleep and cough/cold preparations). **Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, doxepin** — including "low-dose for neuropathic pain or sleep": the effect is not dose-spared. **Olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine, chlorpromazine**. **Hyoscine** (butylbromide/hydrobromide), atropine, benztropine, prochlorperazine, tolterodine, antispasmodics. Commonly forgotten: **frusemide, digoxin, warfarin, prednisolone and codeine** score low-grade (1) anticholinergic activity on validated scales.

**The scales.** Several exist and disagree at the margins: **Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB)** (drugs scored 1/2/3; total ≥3 is the usual concern threshold), **Anticholinergic Risk Scale**, **Anticholinergic Drug Scale**, and the **Drug Burden Index** — an Australian-developed measure indexing **both anticholinergic and sedative exposure** to dose, correlating with impaired physical and cognitive function. They exist to **flag cumulative burden and prompt review**, not to diagnose. In new confusion, totalling anticholinergic load is part of the delirium workup.

## 3.4 The tools: Beers, STOPP/START, and Australian medication review

**The short version**
- **Beers criteria (AGS, US; updated 2023)** — explicit list of **potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs)** in ≥65s: avoid outright, avoid in specific conditions, use with caution, avoid in renal impairment, plus key drug–drug combinations.
- **STOPP/START (European; version 3, 2023 — 133 STOPP + 57 START criteria)** — organised **by physiological system**, so it maps onto a clinical review. ⚑ **START is the unique element: potential prescribing *omissions* — under-treatment is as much an error as over-treatment.**
- Both are **screening prompts, not prohibitions** — a listed drug may still be right; the tool obliges you to justify it.
- Australia has no single national PIM list in routine use — both are used, alongside **Therapeutic Guidelines** and the **AMH** for local dosing.
- The Australian *system-level* answer is structured pharmacist review: **HMR** (community) and **RMMR** (aged care).

| High-yield item | The problem | Safer approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Benzodiazepines / Z-drugs** (esp. long-acting) | Falls, fractures, delirium, cognitive impairment; tolerance within weeks | Non-drug insomnia management (CBT-i); if unavoidable — short-acting, lowest dose, defined stop date |
| **First-generation antihistamines** (promethazine, diphenhydramine) | Strongly anticholinergic; often OTC, self-initiated | Non-sedating agent if genuinely needed; never as a hypnotic |
| **Anticholinergics generally** | Cumulative burden; ⚑ 2023 Beers added them to drugs to avoid with falls/fractures | Total the burden; substitute or cease |
| **NSAIDs** — esp. with CKD, anticoagulant/antiplatelet, HF, prior PUD | GI bleeding, AKI, fluid retention, ↑BP; risk even from short courses | Paracetamol first; topical NSAID; if oral — shortest course, lowest dose, **plus PPI**, monitor renal function |
| **PPI beyond 8 weeks** without indication | C. difficile, fracture, hypomagnesaemia, pneumonia associations | Confirm indication (Barrett's, chronic NSAID + risk factors, severe erosive disease justify continuing); else step down/cease |
| **Long-acting sulfonylureas** (glibenclamide) | Prolonged severe hypoglycaemia | ⚑ 2023 Beers now cautions about **all** sulfonylureas, preferring short-acting if needed |
| **Digoxin** — dose and indication | Narrow index + falling renal clearance; not first-line for AF rate control or HFrEF | Avoid >125 microgram/day; monitor renal function, K⁺, Mg²⁺; recheck indication |
| **Antipsychotics for BPSD** | Modest efficacy; ↑stroke and ↑mortality in dementia; sedation, falls, EPSE | Non-pharmacological first; if used — specific target symptom, lowest dose, **planned review and cessation** |
| Aspirin for **primary** prevention | ⚑ 2023 Beers moved from "caution" to **avoid initiating** | Reserve antiplatelets for established vascular disease |
| Anticoagulant choice | ⚑ 2023 Beers prefers DOACs over warfarin but singles out **rivaroxaban to avoid** vs other DOACs on bleeding grounds | Choose on renal function, interactions, bleeding profile |

**Australian medication review.** **HMR (Home Medicines Review)** — the GP referral and medication management plan are **MBS-funded (item 900)**; the pharmacist review itself is funded through the Community Pharmacy Agreement via the Pharmacy Programs Administrator — GP (or specified specialist) referral, for a **community-dwelling** patient at risk of medication misadventure; a **credentialed pharmacist** interviews the patient (usually at home) and reports to the GP, who writes an agreed medication management plan; generally one per 24 months, follow-ups if circumstances change. **RMMR** — the **RACF** equivalent, collaborative GP–pharmacist review alongside facility-level Quality Use of Medicines activities. The pharmacist's role extends to dose administration aids, dispensing checks, reconciliation and counselling. The **ACSQHC Medication Safety Standard** makes reconciliation at every transition, a best-possible medication history, and a current medicines list given to the patient a system obligation.

## 3.5 Deprescribing

**The short version**
- Deprescribing = **planned, supervised** dose reduction or cessation of medicines whose harms now outweigh benefits, within the patient's goals of care. Active care, not withdrawal of care.
- Sequence: **list everything → establish indication → weigh benefit vs harm against remaining life expectancy and goals → prioritise → taper where needed → monitor**.
- **Time to benefit** is pivotal: a preventive drug only helps if the patient lives to reach its benefit horizon.
- **Taper** where withdrawal or rebound is a real risk; many drugs can simply be stopped.
- ⚑ It is a **shared decision** — cessation without the patient's (or substitute decision-maker's) agreement predictably fails.

**Step 1 — comprehensive list.** Include OTC (NSAIDs, PPIs, antihistamines, laxatives, aspirin), complementary/herbal (St John's wort — major CYP inducer; fish oil, ginkgo — bleeding), topicals, eye drops (timolol → bradycardia, bronchospasm), inhalers, and PRNs actually taken. Ask the patient to bring in everything.

**Step 2 — indication.** For each: why started, by whom, when, and does that reason still hold? Typical findings — a PPI from ICU stress-ulcer prophylaxis 4 years ago; an antipsychotic for post-op delirium never ceased; a bisphosphonate at year 9; an antihypertensive now causing postural hypotension after 8 kg weight loss.

**Step 3 — benefit vs harm in context.** Ask explicitly: likely remaining life expectancy, **degree of frailty**, and what the patient actually wants (longevity vs symptom control vs staying home vs avoiding hospital). Symptom-relieving drugs that work retain value at any prognosis; **preventive** drugs do not.

**Step 4 — prioritise.** Stop the drug with **highest harm, lowest current benefit** first, one at a time so change can be attributed. Rank: no valid indication → causing an identified ADR → high-risk class (anticholinergic, sedative, hypoglycaemic) → preventive drug whose time-to-benefit exceeds life expectancy → drug the patient doesn't want.

**Step 5 — taper where withdrawal or rebound is a risk.** ⚑

| Class | Withdrawal/rebound risk |
|---|---|
| **Benzodiazepines / Z-drugs** | Anxiety, insomnia, tremor, myoclonus, **seizures**, delirium — taper ~10–25% every 1–2 weeks, slower at the end; ~38% report withdrawal symptoms |
| **Opioids** | Autonomic withdrawal syndrome, pain flare |
| **Antidepressants** (esp. short t½ — paroxetine, venlafaxine) | Discontinuation syndrome: dizziness, "electric shocks", flu-like symptoms, relapse |
| **Corticosteroids** (>3 weeks) | Adrenal insufficiency; disease flare |
| **Beta-blockers** | Rebound tachycardia, angina, hypertension, MI |
| **PPIs** | Rebound acid hypersecretion — halve dose or alternate-day, then cease; H2RA/antacid cover |
| **Levodopa** | Motor deterioration; abrupt cessation risks **neuroleptic-malignant-like syndrome** |
| **Antiepileptics** | Seizure/status risk — taper over weeks–months |
| Also clonidine (rebound hypertensive crisis); antipsychotics (cholinergic rebound, dyskinesia, relapse) | |

Generally safe to stop outright: statins, vitamin/mineral supplements, bisphosphonates, most antihistamines, antispasmodics, potassium supplements (with monitoring).

**Step 6 — monitor.** Set a review date; tell the patient what to watch for; document the reason for cessation so another prescriber doesn't reflexively restart it; reinstate if the symptom genuinely returns.

**Time to benefit — the exam answer.** **Statins** need ~**2–5 years** before stroke/MI reduction appears; **bisphosphonates** — the Australian guideline says reconsider continuing after **5–10 years**, and trial data show cessation within that window does not clearly alter fracture risk in lower-risk patients in many people without altering fracture risk; **intensive glycaemic control** takes years to reduce microvascular endpoints while causing hypoglycaemia within days. Hence in a frail 90-year-old with limited life expectancy, statins, bisphosphonates and tight HbA1c targets are the classic candidates: harm is immediate, benefit lies beyond the horizon. **Caveat:** this applies to *primary* prevention and genuinely limited prognosis — don't reflexively stop a statin in a robust 82-year-old with established coronary disease, and 2023 Beers cautions specifically against reflexive **digoxin cessation in HFrEF**.

## 3.6 Specific high-risk drug groups

**The short version**
- The recurring harms are **falls, bleeding, delirium, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, AKI and QT prolongation**.
- ⚑ **Falls risk is not, by itself, a reason to withhold anticoagulation in AF** — the single most-tested myth in this area.
- **Pethidine has no place in geriatric prescribing**; **tramadol** is a poor choice in older people.
- Diuretics and SSRIs are the two commonest causes of drug-induced hyponatraemia.

| Class | Problem in older people | Safer approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Warfarin / DOACs** | Bleeding; renal accumulation of DOACs; warfarin interactions (antibiotics, amiodarone, NSAIDs) and dietary variability | Dose DOACs on **CrCl (Cockcroft–Gault)**, weight and age per approved criteria; avoid DOACs in severe renal impairment/valvular AF; avoid concurrent NSAIDs |
| ⚑ **Anticoagulation and falls in AF** | Falls routinely misused to justify withholding therapy, leaving high-CHA₂DS₂-VA patients unprotected | Modelling: a patient would need ~**295 falls per year** for fall-related bleeding to outweigh warfarin's stroke reduction; observational data show fallers have higher ICH risk **regardless of** warfarin, aspirin or nothing. Falls should prompt **falls management** (deprescribe sedatives/antihypertensives, vitamin D/exercise, home hazards, vision) — not withholding. Genuine contraindications: recurrent unexplained head strike, uncontrolled bleeding source, patient preference |
| **NSAIDs** | GI haemorrhage, AKI, HF decompensation, ↑BP; with ACEI + diuretic = "**triple whammy**" → AKI | Regular paracetamol first; topical NSAID for localised OA; if systemic — shortest course + PPI + renal monitoring |
| **Opioids** | Sedation, delirium, respiratory depression, constipation, falls | Start ~half the adult dose, regular aperient from day 1, avoid concurrent benzodiazepine. **Pethidine — avoid**: short duration; metabolite **norpethidine** accumulates in renal impairment → tremor, myoclonus, agitation, **seizures**, and is not naloxone-reversible. **Tramadol — problematic**: lowers seizure threshold (↑ with SSRIs/TCAs), **serotonin syndrome**, **hyponatraemia**, delirium, variable efficacy via CYP2D6 polymorphism |
| **Benzodiazepines / Z-drugs** | Falls, hip fracture, delirium, daytime sedation, dependence within weeks | Avoid initiating; if inherited, taper. Prefer **oxazepam/temazepam** (no phase I metabolism) if one is genuinely required |
| **Antipsychotics** | ↑Stroke and ↑mortality in dementia, EPSE, falls, QT, aspiration, metabolic effects | Non-pharmacological first; specific target symptom, lowest dose, shortest duration, planned review |
| **Anticholinergics** | See 3.3 — delirium, cognitive decline, retention, falls | Calculate burden; substitute or cease |
| **Digoxin** | Narrow index; renal accumulation; toxicity precipitated by hypokalaemia/hypomagnesaemia — nausea, confusion, visual halos (xanthopsia), bradyarrhythmia | Low dose (avoid >125 microgram/day); monitor renal function and electrolytes; confirm the indication persists |
| **Sulfonylureas / insulin** | Hypoglycaemia — presents atypically as falls, confusion or "delirium"; blunted autonomic warning; prolonged with glibenclamide and renal impairment | ⚑ **Relax targets with frailty**: RACGP individualises, with **HbA1c ~8% (64 mmol/mol) rather than 7%** appropriate for many older people, while noting >8–8.5% associates with harm. Avoid sulfonylureas in frailty or erratic eating; simplify insulin; document a hypoglycaemia plan |
| **Diuretics** | Hyponatraemia (thiazides, esp. **indapamide**, often within weeks), hypokalaemia, volume depletion, AKI, gout, incontinence, falls | Check U&E ~1–2 weeks after starting or dose change, and during intercurrent illness; sick-day rules |
| **SSRIs/SNRIs** | **Hyponatraemia/SIADH** (several-fold higher risk in older people; multiplicative with thiazides), falls, GI bleeding (esp. with NSAID/anticoagulant), QT (citalopram) | Baseline and ~2-week sodium check; avoid stacking hyponatraemia-causing drugs |
| **QT-prolonging drugs** | Additive risk → torsades; amplified by hypokalaemia/hypomagnesaemia, bradycardia, renal impairment | Offenders: citalopram/escitalopram, haloperidol, quetiapine, amiodarone, sotalol, macrolides, quinolones, ondansetron, domperidone. Count co-prescriptions; ECG; correct electrolytes |

## 3.7 Adverse drug reactions and admission

**The short version**
- Medication-related problems account for roughly **250,000 Australian hospital admissions annually** (~$1.4 billion), and **about half are preventable**; another ~400,000 ED presentations are medicine-related.
- ⚑ **Any new symptom in an older person is a drug adverse effect until proven otherwise.**
- ADRs in older people present **atypically and non-specifically** — falls, confusion, incontinence, immobility, anorexia, "off legs" — the geriatric giants are frequently iatrogenic.
- Highest-risk transitions are **admission and discharge**; reconciliation at every transition is the fix.
- Safe prescribing is procedural: start low, go slow, one change at a time, indication for every drug, review date for every drug.

**Scale of harm.** In Australian RACFs, **98%** of residents have ≥1 medication-related problem and **~81%** are exposed to ≥1 potentially inappropriate medicine — long-term PPIs (~42%), benzodiazepines (~38%), antipsychotics (~31%) lead. In general practice, ~1 in 10 patients on continuous medication reports an adverse medicine event in the preceding six months.

**Practical safe-prescribing rules.**
- **Start low, go slow — but go.** Under-treatment (the START half) is equally an error: untreated osteoporosis after minimal-trauma fracture, no anticoagulation in AF, no post-MI ACEI/beta-blocker, no laxative with a regular opioid.
- **One change at a time**, so a new symptom or improvement can be attributed.
- **A review date for every new medicine**, and an **indication on every prescription** and discharge summary.
- **Check the practicalities**: swallowing (dysphagia → liquid, dispersible or patch), dexterity (blister packs, eye drops, inhaler technique — spacer or DPI), vision/health literacy, cognition and who supervises, and cost/PBS status — affordability drives silent non-adherence.
- **Dose administration aids** aid adherence but obscure PRN use and are hazardous if the regimen changes without the pack being updated.
- **Reconcile at every transition** — admission, ward transfer, discharge, RACF entry — against a best-possible medication history from ≥2 sources; give the patient a current dated medicines list.
- Consider **HMR/RMMR**; involve pharmacist and carer early.

## 4.1 Frailty

**The short version**

- Reduced multisystem reserve → a small stressor (UTI, new drug, a night in ED) causes disproportionate functional loss.
- **Fried phenotype**: weight loss, exhaustion, weakness (grip), slowness (gait), low activity. **0 robust, 1–2 pre-frail, ≥3 frail.**
- **Rockwood cumulative deficit model**: frailty index = deficits present ÷ deficits counted (30–70 items). Continuous but impractical by hand; the **Clinical Frailty Scale** (9 points, judgement-based, baseline function) is its bedside form.
- Predicts mortality, delirium, falls, surgical morbidity and institutionalisation **better than age or comorbidity count**.
- **Dynamic and partly reversible** — the examinable punchline, not a nihilistic label.

Fried thresholds: >5% unintentional weight loss over a year; tiredness most of the time; grip, gait speed and weekly activity each in the lowest quintile for sex. Biologically coherent — sarcopenia, neuroendocrine dysregulation and inflammation in a self-reinforcing cycle — but it needs a dynamometer and a measured walk, ignores cognition, and cannot be scored in a bed-bound patient.

**Clinical Frailty Scale** ⚑ — nine levels: very fit → fit → managing well → living with very mild frailty → mild → moderate → severe → very severe frailty → terminally ill. Grading synthesises mobility, energy, IADL then ADL dependence.

- ⚑ **v2.0 (2020) relabelled the levels** ("Vulnerable" → "Living with Very Mild Frailty"; 5–8 → "Living with… frailty"). Numbers unchanged; old wording persists on hospital forms.
- ⚑ Score the **baseline ~2 weeks before the acute presentation** — scoring the illness is the commonest error.
- ⚑ Not validated <65 yrs, **not for stable long-term disability** (frailty ≠ disability), and **not a sole triage rule**. CFS ≥5 commonly triggers geriatrics referral.

**Why it matters, and what reverses it.** Frailty drives the peri-operative risk discussion (it independently predicts post-op delirium, complications and loss of independence) and **changes treatment targets** — loosen HbA1c and BP targets, deprescribe. Reversibility: progressive **resistance exercise** (best evidence, works into the 90s); **protein and energy with exercise** (alone you add fat, not muscle); **deprescribing** anticholinergics, sedatives, hypotensives; treating depression, anaemia, pain, poor dentition and sensory impairment.

## 4.2 Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment

**The short version**

- Multidimensional **interdisciplinary diagnostic process** producing a coordinated plan — assessment *plus* delivery, not a form. Domains: **medical, functional, cognitive/psychological, social and environmental.**
- Inpatient CGA → **more likely alive and in own home at 3–12 months** (RR ~1.06) and **~20% fewer residential care admissions** (RR ~0.80); mortality alone barely moves.
- **Ward-based** CGA beats a roving consult service that only advises. Australian settings: GEM, orthogeriatrics, ACAT, My Aged Care, Support at Home.

| Domain | Content |
|---|---|
| Medical | • Problem list, comorbidity • Medication review incl. OTC • Nutrition • Continence • Pain • Sensory function • Dentition |
| Functional | • ADLs/IADLs • Gait, balance, falls • Transfers • Aids and equipment |
| Cognitive/psychological | • Cognition and delirium status • Mood • Alcohol • BPSD |
| Social/environmental | • Carer capacity and strain • Home layout, stairs, bathroom • Finances, driving • Advance care planning and substitute decision-maker |


**Who does what**: geriatrician — diagnosis, deprescribing, prognosis, goals of care; nursing — continence, skin, delirium screening, day-to-day function; physio — mobility, balance, exercise; OT — ADL/IADL performance, home hazards, equipment; speech pathology — swallow; dietitian — malnutrition; pharmacist — anticholinergic burden; social work — carer support, accommodation, elder abuse. The **family meeting** converts parallel assessments into one plan.

**Australian pathway**: **GEM** subacute beds; **orthogeriatric** co-management of hip fracture (less delirium, shorter stay, better mortality); **My Aged Care** as single entry point → **ACAT/ACAS** assessment → approval; Transition Care post-hospital; **residential aged care** (ACAT approval plus means assessment).

- ⚑ **Support at Home replaced Home Care Packages and Short-Term Restorative Care from 1 Nov 2025**, with **8 ongoing classification levels**. "HCP levels 1–4" is out of date; entry-level help remains via CHSP.

- ⚑ CGA content is embedded in **NSQHS Standard 5 (Comprehensive Care)**, which mandates screening for falls, pressure injury, delirium, malnutrition and self-harm — the geriatric syndromes are an accreditation obligation, not optional.

## 4.3 Functional assessment

**The short version**

- **ADLs** (Katz): bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding. ⚑ The **DEATH** mnemonic (Dressing, Eating, Ambulating, Toileting, Hygiene) is a *different* list — it has ambulating and hygiene but no continence or transferring, so do not use it to recall Katz.
- **IADLs** (Lawton, 7–8 items): shopping, finances, medications, transport, cooking, housework, telephone. The **SHAFT** mnemonic (Shopping, Housekeeping, Accounting, Food preparation, Transport/Telephone) covers five of them.
- **IADLs are lost first** — cognitively demanding, so the **more sensitive early marker**. ADL loss is later and more advanced.
- Barthel = 10 weighted ADL items, max 100. Katz = 6 ADLs scored 0/1, hierarchical loss (bathing usually first). A documented **premorbid baseline** is what makes CFS scoring, rehab goals and discharge planning possible.

Ask about **performance, not capability**, and corroborate with a collateral informant — patients over-report. Probes: who shops and how do they get there; who manages the tablets; who pays the bills; can they shower unaided; falls; still driving. Timed Up and Go and gait speed add objectivity. Barthel has a **ceiling effect** — a perfect score says nothing about IADLs.

**Why it changes decisions**: it separates acute decline from long-standing dependence, sets rehab goals, and determines whether "home with a package" is realistic. Unexplained IADL decline should prompt cognitive assessment, mood screening and a medication review, not a referral for home help.

## 4.4 Urinary and faecal incontinence

**The short version**

- Types: **stress, urge, overflow, functional, mixed** — separable on history.
- **Screen reversible causes first**: infection, constipation, delirium, drugs, restricted mobility, atrophic vaginitis, polyuria.
- Assessment: bladder diary, examination (abdomen, PR, pelvic/prostate), urinalysis/MSU, **post-void residual**. Ladder: **behaviour → pelvic floor training and bladder retraining → drugs → containment/surgery.**
- ⚑ **Avoid oxybutynin in the frail or cognitively impaired** — high anticholinergic load, crosses the BBB.
- ⚑ **Overflow diarrhoea from impaction** is the classic trap — liquid stool past a hard plug, mistreated with loperamide.



| Type | History | Mechanism / setting |
|---|---|---|
| Stress | Leak on cough/sneeze/lift; small volumes; no urge | Pelvic floor or sphincter deficiency; parity, post-menopause, post-prostatectomy |
| Urge | Sudden undeferrable urge, larger volumes, nocturia | Detrusor overactivity; idiopathic or neurological (stroke, PD, MS, dementia) |
| Overflow | Dribbling, hesitancy, poor stream, palpable bladder, high PVR | Obstruction (BPH, stricture, constipation) or underactive detrusor (diabetic neuropathy, anticholinergics) |
| Functional | Normal tract; can't reach the toilet in time | Mobility, dexterity, vision, cognition, restraints |
| Mixed | Features of more than one | Common in older women — treat the worse component first |

**Reversible contributors**: symptomatic UTI (not asymptomatic bacteriuria); constipation; delirium; **drugs — diuretics, alpha-blockers, sedatives, opioids, anticholinergics, CCBs (oedema → nocturnal diuresis), SGLT2 inhibitors, caffeine, alcohol**; restricted mobility; atrophic vaginitis; polyuria from hyperglycaemia or hypercalcaemia.

**Assessment**: a *bladder diary* over ~3 days separates polyuria from frequency, and stress from urge. *Post-void residual* by bladder scan after voiding — cut-offs vary locally, but **PVR must be measured before drug treatment of urge incontinence**, since an antimuscarinic in an obstructed bladder precipitates retention.

**Ladder**

1. **Continence-promoting behaviour** — treat constipation, morning-dose diuretics, sensible fluids, less caffeine and alcohol, accessible toilet, raised seat, night commode, lighting, easy clothing. In dementia, **prompted/timed voiding** is the mainstay.
2. **Pelvic floor muscle training** — first-line for stress and mixed incontinence in women (supervised beats a leaflet); effective post-prostatectomy. **Bladder retraining** with progressive deferment is first-line for urge.
3. **Drugs** — antimuscarinics (solifenacin, darifenacin, tolterodine) or the beta-3 agonist mirabegron. ⚑ Prefer low CNS-penetration agents or mirabegron in the old and cognitively impaired; **avoid oxybutynin**, as cumulative anticholinergic exposure is linked to cognitive impairment and dementia risk. **Topical vaginal oestrogen** helps atrophic urgency.
4. **Containment** (pads, pull-ups, condom drainage) — for dignity and skin protection, but after assessment, not instead of it. ⚑ **Indwelling catheters are not a continence treatment**: reserve for retention, sacral pressure injury needing a dry field, or palliative comfort. Surgery: sling/colposuspension, prostate surgery.

**Faecal incontinence**: impaction with overflow; sphincter or pudendal damage; neurological disease; diarrhoeal states; rectal prolapse; functional. PR for tone, squeeze, loading, mass, prolapse, perianal sensation. Manage with disimpaction, then a **planned bowel regimen** (toileting after meals to use the gastrocolic reflex, consistency modified with fibre or loperamide) and barrier skin care.

**Constipation**: drugs (opioids, anticholinergics, iron, CCBs, antacids), immobility, dehydration, low fibre, hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, diabetes, Parkinson's, cord lesions, painful anorectal conditions — and **always exclude colorectal cancer** if new, with bleeding, weight loss or anaemia. Australian ladder: non-drug measures → **bulk-forming (psyllium)**, unsuitable if immobile or poorly hydrated → **osmotic: macrogol first-line** → **stimulant (senna, bisacodyl)**, often with an osmotic → docusate is weak alone → **rectal measures** for impaction. ⚑ **Opioids need a prophylactic aperient prescribed with the opioid** — tolerance does not develop.

## 4.5 Pressure injuries

**The short version**

- Injury to skin and underlying tissue from **sustained pressure, shear and friction** over a bony prominence, amplified by moisture and poor perfusion.
- Risk = immobility + malnutrition + moisture + impaired sensation + poor perfusion + shear + frailty. Screen on admission with a validated tool (Braden, Waterlow, Norton) **plus head-to-toe skin inspection**.
- Stages 1–4 by depth, plus **unstageable** and **deep tissue injury**. Prevention: repositioning, support surfaces, skin and moisture care, nutrition, heel offloading, early mobilisation.
- ⚑ Pressure injury is **HAC 1 on the ACSQHC hospital-acquired complications list** and a named harm in the Comprehensive Care Standard.

| Stage | Essence (described, not reproduced) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Skin intact; localised **non-blanchable** redness over a bony prominence. In darkly pigmented skin look for temperature and induration rather than colour. |
| 2 | **Partial-thickness** dermal loss — shallow wound with a viable pink-red base, or a serum-filled blister. No slough or fat visible. Not for skin tears or moisture damage. |
| 3 | **Full-thickness** loss with subcutaneous fat visible; bone, tendon and muscle **not** exposed. Depth varies by site — shallow at the ear, deep at the sacrum. |
| 4 | Full-thickness loss **exposing bone, tendon, muscle or joint**. Osteomyelitis risk. |
| Unstageable | Full-thickness loss with the base **obscured by slough or eschar**; stageable once visible. **Stable dry adherent eschar on an ischaemic heel is left alone.** |
| Deep tissue injury | **Intact or blistered skin** with persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon or purple discolouration. Damage begins at the bone–muscle interface and may declare rapidly. |

**Sites**: sacrum, heels, occiput, scapulae (supine); greater trochanter, lateral malleolus, ear (side-lying); ischial tuberosities (sitting — chair-bound patients are high risk). **Device-related** injuries take the device's shape — look under masks, probes, collars, casts.

**Prevention**: documented individualised repositioning, 30° tilt not 90° side-lying, slide sheets, head of bed low to limit shear; high-specification foam mattress minimum (alternating-pressure if higher risk) plus a chair cushion; **float heels**; daily skin inspection; barrier creams for incontinence-associated dermatitis; **do not massage reddened prominences**; nutrition; early mobilisation.

**Management**: treat the cause — offload completely, optimise nutrition and perfusion, manage moisture and pain. Match dressing to wound: debride necrotic or sloughy wounds (autolytic hydrogel, enzymatic, or sharp/surgical if extensive or septic); hydrogel or hydrocolloid if dry; foam or alginate/hydrofibre for heavy exudate. Systemic antibiotics only for clinical infection — **do not treat colonisation**. Revisit the plan if no improvement in 2 weeks.

## 4.6 Malnutrition and unintentional weight loss

**The short version**

- Under-recognised: Australian studies find tens of percent of older inpatients and aged care residents malnourished or at risk.
- Screen with **MST** (weight loss + appetite; **≥2 = at risk**) or **MNA/MNA-SF**; a positive screen triggers **dietitian assessment**, not a supplement drink alone.
- Structured "**D**" causes: dentition, dysphagia, dysgeusia, depression, dementia, drugs, disease, diarrhoea, disability, dollars, dislike of the food. Consequences: sarcopenia, falls, impaired healing, infection, delirium, mortality.
- ⚑ **PEG in advanced dementia does not prolong survival, prevent aspiration, or heal or prevent pressure injuries** — careful hand feeding preferred.
- ⚑ Malnutrition is a **hospital-acquired complication in its own right** on the ACSQHC HAC list (as is pressure injury) and is a mandated Comprehensive Care Standard screen.

**Assessment.** MNA-SF adds mobility, acute illness/stress, neuropsychological problems and BMI or calf circumference to intake and weight loss. Full assessment: weight trajectory and % loss, BMI (interpret cautiously — oedema, amputation, vertebral height loss), diet history, grip strength, muscle and fat stores. ⚑ **Albumin measures inflammation, not nutrition** — a normal albumin does not exclude malnutrition.

**Causes by category**: medical (malignancy, heart failure, COPD, CKD, hyperthyroidism, malabsorption, pain); oral (dentures, xerostomia, candidiasis); neurological (post-stroke dysphagia, Parkinson's, feeding apraxia); psychiatric (**depression — leading and treatable**); drugs (digoxin, metformin, SSRIs, opioids); social (can't shop or cook, poverty, isolation).

**Management**: treat the cause; **food first** — fortification (cream, butter, milk powder, oils), small frequent energy-dense meals, preferred food, protected mealtimes, feeding assistance, and **stopping unnecessary therapeutic diets** (a low-salt "diabetic" diet in a malnourished frail resident does net harm); **oral supplements between meals**, not instead of them; dietitian referral for every positive screen. Enteral feeding suits **time-limited reversible** situations (acute stroke, head and neck cancer).

**PEG in advanced dementia** ⚑ — no survival benefit; aspiration continues (oropharyngeal secretions and refluxed feed); no pressure injury or functional benefit. Against real burdens: site infection and leakage, agitation, restraints, diarrhoea, loss of the pleasure and human contact of eating. **Careful hand feeding** is preferred. Difficulty eating in advanced dementia **marks the terminal phase** — the response is a goals-of-care conversation, not a procedure.

**The exam stem — dependent 75-year-old with Alzheimer's in a nursing home, refusing food for two days.** Two days is an **acute change**: a symptom to diagnose, not a nutrition problem to bypass.

1. **Reversible cause first** — delirium and its precipitants (UTI, pneumonia, dehydration, drug change, retention); **pain** (arthritis, unwitnessed fracture, pressure injury); **oral/dental** problems (candidiasis, ulcers, abscess, ill-fitting dentures); **depression**; **new medications**; **impaction**; new dysphagia.
2. **Assess** — collateral history of baseline intake, observe a meal, obs, examine the **mouth**, chest, abdomen, PR, skin; bloods for infection, electrolytes, calcium, glucose; medication chart review.
3. **Treat what you find**, plus feeding assistance, texture modification, preferred foods, oral care and hydration.
4. **Do not jump to NG or PEG.** If nothing reversible is found and this is end-stage dementia, the answer is goals of care with the substitute decision-maker, comfort feeding and palliative care.

**Refeeding syndrome**: refeeding a chronically underfed patient drives insulin-mediated intracellular shift of phosphate, potassium and magnesium. Risk: very low BMI, large weight loss, negligible intake for days, low electrolytes before feeding, alcohol. Features: **hypophosphataemia** (hallmark), hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, thiamine deficiency, fluid overload → arrhythmias, cardiac and respiratory failure, Wernicke's. Prevention: **thiamine before and during early feeding**, start at reduced energy, replace electrolytes daily.

## 4.7 Sensory impairment

**The short version**

- Vision and hearing loss are common, under-detected, and routinely mistaken for cognitive decline.
- Consequences: **falls, delirium, isolation, depression, apparent dementia, medication errors, poor rehab participation.**
- Hearing loss is an established **modifiable dementia risk factor**. The interventions are cheap and unglamorous — glasses on, hearing aids in, wax out, light on.

**Vision**: presbyopia, cataract, AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy. Ask about reading, TV, faces and driving; check acuity **with their usual glasses**. Cataract surgery reduces falls. ⚑ **Multifocal/bifocal lenses increase falls risk** on stairs and kerbs — single-vision distance glasses for outdoor walking is a genuine falls intervention.

**Hearing**: presbycusis (bilateral, high-frequency, worse in noise), **cerumen impaction — check first**, ototoxicity (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, cisplatin). Face the patient, cut background noise, speak lower-pitched rather than louder, use a ward amplifier, refer to audiology, maintain aids — one left in the bedside drawer helps no one. **Dual sensory impairment compounds risk**, and sensory deprivation is a target of delirium prevention.

## 4.8 Immobility and hospital-associated deconditioning

**The short version**

- Bed rest is a **treatment with side effects**, and the dose–response is steep in older people.
- Ten days of strict bed rest in **healthy** older volunteers cost roughly **1 kg of leg lean mass (~1.5 kg whole-body)** — more than young adults lose in 28 days.
- Cascade: immobility → sarcopenia and weakness → falls and dependence → longer stay, institutionalisation, death.
- **Early mobilisation is a clinical intervention**, not a courtesy — it belongs on the ward-round problem list.

| System | Complication of immobility |
|---|---|
| Musculoskeletal | • Muscle atrophy and strength loss • Contractures • Disuse osteoporosis |
| Cardiovascular | • **VTE — DVT and PE** • Orthostatic hypotension • Reduced cardiac reserve |
| Respiratory | • Atelectasis and **hospital-acquired pneumonia** • Poor secretion clearance • Aspiration if fed supine |
| Skin / GI / GU | • Pressure injuries • Constipation and impaction • Retention, incontinence, CAUTI |
| Neuro / metabolic | • Delirium • Depression, apathy, learned dependence • Insulin resistance, negative nitrogen balance, anorexia |

**What to do**: mobilise on day one unless genuinely contraindicated; sit out for meals; walk to the toilet rather than use a bottle or pan; **remove tethers** (IV lines, catheters, telemetry, oxygen tubing) early — every attachment is a mobility restraint. Early physiotherapy with a documented mobility plan. Treat pain, delirium and postural hypotension: they are the reasons people won't move. Footwear and aids on the ward; avoid restraints; VTE prophylaxis. ⚑ Australian programmes such as Eat Walk Engage sit within the Comprehensive Care Standard framework.

## 4.9 Elder abuse — recognition and response

**The short version**

- A single or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, **within a relationship of expected trust**, causing harm or distress to an older person.
- Forms: **psychological, financial, neglect, physical, sexual** — often more than one at once.
- ⚑ AIFS National Elder Abuse Prevalence Study (2021): **~15% of community-dwelling Australians 65+ reported abuse in the past year; psychological commonest (~12%)**, then neglect (~3%), financial (~2%), physical (~2%), sexual (~1%). **Adult children are the largest perpetrator group** and dominate financial abuse. Texts calling financial abuse commonest describe **helpline caseloads**, not prevalence.
- Ask **alone, directly and routinely** when risk features are present. **1800 ELDERHelp — 1800 353 374**; aged care providers also report under **SIRS**.

| Form | Presentation clues |
|---|---|
| Psychological | • Fearfulness, withdrawal, deference to a companion who answers for them • Threats of being "put in a home" • Isolation |
| Financial | • Can't pay for care despite apparent means • Sudden change of will or power of attorney • Missing possessions • A relative controlling the money who resists services (they cost money and bring witnesses) |
| Neglect | • Pressure injuries, dehydration, malnutrition, poor hygiene • Untreated problems, medications not given |
| Physical | • Injury inconsistent with the explanation • Bruises in unusual sites (inner arms, trunk, neck, ears) • Injuries of different ages • Delayed presentation |
| Sexual | • Genital/anal injury, bleeding, STI • New distress with personal care |

**Risk factors** — victim: cognitive impairment, dependence, isolation, advanced age, prior family violence. Perpetrator: carer stress, financial dependence on the older person, substance misuse, shared housing, inheritance entitlement. Systemic: understaffed residential care, ageism.

**How to ask**: see them **alone**, on a plausible pretext; normalise ("we ask everyone about safety at home") then be specific — do you feel safe at home; does anyone use your money without asking; has anyone hurt or threatened you. Professional interpreter, **never a family member**.

**Capacity and undue influence.** Distinguish a **capable person making an unwise choice** (their right) from one produced by **impaired capacity or undue influence**. Capacity is decision- and time-specific and presumed until shown otherwise; the threshold rises with the gravity of the transaction. Undue influence is about **process** — isolation from independent advice, dependency on the beneficiary, secrecy and urgency, a decision inconsistent with known values. Check whether an enduring power of attorney exists and whether the attorney acts within scope; involve the public advocate or state tribunal (NCAT, VCAT).

**Response pathway**: treat injuries, assess capacity and risk, admit if there is no safe discharge → social work, geriatrician, GP (carer stress may be the driver; respite may be the intervention) → **1800 ELDERHelp (1800 353 374)** for state/territory helplines → **police** for assault, fraud or theft → **guardianship tribunal** if capacity impaired → **SIRS**: approved aged care providers report eight incident types (unreasonable force; sexual misconduct; psychological or emotional abuse; unexpected death; staff theft or financial coercion; neglect; inappropriate restrictive practices; unexplained absence) to the ACQSC — **Priority 1 within 24 h, Priority 2 within 30 days**. ⚑ Clinician mandatory reporting is **not uniform across Australian jurisdictions**, but professional duties, SIRS obligations and criminal law still apply.

# Geriatrics Notes — Section 5: Capacity, Ethics and End-of-Life Care
*Sources: End of Life Law in Australia (QUT), Advance Care Planning Australia, ACSQHC National Consensus Statement on end-of-life care + Comprehensive Care Standard, NSW Health/CEC Last Days of Life resources, Victorian DH (Medical Treatment Planning and Decisions Act), NCAT guardianship guidance, palliAGED, CareSearch, Therapeutic Guidelines (Palliative Care), Coroners Court of Victoria. Scope: capacity assessment, substitute decision-making, ACP, goals of care, ethics, terminal symptom control and death certification in the older patient — written-paper level, Australian practice.*

## 5.1 Capacity to consent

**The short version**
- Capacity is **presumed** in every adult until the contrary is shown. The burden sits on the person alleging incapacity, not on the patient to prove competence.
- Capacity is **decision-specific** and **time-specific**. A patient can lack capacity for a Whipple and retain it for a flu vaccine, and can lack it at 0300 with delirium and have it back by Thursday.
- Four functional abilities are assessed: **understand → retain → use/weigh → communicate**.
- ⚑ Capacity is **not** determined by age, diagnosis (including dementia or mental illness), disability, appearance, communication difficulty, eccentric or "unwise" choice, or the clinician disagreeing with the decision. This is the classic exam distractor.
- A patient with capacity may refuse life-sustaining treatment for any reason or no reason, and that refusal binds you.

### The functional test
| Element | What you are actually testing | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Understand** | Nature of the problem, proposed treatment, realistic alternatives (incl. doing nothing), material risks/benefits | Cannot paraphrase back why surgery is offered |
| **Retain** | Holds it *long enough to make this decision* — not permanently | Amnestic patient re-asks every 90 s, cannot bridge to a conclusion |
| **Use / weigh** | Compares options, applies own values, appreciates it applies **to them** | Accepts the facts but says "I don't have cancer" — failure of appreciation, the commonest genuine deficit |
| **Communicate** | By any means — speech, writing, blink, board, interpreter | Fails only if *no* channel exists after real effort |

All four are required. Failing any one, after optimisation, = lacks capacity for that decision.

### Assessed vs NOT assessed (learn this table)
| **Assessed** | **NOT assessed / does not of itself establish incapacity** |
|---|---|
| • Understanding of the specific decision<br>• Retention long enough to decide<br>• Ability to use/weigh, incl. appreciation it applies to self<br>• Ability to communicate a choice | • **Age** — being 92 is not incapacity<br>• **Diagnosis** — dementia, delirium hx, schizophrenia, ID, ABI, stroke<br>• **MMSE/MoCA score** — supports, never substitutes<br>• **Disability**, incl. aphasia, deafness, blindness<br>• **Communication difficulty** or need for an interpreter<br>• **The decision being unwise, risky or against advice**<br>• **Clinician or family disagreement** with the outcome<br>• Unconventional lifestyle, religious/political beliefs, sexuality<br>• Residence in RACF or being under guardianship for *other* matters<br>• Being an involuntary mental health patient (separate legal stream) |

⚑ Wording is codified differently across jurisdictions, but the presumption of capacity, the decision-specific approach and "unwise decision ≠ incapacity" are consistent nationally and with common law. Do not cite UK Mental Capacity Act 2005 sections in an Australian paper — the principles overlap, the statutory frame does not.

### Fluctuating capacity
Delirium, DLB, post-ictal states, hypoglycaemia, sundowning, sedative load, hepatic/uraemic encephalopathy. Assess during a **lucid interval** where the decision can wait; defer non-urgent decisions until the reversible cause is treated — **treat delirium first, then assess**. If genuinely intermittent and the decision cannot wait, document the assessment at the time of the decision and re-assess for each new decision.

### Maximising capacity before you judge it
Optimise first, or the assessment is invalid:
- Hearing aids in and working, glasses on, quiet room, face the patient.
- Professional **interpreter** (not the daughter); Aboriginal Liaison Officer / Health Practitioner where relevant.
- Pick the patient's best time of day; avoid post-medication rounds and the post-prandial slump.
- Treat reversibles: delirium, pain, constipation, retention, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, sedatives, alcohol withdrawal, severe depression.
- Chunk information, plain language, written/pictorial aids, repeat across sittings; allow a supporter (**supported decision-making** — supporting a decision is not making it for them).

### Documentation
Record the *decision in question*, the information given, the patient's own words demonstrating each of the four elements, the optimisation steps, the conclusion and who was involved. "Confused — not competent" is indefensible. Any treating doctor can assess capacity; psychiatry/geriatrics referral is for complex, contested or high-stakes cases (testamentary capacity, atypical refusal of life-saving treatment), not routine.

## 5.2 When capacity is absent — the Australian substitute decision-making framework

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Substitute decision-making is state/territory legislation.** The *shape* is national; the *names* are not — describe the hierarchy generically, then name the local term.
- General hierarchy: **valid advance care directive → person the patient appointed → tribunal-appointed guardian → statutory "default" person (spouse/partner → unpaid carer → close relative/friend) → public guardian/advocate**.
- ⚑ An enduring power of attorney for **financial** matters does **not** confer health decision-making power. Separate instrument, separate power.
- Substitutes apply **substituted judgement** — what *this patient* would have decided — falling back to best interests only when preferences are unknowable.
- **Emergency**: treat without consent to save life, prevent serious damage to health, or relieve significant pain/distress. Do not delay resuscitation to ring a daughter in Perth.

### The hierarchy in practice
1. **Valid, applicable advance care directive** — a valid refusal outranks everyone, including the family and the appointed substitute. Substitutes cannot override the patient's own recorded refusal.
2. **Patient-appointed decision-maker** — appointed *while the patient had capacity*, under the local instrument (enduring guardian, EPOA for personal/health matters, medical treatment decision maker).
3. **Tribunal-appointed guardian** — where none appointed, or the appointment is contested/exhausted. Made by **NCAT** (NSW), **VCAT** (Vic), **QCAT** (Qld), **SACAT**, **SAT** (WA), **TASCAT**, **ACAT** (ACT), NTCAT.
4. **Statutory default person** where nobody was appointed. NSW "**person responsible**": guardian with the relevant function → spouse/de facto in a close continuing relationship → **unpaid** carer (incl. the person who was the unpaid carer before RACF entry) → close relative or friend with a personal interest. Victoria (MTPDA 2016): appointed medical treatment decision maker → VCAT guardian → spouse/domestic partner → primary carer → oldest adult child → oldest parent → oldest adult sibling; the **first willing and available person only**, one decision maker at a time. ⚑ Other states use similar categories in their own order — do not assume NSW or Vic order applies elsewhere.
5. **Public Guardian / Public Advocate** — backstop where nobody suitable exists, or as the tribunal's appointee. Also the route for disputes and for decisions the default person cannot make.

### Financial vs personal/health powers
| Domain | Instrument (generic) | Covers | Does **not** cover |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial | Enduring power of attorney (financial) | • Bank accounts, bills, property<br>• Selling the house, RAD | Consent to/refusal of treatment |
| Personal / health | Enduring guardian (NSW/WA); EPOA personal/health (Qld, ACT); medical treatment decision maker (Vic) | • Treatment consent/refusal<br>• Where the person lives<br>• Services accessed | Financial transactions |
| Tribunal-appointed | Administrator / financial manager | Finances only | Health decisions |

⚑ Some jurisdictions bundle both into a single enduring document with separable powers; others keep them strictly separate. **Read the actual document** — check it is signed, witnessed, not revoked, and that the relevant power is ticked. A common exam and ward error is accepting "I have power of attorney" as authority to consent to surgery.

### Limits on substitutes, and treatment without consent
- Cannot consent to what the patient **actively objects** to — that generally needs tribunal authorisation.
- Cannot demand treatment that is not clinically indicated.
- "Special"/restrictive treatments (sterilisation, some psychiatric treatment, restrictive practices, research) need tribunal or specific statutory approval.
- ⚑ Queensland requires substitute consent for withholding/withdrawing life-sustaining measures in a wider set of circumstances than most states — **but if the substitute refuses, the clinician may still withhold or withdraw where continuing would not be good medical practice**, so a substitute cannot compel non-beneficial treatment. Check local law.
- **Emergency**: treatment without consent is lawful where urgent and necessary to save life, prevent serious damage to health, or relieve significant pain/distress, in a patient lacking capacity with no known valid refusal. Also permitted for **minor and uncontroversial** treatment where no person responsible is available and the patient does not object — document the reasoning.

## 5.3 Advance care planning and advance care directives

**The short version**
- ACP is the **process**; an advance care directive (ACD) is one possible **product**. Naming a substitute decision-maker is often the more useful output.
- Two content types: a **values/preferences statement** (guides interpretation, not binding of itself) and an **instructional directive** (a specific consent to or refusal of nominated treatment — legally binding when valid and applicable).
- A directive only operates **once capacity is lost**. While the patient has capacity, ask the patient.
- ⚑ Statutory forms and names differ by jurisdiction, and NSW relies on **common law** directives rather than a statutory form — a clear, capacitous, specific and applicable common-law refusal is still binding.
- A valid, applicable refusal must be followed; overriding it risks battery/assault, not merely a complaint.

### Jurisdictional shorthand (names only — verify local form before relying on it)
| Jurisdiction | Directive commonly called | Appointed decision-maker commonly called |
|---|---|---|
| NSW | Advance Care Directive (**common law**; NSW Health template is not a statutory form) | Enduring guardian; otherwise "person responsible" |
| Vic | Advance Care Directive — with **instructional** and **values** directives | Medical treatment decision maker |
| Qld | Advance Health Directive | Enduring attorney (personal/health); statutory health attorney as default |
| SA | Advance Care Directive (Advance Care Directives Act 2013) | Substitute decision-maker |
| WA | Advance Health Directive | Enduring guardian |
| Tas | Advance Care Directive (statutory scheme introduced by 2021 amendment) | Enduring guardian |
| ACT | Health Direction | Enduring power of attorney; health attorney |
| NT | Advance Personal Plan | Decision maker under the plan |

### When a directive must be followed
Must be **valid** (made with capacity, voluntarily, witnessed per local law, not revoked) **and applicable** (covers the situation that has actually arisen). A refusal of "ventilation for irreversible respiratory failure" may not apply to a reversible aspiration pneumonia — but the values behind it still inform the goals discussion. In genuine doubt, provide life-sustaining treatment while resolving the question urgently; good-faith reliance on an apparently valid directive is protected in most jurisdictions.

### Who should have one, and when to raise it
Advanced chronic disease, frailty (CFS ≥5), dementia at diagnosis while capacity persists, RACF entry, dialysis, major surgery, or the "surprise question" group (*would you be surprised if this patient died within 12 months?* — no = raise it). Best raised in general practice when stable, not at 2 am in resus. Triggers: 75+ health assessment, new dementia diagnosis, RACF entry, post-hospitalisation and care plan reviews.

### Common failure modes
- **Nobody can find it** — in a drawer at home or the GP file, not the eMR or My Health Record. Fix: upload, scan on admission, copy to RACF and NOK, flag in the eMR.
- **Family override it.** Family have no authority to do so; the appointed substitute is bound by it too. Their distress is real, but is not managed by ignoring the document.
- **Written without medical input** — "no machines", "no heroic measures" is uninterpretable and generates the very disputes it was meant to prevent.
- **Out of date** — written pre-diagnosis; re-check against current views if capacity permits.
- **Directive but no substitute appointed**, so nobody has authority for what the document does not cover.

## 5.4 Goals of care and treatment limitation

**The short version**
- Goals-of-care forms (state variants: Resuscitation Plan, ARP, Goals of Patient Care) record the **overall aim of admission** — curative/restorative, curative with limits, or comfort/terminal — plus specific limitations.
- ⚑ **Not-for-CPR ≠ not-for-treatment.** An NFR patient can still have antibiotics, fluids, transfusion, NIV, surgery. Conflating them causes real harm and is a favourite exam distractor.
- **CPR is a treatment, not a default rite** — it can be judged non-beneficial when it cannot achieve its purpose (arrest as the terminal event of metastatic disease or end-stage frailty).
- A doctor is **not obliged** to provide treatment that cannot achieve its purpose — but this must be **communicated**, not announced. Consent is not needed to withhold non-beneficial treatment in most jurisdictions (⚑ Qld differs); discussion is still obligatory under the ACSQHC consensus statement.
- Withholding and withdrawing are ethically and legally equivalent — a stoppable trial of therapy beats never starting.

### Structuring the conversation
1. **Prepare** — know the trajectory, the reversibility, the baseline function/CFS. Right people, quiet room, interpreter.
2. **Ask what they understand** and have been told; seek permission to discuss the bigger picture.
3. **Explore values** — what matters most, what would be unacceptable, prior experience of ICU/CPR.
4. **Recommend**, don't offer a menu: "I'd recommend we treat the pneumonia fully but not attempt CPR, because it would not bring you back." "Do you want everything done?" shifts an unfair burden onto families.
5. **Name the limits explicitly** and say what happens if things worsen (ward-level ceiling; NIV yes / intubation no).
6. **Document** on the local form; communicate to team, GP, RACF and family; review on change of condition.

### Non-beneficial ("futile") treatment
"Futile" has largely been retired in Australian documents in favour of **non-beneficial** or **potentially inappropriate**, because pure physiological futility is rare and most disputes are really about value-laden benefit. Frame it as *this treatment cannot achieve the goal you and I care about*. Where disagreement persists: second opinion, palliative care/ICU liaison, clinical ethics consultation, time-limited trial with agreed endpoints, tribunal or Supreme Court as last resort.

## 5.5 The ethical framework in older patients

**The short version**
- The four principles (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) still frame it, but the recurring geriatric tension is **autonomy vs beneficence** — the capacitous patient who wants to go home to squalor.
- **Dignity of risk**: a person with capacity has the right to take risks. Risk-averse paternalism is itself a harm.
- The patient is the client, not the family. **Wishes of the family do not override wishes of the patient**, capacitous or not.
- Confidentiality persists in dementia; carers need enough information to care safely, not everything.
- ⚑ For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients and many CALD families, decision-making may legitimately be **collective** — this is not a capacity problem, and accommodating it is good practice, not a concession.

### The unsafe discharge
A capacitous patient may discharge to a home you consider unsafe. Obligations: assess and document capacity for *this specific decision*; disclose risks plainly; **minimise** rather than eliminate risk (OT home assessment, community services, personal alarm, falls modifications, meals, GP follow-up); leave the door open for return. Escalate to guardianship only where capacity is genuinely absent and risk serious — it is not a tool for enforcing clinician preference.

### Family, confidentiality and carers
- Ask the capacitous patient who may be told what, and record it. With incapacity, disclose to the substitute what the decision requires; others get what the patient would have wanted them to have.
- **Elder abuse** — ⚑ **psychological abuse is the commonest form by prevalence (~12%, AIFS 2021)**; financial abuse (~2%) tops helpline caseloads, which is where the "financial is commonest" claim comes from. Adult children are the largest perpetrator group. Red flags: new EPOA signed under pressure, unexplained withdrawals, a relative who blocks private consultation, escalating neglect. Always see the patient alone at some point.
- Collusion ("don't tell Dad it's cancer") — explore the fear behind it and ask what the patient wants to know, but do not lie to a patient who wants the truth.

### Cultural considerations
- Offer, don't assume: many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients want family/community involvement, may wish to return to Country to die, and may have protocols around naming and images of the deceased. Involve Aboriginal Health Workers/Liaison Officers early; ask about Sorry Business.
- Accredited interpreters for all consent and goals-of-care discussions — family interpreters distort in both directions.
- Where family request non-disclosure on cultural grounds, the patient's own wishes about being informed remain the reference point.

## 5.6 Palliative and end-of-life care in the older person

**The short version**
- **Trajectories**: cancer = good function then steep terminal decline over weeks–months; organ failure (CCF, COPD) = sawtooth of exacerbations, any of which may be fatal, so prognosis is opaque; **frailty/dementia = low function, prolonged slow decline over years**. Frailty patients are under-referred to palliative care because there is no single dramatic drop.
- **Recognising dying** (days): profound weakness, bed-bound, minimal intake, drowsy, unable to swallow tablets, Cheyne–Stokes, mottled peripheries, terminal secretions.
- Act on it: stop non-essential drugs and observations, convert essentials to subcut, **prescribe anticipatory ("just in case") medications**, address hydration expectations, and tell the family plainly that the person is dying.
- **Double effect**: proportionate, titrated symptom relief is lawful even if it may hasten death, because the intention is symptom control. Well-titrated opioids in the dying do not measurably shorten life.
- ⚑ Voluntary assisted dying is legislated and operating in **all six states plus the ACT** (ACT commenced 3 November 2025); the **NT** remains the exception. Eligibility and process are jurisdiction-specific; conscientious objection is permitted, but some jurisdictions impose minimum information/referral duties on objectors.

### Anticipatory subcutaneous prescribing (last days)
Prescribe all five domains PRN before they are needed, plus a syringe driver if symptoms recur. Doses below are **opioid-naive adult starting points** from Australian last-days-of-life guidance — reduce in frail, elderly and renally impaired patients and titrate to effect; check local formulary.

| Symptom | Drug (subcut) | Typical opioid-naive PRN start | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain | **Morphine** | 2.5 mg 1-hourly PRN | First-line for most. If already on opioids, PRN = ~1/6 of the 24 h subcut dose |
| Dyspnoea | **Morphine** | **2.5 mg subcut 1-hourly PRN, max 15 mg/24 h in the opioid-naive** — ⚑ NSW CEC gives the **same** starting dose as for pain, despite the common teaching that breathlessness needs less | Oxygen helps only the hypoxic; add benzo if anxiety-driven |
| Nausea/vomiting | ⚑ **Haloperidol (1st line)** → metoclopramide (2nd) — NSW CEC anticipatory prescribing | **Haloperidol 1 mg subcut 4-hourly PRN (max 3 mg/24 h)**; metoclopramide 10 mg 8-hourly PRN (max 30 mg/24 h) | Avoid metoclopramide in complete bowel obstruction and Parkinson's; haloperidol preferred in renal impairment |
| Agitation / terminal restlessness | **Haloperidol** (delirium) or **midazolam** (anxiety/distress) | Haloperidol 0.5–1 mg; midazolam 2.5 mg 2-hourly | Always exclude retention, faecal loading, pain, opioid toxicity, hypoxia first |
| Respiratory secretions | **Hyoscine butylbromide** or **glycopyrrolate** | Hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg 4-hourly; glycopyrronium 200 mcg 4-hourly | Neither dries existing secretions — **give early**; reposition; ⚑ do **not** suction routinely; hyoscine butylbromide does not cross the BBB (no sedation/delirium), unlike hyoscine **hydro**bromide |

### Pain and opioids in the older/renal patient
- ⚑ **Morphine accumulates in renal impairment** (M6G) → myoclonus, sedation, delirium, respiratory depression. eGFR <30: prefer **fentanyl** or **hydromorphone** (specialist advice), or morphine at reduced dose/extended interval with close review.
- Oral→subcut morphine ≈ **3:1** (30 mg PO ≈ 10 mg subcut). Always verify against a current conversion chart.
- On **rotating** opioid or route for toxicity/inadequate analgesia, calculate the equianalgesic dose then **reduce by 25–50%** for incomplete cross-tolerance, and provide adequate breakthrough.
- Breakthrough = **1/6 of the total 24 h dose**. Always co-prescribe an aperient. Methadone: specialist only.
- Opioid **toxicity** = drowsiness + myoclonus + pinpoint pupils + delirium ± respiratory depression. Reduce/rotate and hydrate first; naloxone only for genuine life-threatening respiratory depression, in small titrated doses (a full dose precipitates crisis pain and withdrawal).

### Other terminal-phase points
- **Oxygen** relieves dyspnoea only in the **hypoxic**. Otherwise a **fan**, cool air, positioning, breathing technique and low-dose opioid do more; oxygen adds masks, tethering and dry mouth.
- **Hydration/nutrition**: artificial hydration in the last days does not reliably improve comfort and may worsen secretions and oedema — mouth care is what matters. PEG feeding in advanced dementia does not prolong life or prevent aspiration.
- **Deprescribe**: statins, bisphosphonates, antihypertensives, oral hypoglycaemics, anticoagulants, vitamins, dementia drugs. Keep analgesia, antiemetics, anxiolytics, antisecretories, anticonvulsants (convert to subcut).
- **Terminal delirium** distresses families; explain it, treat reversibles, haloperidol ± midazolam, avoid restraints.
- **Double effect** conditions: the act itself is not wrong (giving analgesia); the intention is symptom relief; death is foreseen but is not the means to that relief; the dose is **proportionate** to the symptom. Escalating doses against escalating symptoms, documented, is lawful. A large dose in the absence of symptoms is not.
- **VAD** is a distinct, patient-initiated legal process — not palliative care, not double effect. Eligibility broadly requires an adult with decision-making capacity, an advanced progressive terminal condition with a defined prognosis, residency criteria, an enduring voluntary request and multiple independent assessments — ⚑ **specifics vary by state; verify locally**.

## 5.7 Death certification and after-death care

**The short version**
- If you can state a probable natural cause of death and none of the reportable criteria apply → complete the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death.
- **Reportable to the coroner** (broadly, and worded slightly differently in each state): violent, unnatural, unexpected or accidental death; death of unknown cause; unidentified person; death during or shortly after a medical/surgical/anaesthetic procedure where unexpected; death in custody or in care (incl. under mental health orders); death where no doctor is able to certify within the statutory period.
- ⚑ Death in a RACF is **not automatically reportable** in most jurisdictions if expected and from a known natural cause — but "death in care" definitions vary, and a fall-related fracture followed weeks later by death **is** injury-related and usually reportable. Check state criteria.
- Practical order: verify and document → notify NOK, GP, RACF → certify or report to coroner → cremation paperwork → ⚑ **deactivate/remove implanted defibrillator before cremation**.

Verification of death: no central pulse, no heart or breath sounds over an adequate observation period, fixed unreactive pupils, no response to central stimulus. Record the time of **verification** (not necessarily time of death), the findings, and who was informed. Bereavement care — follow-up contact, grief service information, a route back for questions — is part of the episode of care under the ACSQHC framework, not an optional extra.

## 6.3 Confusable pairs

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| **Delirium vs dementia** | Hours–days and fluctuating with impaired attention and altered consciousness, vs months–years, progressive, attention and consciousness preserved until late |
| **Delirium vs depression** | Inattention and clouded consciousness vs intact attention if engaged, clear sensorium, mood-congruent content; delirium worse at night, depression worse in the morning |
| **Dementia vs depression** | Attitude to deficits: dementia conceals, confabulates, tries hard; depression complains, gives "I don't know", poor effort but benefits from cueing |
| **Hypoactive delirium vs depression** | Both quiet and withdrawn — but delirium has acute onset, fluctuation and *inattention*; depression has weeks–months of anhedonia with testable attention |
| **Alzheimer's vs vascular dementia** | Smooth insidious episodic-memory-first decline vs stepwise course, vascular risk factors, focal signs, executive/processing loss with early gait and urinary change |
| **DLB vs Parkinson's disease dementia** | The one-year rule — dementia before or within 1 year of parkinsonism = DLB; dementia >1 year after established motor PD = PDD. Same pathology, different label |
| **DLB vs Alzheimer's** | DLB fluctuates markedly, has formed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism, RBD and neuroleptic sensitivity; AD has steady progression with early amnesia and no early motor signs |
| **Frontotemporal dementia vs psychiatric illness** | FTD is progressive with loss of empathy, hyperorality, ritualistic behaviour and frontal atrophy in a 50s–60s patient; primary psychiatric illness is episodic, with insight and treatment response, and does not progress on imaging |
| **MCI vs early dementia** | Function. MCI = objective decline with independence preserved; once IADLs fail, it is dementia |
| **NPH vs Parkinson's disease** | NPH gait is wide-based, magnetic and shuffling **with** early urinary incontinence and ventriculomegaly, and arms are spared; PD is asymmetrical with rest tremor, rigidity, small-stepped festinant gait and levodopa response |
| **Pseudodementia vs dementia** | Short datable history, mood change precedes cognition, poor effort, cueing helps, cognition recovers with antidepressant — versus years of decline, confabulation and no cueing benefit |
| **Fall vs syncope vs seizure** | Mechanical fall: trip trigger, no LOC, hands out, immediate recovery. Syncope: seconds of LOC, pale/limp, facial injury, oriented within seconds. Seizure: aura, 1–3 min, lateral tongue bite, post-ictal confusion for minutes–hours |
| **Orthostatic vs vasovagal vs cardiac syncope** | Orthostatic: on standing, drop ≥20/10 within 3 min, drug-related. Vasovagal: emotional/pain/micturition trigger with long autonomic prodrome. Cardiac: exertional or supine, **no prodrome**, palpitations, family history of sudden death, abnormal ECG |
| **Osteoporosis vs osteomalacia vs Paget's** | Osteoporosis: normal calcium/phosphate/ALP, low T-score, fragility fracture. Osteomalacia: low vitamin D, low calcium/phosphate, **high ALP**, bone pain, proximal myopathy. Paget's: isolated markedly high ALP with normal calcium, focal bone pain/deformity |
| **Osteopenia vs osteoporosis on DXA** | T −1.0 to −2.5 vs T ≤ −2.5 — but a minimal-trauma fracture over 50 makes the diagnosis osteoporosis whatever the T-score; in the osteopenic range use FRAX/Garvan to decide treatment |
| **Urge vs stress vs overflow vs functional incontinence** | Urge: sudden undeferrable urge, large volumes, nocturia. Stress: leak on cough/lift, small volumes, no urge. Overflow: hesitancy, dribbling, palpable bladder, high PVR. Functional: normal tract, can't get there — mobility, cognition, environment |
| **Constipation with overflow vs infective diarrhoea** | Overflow: liquid stool past a hard plug, loaded rectum on PR, no fever, opioids/immobility. Infective: acute, febrile, other cases on the ward, empty rectum. Loperamide is the wrong answer in the first |
| **Frailty vs comorbidity vs disability** | Frailty = loss of reserve, so a small stressor causes disproportionate decline (CFS/Fried). Comorbidity = a count of diseases. Disability = established functional dependence. They overlap, but CFS is not for stable long-term disability |
| **Advance care directive vs statement of preferences** | An instructional directive is a specific consent to or refusal of nominated treatment — **legally binding** when valid and applicable; a values/preferences statement guides interpretation but does not of itself bind |
| **Capacity vs competence vs guardianship** | Capacity is the clinical, decision- and time-specific functional assessment (understand/retain/weigh/communicate); competence is the legal conclusion a court reaches; guardianship is a tribunal *appointment* of a substitute for a defined class of decisions |
| **EPOA (financial) vs medical/personal decision maker** | Financial power covers money, bills and property only; treatment consent needs the personal/health instrument (enduring guardian, EPOA personal/health, medical treatment decision maker). Read the actual document |
| **Not-for-CPR vs not-for-active-treatment** | NFR limits **only** attempted resuscitation at arrest — antibiotics, fluids, transfusion, NIV and surgery all continue unless separately limited. Conflating them causes real harm |
| **Double effect vs voluntary assisted dying** | Double effect: intention is symptom relief, dose proportionate and titrated, death foreseen but not the means — lawful everywhere. VAD: a distinct patient-initiated statutory process with eligibility criteria and independent assessments; not palliative care |
| **Delirium tremens vs delirium from other causes** | DT is withdrawal at day 2–4 with prominent tremor, sweating, tachycardia, fever and vivid hallucinations — the one delirium where a benzodiazepine (oxazepam/lorazepam if frail or hepatic) is the treatment rather than the cause |

## 6.4 The must-not-miss list

| Trap / time-critical item | Correct action |
|---|---|
| Benzodiazepine given for undifferentiated delirium | Wrong — treat the cause plus non-pharmacological measures; benzos only for alcohol/benzo withdrawal |
| Antipsychotic in Lewy body dementia or PD | Contraindicated — severe neuroleptic sensitivity; low-dose short-acting benzodiazepine if sedation is unavoidable |
| "Unexplained mechanical fall" with amnesia for the event | Treat as syncope — ECG in everyone, postural BP, echo if murmur, loop recorder if recurrent |
| Denosumab stopped, delayed or lapsed | Rebound vertebral fractures — keep the 6-monthly interval; if stopping, transition immediately to a bisphosphonate |
| Unwitnessed long lie after a fall | Rhabdomyolysis/AKI, hypothermia, pressure injury, aspiration — CK, fluids, skin check, and change the discharge plan |
| Hip fracture waiting on surgery | Nerve block and analgesia now, surgery within 48 h, mobilise day 1 unrestricted, delirium prevention, bone protection before discharge |
| Treating a patient as lacking capacity because the decision is unwise | Not a capacity test — presume capacity, assess the four functional elements, document, respect dignity of risk |
| PEG feeding requested in advanced dementia | No survival, aspiration, pressure injury or functional benefit — careful hand feeding and a goals-of-care conversation |
| Elder abuse missed behind a controlling companion | See the patient alone, professional interpreter, assess capacity and undue influence, 1800 ELDERHelp, police/tribunal/SIRS as indicated |
| Refeeding a chronically underfed patient at full rate | Thiamine before and during feeding, start low, daily phosphate/potassium/magnesium |
| New hyponatraemia in a patient started on an SSRI, thiazide or tramadol | Drug-induced — stop or change the culprit; check sodium at baseline and ~2 weeks |
| Withholding anticoagulation in AF because of falls | Not justified — manage the falls; treat the bleeding risk factors, not the indication |
| Valid advance care directive overridden by distressed family | The directive prevails — family have no authority to override it |

## ⚠ Inconsistencies found in Sections 1–5

1. **Commonest form of elder abuse.** Section 4.9 (citing AIFS 2021) says **psychological** is commonest (~12% vs ~2% financial) and warns that "financial commonest" reflects helpline caseloads, not prevalence. Section 5.5 states "financial abuse is commonest, often by the EPOA holder". These cannot both stand; 4.9 is the better-sourced claim, but the conflict is left visible rather than silently resolved.

2. **Bisphosphonate drug-holiday timing.** Section 2.7 says reassess after **5 years oral / 3 years IV**; Section 3.5 says cessation after **3–6 years** does not alter fracture risk. Compatible in spirit, different figures — quote 2.7's if asked for one number.
