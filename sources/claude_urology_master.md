# Urology Notes — Section 1: Haematuria and Urological Malignancy
*Sources: 2026 Guidelines for the Early Detection of Prostate Cancer in Australia (PCFA/RACGP-endorsed), RACGP Red Book 10th edn, Cancer Council Australia Optimal Care Pathways, EAU Guidelines (NMIBC, MIBC, RCC, UTUC, Prostate Cancer) 2025, WA Health Urology Referral Access Criteria, eviQ. Scope: assessment of haematuria and the four urological malignancies most likely to be examined — bladder, renal, upper tract urothelial and prostate.*

## 1.1 Haematuria

**The short version**
- Visible haematuria (VH) in an adult is malignancy until proven otherwise — full upper + lower tract workup regardless of age, even if it settles and even if a UTI was cultured.
- Non-visible haematuria (NVH) is defined on **microscopy** (≥3 RBC/hpf), not on dipstick alone. Dipstick positive → confirm with MC&S before acting.
- Transient causes must be excluded/treated first: UTI, menstruation, vigorous exercise, recent instrumentation, sexual activity. Recheck 6–12 weeks after treating UTI.
- Standard workup = upper tract imaging (CT IVP/CT urography, or US if young/renal impairment/contrast contraindication) **plus** cystoscopy. Cytology is optional and is a poor rule-out test.
- Proteinuria, dysmorphic RBCs/red cell casts, hypertension or rising creatinine = **nephrological**, not urological — refer renal.

### Definitions and the dipstick trap

Dipstick detects haem peroxidase activity, so it turns positive for myoglobin and free haemoglobin as well as intact RBCs — rhabdomyolysis and haemolysis give "blood +++" with a bland microscopy. Beetroot, rifampicin, phenazopyridine and porphyria discolour urine with no dipstick positivity at all. Sequence in any stem: dipstick → formal MC&S → decide.

NVH is persistent if present on ≥2 of 3 specimens. ⚑ The old "symptomatic vs asymptomatic NVH" split from UK guidance has largely given way to **risk-stratified** pathways: the question is not "is it symptomatic" but "how many malignancy risk factors, and how many RBC/hpf".

### When investigation is *not* warranted

| Situation | Why | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Culture-proven UTI | Inflamed urothelium bleeds | Treat, then **repeat microscopy 6–12 weeks post-treatment**. Persistent haematuria → investigate |
| Menstruation | Contamination | Repeat MSU ≥7 days after menses ends |
| Vigorous exercise (running, contact sport) | Bladder wall trauma, RBC trauma | Repeat after 48–72 h abstinence from exercise |
| Recent catheter/cystoscopy/TURP | Iatrogenic | Repeat after healing interval |
| Known glomerular disease with stable function, proteinuria, dysmorphic RBCs | Not a urothelial problem | Renal referral, not cystoscopy |

⚑ A cultured UTI in a patient with **visible** haematuria does **not** excuse imaging and cystoscopy. Assuming "it was just the UTI" in a 65-year-old smoker is the commonest way bladder cancer is missed — a favourite short-answer trap. Conversely, the truly low-risk NVH patient (<50, <10 pack-years, <10 RBC/hpf, no other risk factors, no LUTS) can reasonably be managed by shared decision-making with repeat urinalysis within 6 months.

Risk factors lowering the threshold to investigate: age >50, male sex, smoking (dose-dependent, dominant modifiable risk), aromatic amine exposure, cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide, pelvic radiotherapy, chronic IDC, analgesic nephropathy, aristolochic acid, schistosomiasis, prior urothelial cancer.

### Investigation pathway

1. **MSU MC&S** — exclude infection, look for dysmorphic RBCs/casts.
2. **Bloods** — UEC/eGFR, FBC, coagulation if anticoagulated, PSA in men where relevant, urine ACR.
3. **Upper tract imaging**
   - **CT IVP / CT urography** — reference standard: renal parenchymal masses, stones, and critically the pelvicalyceal and ureteric urothelium on the delayed excretory phase. Preferred for VH, age >50, or any risk factors.
   - **US KUB** — acceptable for younger patients, renal impairment, contrast allergy, pregnancy. Materially less sensitive for small stones and urothelial lesions, essentially blind to ureteric TCC; a normal US does not exclude upper tract disease in a high-risk patient.
4. **Flexible cystoscopy** — reference standard for bladder and urethra, required for all VH. Imaging cannot exclude flat CIS (velvety erythematous patch, endoscopically subtle).
5. **Urine cytology** — good specificity and reasonable sensitivity for **high-grade** UC and CIS, poor for low-grade papillary tumours. ⚑ An adjunct only; negative cytology never rules out cancer. Most useful when CIS is suspected.

⚑ Anticoagulation does **not** explain haematuria. Warfarin/DOAC use in a patient with VH is an indication to investigate, not to reassure — a significant proportion have an underlying lesion unmasked by the anticoagulant.

### Causes by age

| Age band | Commonest / must-not-miss |
|---|---|
| Children | • Post-streptococcal GN • IgA nephropathy • Alport syndrome • Hypercalciuria • Wilms tumour • HUS |
| Young adults (<40) | • UTI • Stones • Exercise • IgA nephropathy (synpharyngitic VH) • Thin basement membrane disease • Sickle cell trait (papillary necrosis) |
| 40–60 | • Stones • UTI • **Urothelial carcinoma — risk climbing** • BPH • RCC |
| >60 | • **Bladder urothelial carcinoma** • RCC • Prostate cancer/BPH • Radiation cystitis • Anticoagulant-unmasked lesion |

### Referral urgency (Australian access criteria pattern)

- **Category 1 (≤30 days):** visible haematuria at any age; NVH with a lesion, upper tract abnormality or hydronephrosis on imaging.
- **Category 2 (≤90 days):** persistent NVH with normal imaging.
- Pre-referral work should already include MSU MC&S and upper tract imaging.

## 1.2 Bladder cancer

**The short version**
- >90% urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma; painless visible haematuria is the presentation until proven otherwise.
- Smoking is the dominant risk factor; aromatic amines the classic occupational one; *Schistosoma haematobium* causes **squamous** cell carcinoma.
- TURBT is both diagnostic and therapeutic — it must sample **detrusor muscle** to allow T-staging.
- The pivotal split is **non-muscle-invasive (Ta/T1/CIS)** vs **muscle-invasive (≥T2)**: intravesical therapy + surveillance vs radical treatment.
- Intravesical BCG is for intermediate/high-risk NMIBC (especially CIS and T1 high grade); a single immediate intravesical chemotherapy instillation within 24 h of TURBT reduces recurrence in low-risk disease.

### Risk factors and histology

| Exposure | Histology | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Smoking | Urothelial | ~50% of cases; risk falls but never normalises after cessation |
| Aromatic amines (β-naphthylamine, benzidine) — dye, rubber, leather, painting, aluminium, hairdressing | Urothelial | Latency of decades — take an occupational history |
| Cyclophosphamide / ifosfamide (acrolein) | Urothelial | Mesna reduces haemorrhagic cystitis; malignancy risk persists |
| Pelvic radiotherapy | Urothelial | Also causes radiation cystitis |
| Chronic irritation — long-term IDC, recurrent stones, exstrophy | **Squamous** | Catheter + haematuria in spinal cord injury |
| *Schistosoma haematobium* | **Squamous** | Egypt/sub-Saharan Africa; migrant history |
| Urachal remnant (bladder dome) | **Adenocarcinoma** | Rare, mucinous |
| Aristolochic acid, Lynch syndrome, phenacetin | Urothelial (upper tract) | See 1.4 |

### Presentation

Painless VH (~80%). Irritative LUTS — frequency, urgency, dysuria with sterile pyuria — should raise **CIS**, which is flat, high-grade and prone to progression; recurrent culture-negative "UTIs" in an older smoker is the same trap in different clothing. Advanced: pelvic pain, obstructive uropathy from ureteric orifice involvement, leg oedema, bone pain. Field change is intrinsic — the whole urothelium from renal pelvis to urethra is at risk, hence lifetime surveillance even after superficial disease.

### Diagnosis and staging

Cystoscopy → **TURBT** under anaesthetic, with bimanual EUA before and after resection to assess a palpable mass. Resect to include detrusor: a specimen with no muscle cannot be staged. Blue-light cystoscopy with hexaminolevulinate improves CIS detection; mapping biopsies where cytology is positive but the bladder looks normal. Staging: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with urography for muscle-invasive disease; bone scan if bone pain or raised ALP.

T-stages: **Ta** non-invasive papillary; **Tis** flat CIS; **T1** invades lamina propria; **T2** detrusor muscle; **T3** perivesical fat; **T4** adjacent organs/pelvic sidewall.

### Non-muscle-invasive disease (Ta, T1, CIS)

Roughly 75% of new bladder cancers. Management is stratified by grade, stage, size, multifocality, recurrence rate and presence of CIS.

| Risk group | Typical features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Solitary, primary, Ta low grade, <3 cm, no CIS | • Single immediate intravesical chemo (mitomycin C, gemcitabine or epirubicin) **within 24 h** of TURBT, ideally 2 h • Surveillance cystoscopy |
| Intermediate | Multifocal/recurrent low-grade Ta, or fitting neither low nor high | • BCG induction (6 weekly) + maintenance 3-weekly at 3, 6, 12 months = **1 year** • Or intravesical chemo ≤1 year |
| High | Any T1, any high grade, CIS, multiple adverse features | • **Re-TURBT** (2–6 weeks) for T1, incomplete resection or absent muscle • BCG induction + maintenance **1–3 years** (3-weekly at 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36 months) • Early radical cystectomy if very high risk |

⚑ Immediate instillation is contraindicated with bladder perforation or extensive resection — extravasated mitomycin causes severe chemical peritonitis.

**Intravesical BCG.** Live attenuated *M. bovis*; a non-specific immunostimulant recruiting a local Th1 response that clears residual tumour. Superior to intravesical chemotherapy for preventing recurrence *and* progression in high-risk disease. Adverse effects: irritative LUTS and low-grade fever (common, self-limiting), granulomatous prostatitis, contracted bladder, and rarely **BCGosis** — disseminated infection with sepsis-like illness, hepatitis or pneumonitis, treated with antituberculous therapy. Do not instil after traumatic catheterisation, or with active haematuria or UTI. BCG-unresponsive disease → radical cystectomy.

**Surveillance.** Cystoscopy-based and lifelong, front-loaded: 3 months post-resection for all, then intervals extended by risk; high-risk disease also gets periodic upper tract imaging and cytology. No blood test substitutes for cystoscopy.

### Muscle-invasive disease (≥T2)

- **Neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy** (dose-dense MVAC or gemcitabine–cisplatin) before radical cystectomy — roughly a 5% absolute gain in 5-year OS in pooled trial data. Needs adequate renal function and performance status; carboplatin is **not** an equivalent substitute in the curative setting.
- **Radical cystectomy** + bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy — cystoprostatectomy in men, anterior exenteration in women. Diversion by ileal conduit (commonest), orthotopic neobladder, or continent cutaneous diversion.
- **Trimodal therapy** (maximal TURBT + concurrent chemoradiotherapy) is the bladder-preserving alternative for selected patients — solitary tumour, no hydronephrosis, no extensive CIS, complete resection — and for those unfit for cystectomy.
- Metastatic: platinum-based chemotherapy; checkpoint inhibitors and antibody–drug conjugates (enfortumab vedotin) in combination/later lines — check eviQ.

## 1.3 Renal cell carcinoma

**The short version**
- Arises from proximal tubular epithelium; **clear cell** is the commonest subtype and the one linked to VHL.
- The classic triad (flank pain + haematuria + palpable mass) occurs in <10% and signals advanced disease — over half are now **incidentalomas** on imaging done for something else.
- Paraneoplastic syndromes are heavily examined: polycythaemia (EPO), hypercalcaemia (PTHrP), hypertension (renin), **Stauffer syndrome** (non-metastatic hepatic dysfunction, reversible after nephrectomy).
- Surgery is the only curative modality — **partial nephrectomy** wherever technically feasible; RCC is intrinsically chemo- and radio-resistant.
- **New left-sided varicocele that does not decompress when supine** = left renal vein obstruction → image the kidney.

### Risk and subtypes

Smoking, obesity, hypertension, acquired cystic disease of dialysis; inherited syndromes — von Hippel–Lindau (clear cell, bilateral/multifocal, plus phaeochromocytoma and haemangioblastomas), hereditary papillary RCC (MET), Birt–Hogg–Dubé (chromophobe/oncocytoma, fibrofolliculomas, pneumothorax), tuberous sclerosis (angiomyolipoma).

| Subtype | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear cell | ~70–75% | VHL pathway; most likely to metastasise; most IO/TKI-responsive |
| Papillary | ~10–15% | Type 1 (MET) better prognosis than type 2 |
| Chromophobe | ~5% | Best prognosis; overlaps morphologically with benign oncocytoma |
| Collecting duct / medullary | Rare | Medullary RCC: sickle cell trait, aggressive |

### Presentation and paraneoplastic syndromes

Incidental detection dominates because CT and US are done so freely — that is *why* the classic triad is now rare, and the exam wants that reasoning. Symptomatic: haematuria, flank pain, weight loss, FUO, anaemia (commoner than polycythaemia despite the teaching), new varicocele, IVC syndrome from tumour thrombus.

| Syndrome | Mediator | Clue in a stem |
|---|---|---|
| Polycythaemia | Ectopic erythropoietin | Raised Hb/Hct with a renal mass |
| Hypercalcaemia | PTHrP (or lytic mets) | High Ca²⁺, **suppressed** PTH |
| Hypertension | Renin | New/refractory HTN with renal mass |
| **Stauffer syndrome** | IL-6 / GM-CSF | Deranged LFTs (↑ALP, ↑transaminases, ↑PT) with **no liver metastases**; resolves after nephrectomy |
| AA amyloidosis, anaemia, pyrexia | Inflammatory cytokines | "FUO" workup |

**Left varicocele.** The left gonadal vein drains into the left renal vein; the right drains straight into the IVC. Left renal vein tumour thrombus therefore obstructs left gonadal drainage. A varicocele that is new, right-sided, or fails to decompress with recumbency mandates cross-sectional imaging.

### Staging and management

Diagnosis is radiological: multiphase contrast CT showing an enhancing solid mass (>15–20 HU enhancement). Biopsy is not routine before nephrectomy for a classic mass, but is used before ablation, before systemic therapy, and where lymphoma/metastasis/abscess is a differential.

| T stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| T1a | ≤4 cm, confined to kidney |
| T1b | >4 to ≤7 cm, confined |
| T2a | >7 to ≤10 cm, confined |
| T2b | >10 cm, confined |
| T3a | Renal vein or its segmental branches, pelvicalyceal system, or perinephric/renal sinus fat — within Gerota's fascia |
| T3b | IVC **below** the diaphragm |
| T3c | IVC **above** the diaphragm, or invades IVC wall |
| T4 | Beyond Gerota's fascia, including contiguous ipsilateral adrenal involvement |

Management:
- **Partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing)** preferred for T1 wherever feasible — equivalent oncological outcomes, better renal function, fewer downstream CKD/cardiovascular events. ⚑ A genuine shift from older texts making radical nephrectomy the default.
- **Radical nephrectomy** for large, central or locally advanced tumours. ⚑ The ipsilateral adrenal is spared unless involved or radiologically abnormal; routine adrenalectomy is no longer standard. Lymphadenectomy is not routinely therapeutic.
- **Active surveillance / percutaneous ablation** (RFA, cryotherapy) for small renal masses in elderly or comorbid patients.
- **Metastatic:** cytoreductive nephrectomy in selected patients; systemic therapy is now **checkpoint inhibitor combinations** (dual IO, or IO + VEGF TKI), not the old interferon/IL-2 era. Metastasectomy for oligometastatic disease.
- ⚑ "Chemo- and radio-resistant" holds for conventional cytotoxics (high P-glycoprotein expression) but RCC is *not* treatment-resistant — it is among the most immunotherapy-sensitive solid tumours. Radiotherapy retains a palliative role for bone and brain metastases.

Metastatic pattern: lung (cannonball lesions), bone (**lytic**, expansile, hypervascular — bleed briskly at surgery), liver, brain. Notorious for late relapse, so surveillance runs for years.

## 1.4 Upper tract urothelial carcinoma

**The short version**
- Uncommon (~5–10% of urothelial cancers) but disproportionately examined because of its associations and its surgery.
- Risk: smoking, aromatic amines, **Lynch syndrome** (MSH2 especially), aristolochic acid/Balkan endemic nephropathy, analgesic (phenacetin) nephropathy.
- Presents with visible haematuria, or flank pain from clot colic/obstruction; a filling defect in the collecting system on CT urography.
- Standard treatment is **radical nephroureterectomy with excision of a bladder cuff**; kidney-sparing endoscopic management for low-risk, low-grade, unifocal small tumours, solitary kidney or CKD.
- Lifelong bladder surveillance follows, because ~20–50% develop subsequent bladder tumours (field change plus seeding).

Diagnosis: CT urography (delayed-phase filling defect), selective ureteric cytology/washings, ureteroscopy with biopsy. Suspect Lynch syndrome and refer for germline testing if young, or with personal/family history of colorectal, endometrial, gastric or ovarian cancer. The bladder cuff must be excised — retained distal ureter carries a high recurrence rate. A single post-operative intravesical chemotherapy instillation reduces subsequent bladder recurrence. ⚑ Platinum chemotherapy is best given **neoadjuvantly** in high-risk disease, because post-nephroureterectomy eGFR often falls below the threshold for cisplatin.

## 1.5 Prostate cancer

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Australia does not run a population screening programme**, but the 2026 national guidelines now *recommend offering* two-yearly PSA to men aged 50–69 after shared decision-making — a shift from the more neutral "if the patient requests it after discussion" framing of 2016.
- ⚑ **DRE is no longer recommended as a routine adjunct to PSA in primary care.** It remains part of specialist assessment.
- ⚑ **mpMRI before biopsy** is now standard; biopsy for PI-RADS 4–5, or PI-RADS 3 with PSA density ≥0.15.
- Grading is by **ISUP grade group** (1–5); clinically significant disease = **grade group ≥2**.
- Bone metastases are characteristically **osteoblastic/sclerotic**, spreading via Batson's venous plexus to the axial skeleton.

### PSA testing — the Australian position

| Group | Recommendation | PSA action threshold (repeat in 1–3 months, then refer if confirmed) |
|---|---|---|
| 45–49, average risk | Not routinely offered; test if the man raises it, after discussion | ≥1.0 µg/L |
| 50–69, average risk | **Offer 2-yearly** after shared decision-making | ≥3.0 µg/L |
| 45–49, higher risk | **Offer 2-yearly from age 45** | ≥1.0 µg/L |
| 50–69, higher risk | Offer 2-yearly | ≥2.0 µg/L |
| ≥70 | Individualised; only if life expectancy >7 years and after documented discussion | ≥5.5 µg/L |

"Higher risk" = at least double population risk: first-degree relative with prostate cancer (especially diagnosed young), Black sub-Saharan African ancestry, or pathogenic **BRCA2** variant.

PSA is organ-specific but **not cancer-specific** — it rises with BPH, prostatitis, UTI, retention, recent ejaculation, cycling and instrumentation, and falls roughly by half on 5α-reductase inhibitors (double the measured value to interpret). The harms of testing are overdiagnosis and overtreatment of indolent grade group 1 disease; mpMRI-first pathways and active surveillance exist to blunt exactly that.

### Diagnosis

Confirmed elevated PSA → urology referral → DRE + **mpMRI** reported with PI-RADS. Biopsy is targeted (usually **transperineal** — lower sepsis rate than transrectal) to PI-RADS 4–5, or PI-RADS 3 with PSA density ≥0.15 µg/L/mL. Negative mpMRI with low PSA density permits monitoring rather than immediate biopsy. Most cancers arise in the **peripheral zone** (palpable on DRE); BPH is transition zone.

### Grading and risk stratification

| ISUP grade group | Gleason score |
|---|---|
| 1 | ≤6 (3+3) |
| 2 | 7 (**3+4**) |
| 3 | 7 (**4+3**) |
| 4 | 8 (4+4, 3+5, 5+3) |
| 5 | 9–10 |

⚑ Grade group is now the reporting standard; "Gleason 7" alone is inadequate because 3+4 and 4+3 behave differently.

| Risk (localised) | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Low | ISUP 1 **and** PSA <10 **and** cT1–T2a ⚑ (EAU says cT1–2; the cT2a ceiling is the D'Amico/NCCN version, and it is the one that keeps the intermediate row below coherent) |
| Intermediate | ISUP 2–3, or PSA 10–20, or cT2b–c (favourable vs unfavourable subdivided by grade group and PSA) |
| High | ISUP 4–5, **or** PSA >20, **or** cT3–4/N+ |

Staging beyond DRE/mpMRI: bone scan ± CT for intermediate-unfavourable and high-risk disease. ⚑ PSMA PET/CT is now widely used in Australia for primary staging and biochemical recurrence, with superior sensitivity to conventional imaging.

### Management by risk

| Risk / setting | Options |
|---|---|
| Low risk, ISUP 1 | **Active surveillance** — serial PSA, repeat mpMRI and biopsy; treat on progression. Distinguish from *watchful waiting* (symptom-directed palliation in men unfit for radical treatment) |
| Intermediate risk | Radical prostatectomy ± pelvic node dissection, **or** radical RT (EBRT ± brachytherapy) with short-course ADT if unfavourable |
| High risk / locally advanced | EBRT + **long-course ADT** (2–3 years), or surgery within a multimodal plan; adjuvant/salvage RT for adverse pathology or PSA relapse |
| Metastatic hormone-sensitive | ADT (LHRH agonist/antagonist or orchidectomy) **plus** an ARPI and/or docetaxel — ⚑ ADT alone is no longer adequate first-line |
| Castration-resistant | ARPIs, taxanes, radium-223 (bone-dominant), ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA, PARP inhibitors for BRCA/HRR mutations |

⚑ LHRH **agonists** cause an initial testosterone surge — cover with an antiandrogen or use a GnRH antagonist if there is a risk of tumour flare (impending cord compression, severe bone pain, obstruction).

### Complications of treatment

- **Radical prostatectomy:** ED (nerve-sparing dependent), stress incontinence, anastomotic stricture, loss of ejaculate.
- **Radiotherapy:** radiation proctitis with rectal bleeding, radiation cystitis, urethral stricture, delayed-onset ED, small second-malignancy risk.
- **ADT:** flushing, loss of libido, ED, sarcopenia and weight gain, gynaecomastia, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance, cardiovascular risk, mood/cognitive effects, and **osteoporosis** — baseline and periodic DXA, calcium, vitamin D, exercise and bone-protective therapy.

### Metastatic pattern

Lumbar spine, pelvis, ribs, proximal femora — **osteoblastic** on plain film/CT, intense bone-scan uptake, raised **ALP with normal or near-normal calcium** (unlike the lytic, hypercalcaemic pattern of myeloma or RCC). Spread via Batson's vertebral venous plexus bypasses the caval circulation, explaining axial predominance. Consequences: cord compression, pathological fracture, bone pain.

## 2.1 LUTS — classifying the symptom, then classifying the patient

**The short version**
- LUTS is a symptom complex, not a diagnosis: **storage** (frequency, urgency, nocturia, urge incontinence), **voiding** (hesitancy, weak/intermittent stream, straining, terminal dribble), **post-micturition** (incomplete emptying, post-micturition dribble).
- The split points to different organs: voiding → outflow obstruction (prostate, stricture) or underactive detrusor; storage → the bladder itself (detrusor overactivity, infection, stone, CIS); post-micturition → urethral pooling.
- ⚑ "Prostatism" is obsolete. Men get storage symptoms without prostates and women get identical voiding symptoms. Say LUTS.
- IPSS: 7 items, 0–5 each, total 0–35 — **mild ≤7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35** — plus a separate 0–6 quality-of-life ("bother") item.
- **Nocturia is its own problem with its own differential.** Work through nocturnal polyuria, global polyuria, reduced capacity and primary sleep disorder before blaming the prostate.

### Why the storage/voiding split earns its keep

Obstruction produces voiding symptoms directly but storage symptoms *indirectly*: chronic outflow resistance causes detrusor hypertrophy and secondary overactivity. Hence the common finals trap — a man with BPH whose dominant complaint is urgency and frequency. Treating him purely as "overactive bladder" risks precipitating retention; treating him purely as obstruction may not fix the bother he presented with. Establish whether he empties (PVR) before choosing. Conversely, storage symptoms with normal flow, no residual and a small prostate are probably not prostatic — idiopathic detrusor overactivity, UTI, bladder stone, bladder pain syndrome.

### Separating the causes on history alone

| Pattern | Discriminating features on history |
|---|---|
| **BPH / outflow obstruction** | Gradual onset over years; voiding-predominant (hesitancy, weak stream, straining, terminal dribble, double voiding); nocturia with *normal-sized* voids; no haematuria or weight loss |
| **Overactive bladder** | Storage-predominant; **urgency is the defining symptom** — sudden, difficult to defer; small-volume frequency; urge incontinence; often triggered (key in the door, running water); stream normal |
| **Prostate cancer** | Usually **asymptomatic** when curable — LUTS is a poor screening symptom. Suspicious: new bone pain, weight loss, haematuria/haematospermia, rapid progression, hard/nodular prostate, renal failure from bilateral ureteric involvement |
| **Neurological cause** | Abrupt onset; leg weakness/numbness, saddle sensory change, faecal incontinence, new ED; back pain; known MS, Parkinson disease, diabetes, prior spinal surgery; **reduced sensation of bladder filling** |
| **Polyuria (systemic)** | Large-volume voids day *and* night; thirst; the volume, not the bladder, is abnormal |
| **UTI / stone / CIS** | Rapid onset, dysuria, haematuria, suprapubic pain, sterile pyuria (CIS, TB) |
| **Urethral stricture** | Younger man; prior instrumentation, catheterisation, trauma or urethritis; spraying stream; poor alpha-blocker response |

### Polyuria: the causes to name

- **Diabetes mellitus** — osmotic diuresis; polyuria + polydipsia + weight loss; check BGL/HbA1c.
- **Diuretics** — especially a loop taken in the afternoon; a timing problem masquerading as a bladder problem.
- **Obstructive sleep apnoea** — nocturnal hypoxia and raised intrathoracic pressure raise ANP, causing genuine nocturnal polyuria. Ask about snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence, obesity; screen with STOP-Bang.
- **Hypercalcaemia** — nephrogenic DI; polyuria, constipation, confusion, bone pain, stones.
- **Diabetes insipidus** (cranial or nephrogenic, incl. lithium), **primary polydipsia**, **hypokalaemia**.
- Peripheral oedema of any cause (heart failure, venous insufficiency, dihydropyridines): daytime third-spaced fluid is mobilised when supine.

### ⚑ The exam stem: "an older man waking several times a night to pass urine"

Nocturia alone does not equal BPH; a stem giving *only* nocturia tests whether you generate a differential rather than pattern-match to prostate. Reason in four steps:

1. **Is total 24-h output high?** >40 mL/kg/24 h = global polyuria → diabetes mellitus, DI, primary polydipsia, hypercalcaemia. These men are polyuric by day as well.
2. **Is output normal but nocturnally weighted?** Nocturnal polyuria index = night volume ÷ 24-h volume; **>33% in older adults (>20% in the young)** = nocturnal polyuria → OSA, heart failure, peripheral oedema, evening diuretic, evening fluid/alcohol/caffeine.
3. **Is nocturnal volume normal but split into small voids?** Reduced functional capacity → detrusor overactivity, BPH with significant residual, bladder stone/tumour, chronic UTI.
4. **Is he waking for another reason and voiding opportunistically?** Insomnia, pain, OSA arousals, restless legs — nocturia is a *consequence* here.

The highest-yield investigation is a **3-day frequency–volume (bladder) diary** with fluid intake — it separates all four groups and costs nothing. Add PVR, urinalysis and renal function. Only then does the prostate belong in the answer.

## 2.2 Assessment

**The short version**
- Everyone: abdominal exam (palpable bladder), meatus/genitalia, **DRE**, and a focused neurological exam if any red flag.
- Everyone: **urinalysis** ± MSU, **renal function**, **PVR**, plus a **frequency–volume diary** if storage- or nocturia-predominant.
- PSA only after discussing consequences; ⚑ **defer ~6 weeks after UTI/prostatitis, instrumentation or catheterisation**, and avoid immediately post-ejaculation. Vigorous massage raises it; gentle DRE has minimal effect — conventionally take blood first anyway.
- Image the upper tracts for renal impairment, large residual, haematuria, recurrent UTI, stones or suspected malignancy — not routinely.
- Refer urgently: retention with renal failure, any visible haematuria, suspicious DRE, any neurological red flag.

### Examination and DRE

Palpate for a distended bladder (dull, arising from the pelvis, cannot get below it). Inspect the meatus for stenosis and the foreskin for phimosis. DRE assesses anal tone, faecal loading (a reversible cause of retention) and the prostate.

| DRE finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Smooth, symmetrical, rubbery, enlarged, sulcus preserved | Benign hyperplasia |
| Hard, craggy, nodular, asymmetric, sulcus lost, fixed | Suspicious for carcinoma — refer regardless of PSA |
| Exquisitely tender, boggy, warm, systemically unwell | Acute prostatitis — ⚑ **do not massage** (bacteraemia); treat and defer PSA |
| Absent/reduced anal tone, loss of perianal sensation | Cauda equina/cord lesion until proven otherwise |

⚑ DRE size correlates poorly with symptom severity and only crudely with true volume (it underestimates). Size matters for *drug choice* (5-ARI benefit) more than for diagnosis.

### Investigations

| Test | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Urinalysis ± MSU | Infection, haematuria, glycosuria | Highest yield per dollar; glycosuria reframes the consultation |
| UEC/eGFR | Obstructive nephropathy | Impaired + LUTS → image tracts, consider high-pressure chronic retention |
| PSA | Prostate cancer risk; also predicts BPH progression | See caveats below |
| PVR (bladder scan) | Emptying failure | Measure **within ~10 min of voiding**; repeat before acting on one high value |
| 3-day frequency–volume diary | Polyuria vs nocturnal polyuria vs small capacity | Often diagnostic on its own |
| Uroflowmetry | Objective flow; needs voided volume ≥150 mL | Cannot distinguish obstruction from a weak detrusor |
| Renal tract ultrasound | Hydronephrosis, residual, stones, bladder wall | For renal impairment, large residual, haematuria, recurrent UTI, stones |
| Urodynamics | Obstruction vs detrusor underactivity | Specialist; pre-surgery if ambiguous or neurogenic |
| Cystoscopy ± CT urography | Haematuria, stricture, stone, tumour | All **visible** haematuria warrants urology referral |

**PVR thresholds** — no universal cut-off. Broadly: <100 mL normal, 100–200 mL equivocal (repeat), >200–300 mL clinically significant, persistently >300–400 mL is retention warranting drainage and specialist input. Interpret against voided volume: 150 mL residual after a 200 mL void is far worse than after an 800 mL void.

**Uroflowmetry** — Qmax below roughly 10 mL/s in an adequate void suggests impaired emptying; above ~15 mL/s makes obstruction less likely. A weak detrusor gives an identical trace, so a low Qmax alone should not commit a man to surgery.

**PSA caveats.** PSA rises with prostate volume, age, UTI/prostatitis, retention, catheterisation or cystoscopy, biopsy, recent ejaculation and prolonged cycling; it falls with 5-ARIs. ⚑ **Australian practice (RACGP Red Book 2024): offer the *opportunity to discuss* the benefits and harms — the Red Book does not recommend offering the test itself, and a PSA should not be ordered without informed consent to screening. Where the man consents, 2-yearly from age 50 (40–45 with significant family history) after informed discussion; ≥3.0 µg/L is repeated in 1–3 months and, if confirmed, referred for urological assessment with mpMRI before biopsy.** ⚑ **The 2026 Australian update supersedes this for the paper — see §1.5 for the risk-stratified table (offer from 45 in higher-risk men, threshold ≥1.0 µg/L at 45–49).** It adds risk-stratified thresholds (lower for higher-risk men, higher in men ≥70) and **conditionally recommends against routine DRE as a PSA adjunct in primary care** — DRE stays part of specialist assessment and is still mandatory when symptoms are suspicious.

**Urgent referral triggers:** retention with renal impairment or bilateral hydronephrosis; any visible haematuria; hard/nodular prostate; suspected cauda equina or cord compression; urosepsis; failed trial of void; symptomatic bladder stones.

## 2.3 BPH management

**The short version**
- Mild symptoms, low bother → **watchful waiting plus behavioural change**. A legitimate exam answer, not a cop-out.
- **Alpha-blocker** = fastest relief (days to weeks), independent of prostate size. Tamsulosin is uroselective; prazosin needs titration and causes first-dose hypotension.
- ⚑ **Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome** — ask about planned cataract surgery *before* prescribing tamsulosin, and tell the ophthalmologist if already started. Stopping pre-operatively does not reliably prevent it.
- **5-ARI** shrinks the gland — benefit only in larger prostates (~>30–40 mL), takes **3–6 months**, **halves PSA**, so ⚑ **double the measured PSA after ≥6 months**; any rise on therapy is suspicious.
- Surgery (TURP is the benchmark) for refractory symptoms, or absolute indications: refractory retention, recurrent UTI, recurrent haematuria, bladder stones, obstructive renal impairment.

### Conservative and behavioural

Education and reassurance; fluid moderation with evening restriction; less caffeine and alcohol; timed and double voiding; treat constipation; review drugs (diuretic timing, anticholinergics, opioids, decongestants); bladder training for storage symptoms; weight loss. Many men with mild symptoms stay stable without drugs.

### Pharmacotherapy

| Class | Examples (Australian) | Mechanism | Onset | Key adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha1-blocker, non-selective** | Prazosin (low starting dose, first dose at night, titrate) | Relaxes prostatic and bladder-neck smooth muscle (dynamic component) | Days–2 weeks | • First-dose and postural hypotension, syncope • Dizziness, headache • Titration, multiple daily doses |
| **Alpha1a-selective** | Tamsulosin MR 400 microgram daily; silodosin | As above, prostate-selective | Days–2 weeks | • **IOFS** (a large proportion of tamsulosin users) • Retrograde/absent ejaculation • Dizziness, nasal congestion • Less hypotension than prazosin, but still possible |
| **5-alpha-reductase inhibitor** | Finasteride 5 mg daily (type 2); dutasteride 500 microgram daily (types 1+2) | Blocks testosterone → DHT; gland volume falls ~20–25% | **3–6 months** | • Reduced libido, ED, ejaculate volume • Gynaecomastia • **PSA falls ~50%** • Teratogenic — women of childbearing potential must not handle broken tablets |
| **Combination** | Tamsulosin + dutasteride (fixed-dose, PBS-listed) | Symptom relief plus disease modification | Immediate + delayed | Additive sexual adverse effects; for larger glands with progression risk |
| **Antimuscarinic** | Oxybutynin, solifenacin, darifenacin, tolterodine | Blocks detrusor M3 receptors | 2–4 weeks | • Dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision • **Cognitive impairment/delirium in older men** — oxybutynin worst • ⚑ Add for storage symptoms only if PVR is not elevated |
| **Beta3 agonist** | Mirabegron 25 or 50 mg daily | Detrusor relaxation during storage | 4–8 weeks | • Hypertension — monitor BP, avoid if uncontrolled • Fewer cognitive effects; preferred in the frail elderly |
| **PDE5 inhibitor** | Tadalafil 5 mg daily | Smooth muscle relaxation; treats LUTS and ED together | 2–4 weeks | • Headache, flushing, dyspepsia • **Contraindicated with nitrates**; additive hypotension with alpha-blockers |

**Choosing.** Voiding-predominant, modest gland → alpha-blocker. Large gland (>30–40 mL) or high PSA with progression risk → add a 5-ARI, counselling that benefit takes months. Persistent storage symptoms on an alpha-blocker with a low PVR → add mirabegron or low-dose antimuscarinic, re-check PVR. Concurrent ED → consider tadalafil.

### Surgery

**TURP** remains the reference procedure. Absolute indications: refractory or recurrent retention, obstructive renal impairment, recurrent UTI, recurrent gross haematuria of prostatic origin, bladder calculi. Relative: bothersome symptoms failing medical therapy.

| TURP complication | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Retrograde ejaculation** | Commonest lasting effect, in the majority of men — consent explicitly, especially if fertility matters |
| **TUR syndrome** | ⚑ Dilutional hyponatraemia from absorption of hypotonic **glycine** irrigant through open venous sinuses. Confusion, restlessness, headache, nausea, visual disturbance ("glycine blindness"), bradycardia/hypertension then collapse, seizures. Risk rises with resection >~60 min or large glands. Stop resecting, support, restrict fluid, correct sodium cautiously; hypertonic saline only for severe neurological features. ⚑ **Now uncommon — bipolar TURP and laser techniques use normal saline irrigation**; the classic syndrome is monopolar |
| Bleeding/clot retention | Transfusion or return to theatre; continuous bladder irrigation post-op |
| Infection/urosepsis | Prophylaxis per local protocol |
| Stricture, bladder neck contracture | Late; poor stream returning after initial improvement |
| Incontinence; ED | Uncommon (sphincter injury); ED less predictable but still consent |
| Re-treatment | A proportion need repeat surgery within ~10 years |

**Alternatives.** TUIP for small glands; **HoLEP**/anatomical enucleation for very large glands (size-independent, low re-treatment); photoselective vaporisation for anticoagulated men; open or robotic simple prostatectomy. ⚑ **Ejaculation-preserving minimally invasive options** — prostatic urethral lift (UroLift), water vapour thermal therapy (Rezūm), Aquablation, prostatic artery embolisation — are increasingly available in Australia; name these when a stem stresses a younger man wanting antegrade ejaculation preserved. Durability data are shorter than for TURP.

## 2.4 Acute urinary retention

**The short version**
- Sudden, **painful** inability to void with a palpable tender bladder. Painless distension points to **chronic** retention or a **neurological** lesion.
- Causes: BPH (commonest in men), constipation, UTI/prostatitis, clot, stricture, post-operative, and **drugs** — anticholinergics, opioids, alpha-agonist decongestants, TCAs, antipsychotics, antihistamines.
- Manage: immediate catheterisation with **documented drained volume**, treat the precipitant, check UEC, start an alpha-blocker, plan a trial of void.
- ⚑ **Rapid complete drainage is appropriate** — routine clamping to decompress slowly is no longer recommended; haematuria ex vacuo and hypotension are uncommon and self-limiting.
- Watch for **post-obstructive diuresis** after large-volume drainage.

### Clinical picture and causes

Acute retention is agonising: restless patient, palpable tender bladder, instant relief on drainage. Precipitants worth naming: recent surgery (especially spinal/pelvic anaesthesia), a new opioid or anticholinergic, an OTC decongestant, alcohol binge, constipation, immobility, UTI or prostatitis, sacral zoster.

**Painless retention** should change your answer. It means either (a) chronic retention, where the bladder has slowly accommodated large volumes, or (b) an afferent neurological lesion so distension is not felt — cauda equina, cord compression, MS, diabetic cystopathy. Painless retention plus back pain, saddle numbness or leg weakness is cauda equina until MRI says otherwise.

### Immediate management

1. Analgesia; confirm distension clinically ± bladder scan.
2. **Catheterise** (urethral; suprapubic if urethral access fails or is contraindicated) and **record the drained volume** — that number drives everything else.
3. Urinalysis/MSU, UEC; defer PSA (retention itself raises it).
4. Treat the precipitant: relieve constipation, stop the offending drug, treat infection.
5. Start an **alpha-blocker** at least 24–48 h before catheter removal — it improves trial-of-void success.
6. Renal tract ultrasound if renal function is impaired, the drained volume was large, or chronic retention is suspected.

### Post-obstructive diuresis (POD)

Defined as urine output **≥200 mL/h for two consecutive hours**, or **>3 L in 24 h**, after relief of obstruction. Mechanism: accumulated urea/sodium osmotic load plus acquired tubular resistance to ADH and aldosterone. Risk rises with drained volumes over roughly 1–1.5 L, prolonged obstruction, older age and renal impairment.

Management: admit rather than discharge; hourly urine output, daily weights, orthostatic BP; electrolytes (Na, K, Mg, PO4) and creatinine **4–6 hourly while the output is brisk**, relaxing to 12-hourly as it settles. Most POD is *physiological* and self-limits within 24 h — over-replacing fluid perpetuates it. When replacement is needed give **roughly 50–75% of the preceding period's losses** as crystalloid, allowing a deliberate mild negative balance, and encourage oral intake. Diuresis beyond ~48 h with dilute urine and high urinary sodium is pathological — involve renal.

### Trial of void (TWOC) and chronic retention

Catheter removal is usually attempted after a few days on an alpha-blocker; success is likelier with smaller drained volumes, younger age and a reversible precipitant. Failure means re-catheterisation and urological referral (surgery, or long-term catheter/clean intermittent self-catheterisation).

⚑ **High-pressure chronic retention** is the entity not to miss: painless, large-volume (often >800–1000 mL) retention with **bilateral hydronephrosis and renal impairment**, classically with **nocturnal enuresis** in an older man ("the man who wets the bed"). Intravesical pressure stays high throughout the cycle and is transmitted to the upper tracts. Catheterise, monitor closely for POD (high risk), correct electrolytes, refer — renal function usually improves substantially after drainage. Low-pressure chronic retention has a compliant bladder, normal upper tracts and normal creatinine, and is far less urgent.

## 2.5 Urinary incontinence

**The short version**
- Four types: **stress** (leak on cough/exertion), **urge** (leak preceded by urgency), **overflow** (dribbling with high PVR), **functional** (bladder works; mobility/cognition/access do not). Mixed is common.
- Assess all: history and diary, urinalysis, **PVR**, cough stress test, pelvic/DRE examination, medication review.
- **Supervised pelvic floor muscle training for ≥3 months is first-line for stress incontinence** in women and post-prostatectomy in men.
- Urge: bladder training, then antimuscarinic or mirabegron, then botulinum toxin or sacral neuromodulation.
- ⚑ Exclude overflow before starting an antimuscarinic — it will make it worse.

**Stress** — sphincter/pelvic floor weakness. Risk factors: parity and vaginal delivery, obesity, chronic cough, menopause; in men, radical prostatectomy. Management: weight loss, treat cough/constipation, supervised pelvic floor training, continence pessary; topical vaginal oestrogen for atrophy. Surgery: **synthetic mid-urethral sling**, autologous fascial sling, colposuspension, urethral bulking; male sling or artificial urinary sphincter post-prostatectomy. ⚑ **Australian divergence:** after the TGA's 2017 review, transvaginal mesh for *prolapse* and mini-slings were cancelled from the ARTG, while **mid-urethral slings for stress incontinence remain available** under tightened conditions, mandated informed consent and credentialling — expect a counselling stem, not "mesh is banned". Duloxetine is not TGA-approved for this indication.

**Urge** — detrusor overactivity. Bladder training, less caffeine, timed voiding, weight loss; then an antimuscarinic (solifenacin, darifenacin — more M3-selective, less cognitively burdensome than oxybutynin) or **mirabegron**, preferred in older patients. Third-line: intradetrusor botulinum toxin (repeat ~6–9 monthly; counsel about needing self-catheterisation), sacral neuromodulation, posterior tibial nerve stimulation.

**Overflow** — chronic retention from obstruction or an underactive detrusor (diabetes, drugs, neurological disease). Relieve obstruction and drain, usually with **clean intermittent self-catheterisation**; never an antimuscarinic.

**Functional** — the answer for a cognitively impaired or immobile inpatient. Fix the environment: bedside commode, accessible toilet, lighting, prompted voiding, clothing, review sedatives and diuretics. Indwelling catheters are not a treatment for incontinence.

## 2.6 Neurogenic bladder — and the must-not-miss

**The short version**
- Lesion site predicts bladder behaviour. **Suprapontine** (stroke, Parkinson, dementia): overactive detrusor, coordinated sphincter → urgency, urge incontinence. **Suprasacral cord** (trauma, MS): overactive detrusor **with detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia** → high pressures, hydronephrosis, renal failure. **Sacral/infrasacral** (cauda equina, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, pelvic surgery): acontractile bladder → retention and overflow.
- Suprasacral lesions threaten the kidneys — surveillance plus pressure-lowering management (antimuscarinic/botulinum toxin with CISC).
- **Cauda equina and cord compression are the emergencies.** Retention with back pain is a neurosurgical problem until proven otherwise.

**Cauda equina red flags:** new bilateral or progressive leg neurology, saddle/perineal sensory loss, painless retention or loss of the sensation of bladder filling, urinary or faecal incontinence, reduced anal tone, new erectile dysfunction, severe back pain. ⚑ Retention is a *late* sign — waiting for it before imaging is the classic error, and a normal PVR does not exclude early cauda equina. Action: immediate senior review, urgent **whole-spine MRI**, neurosurgical/spinal referral. If metastatic cord compression, add high-dose corticosteroid per protocol and involve radiation oncology.

Cord injury also produces **spinal shock** acutely: an areflexic acontractile bladder with retention for weeks to months before detrusor overactivity emerges — catheterise, assess urodynamically later. **Autonomic dysreflexia** (severe hypertension, headache, flushing above and pallor below a lesion at or above T6) is usually triggered by a blocked catheter or distended bladder or rectum; sit the patient up and relieve the trigger.

## 3.1 The acute scrotum

**The short version**

- Acute scrotal pain in a boy or young man is torsion until a surgeon says otherwise. The default disposition is theatre, not imaging.
- Age triages: neonate and 12–18 y → torsion; 7–12 y → torted appendage; sexually active adult → epididymo-orchitis; diabetic older man, pain out of proportion → Fournier's.
- Onset tells you the pathology: instantaneous or waking-from-sleep pain = vascular event; building over days with dysuria and fever = infection.
- Absent cremasteric reflex + high-riding, transversely lying testis is the strongest bedside combination for torsion. Its presence does *not* exclude torsion.
- ⚑ Prehn's sign (relief on elevation → epididymitis) is a historical curiosity with poor accuracy. Do not let it change management; naming it as the deciding factor in an exam answer is a trap.

| Onset | Sudden, seconds to minutes; often wakes from sleep | Subacute, hours to 1–2 days | Gradual over days | Sudden if previously reducible lump becomes irreducible | Gradual over hours | Rapid — hours; pain precedes visible signs |

- *Nausea and vomiting* with scrotal pain shifts probability strongly towards torsion — visceral afferent activation, not gastroenteritis.
- Torsion may present as **abdominal or groin pain with no scrotal complaint**, particularly in an embarrassed adolescent. Every boy with acute lower abdominal pain gets his scrotum examined; missing this is the classic medicolegal case.
- Idiopathic scrotal oedema: a well, afebrile child with an inflamed *scrotal wall* but normal, non-tender testes.
- ⚑ TWIST score (hard testis 2, swelling 2, nausea/vomiting 1, absent cremasteric reflex 1, high-riding testis 1) stratifies risk in some Australian EDs. It is a rule-in tool, not a rule-out, and does not replace surgical judgement.

## 3.2 Testicular torsion

**The short version**

- **Clinical diagnosis. Straight to theatre. Ultrasound must not delay exploration** — if suspicion is high, imaging is an unnecessary and dangerous detour.
- Salvage is time-dependent: near-universal within ~6 h of onset, falling substantially by 12–24 h, and rare beyond 24 h.
- The anatomical substrate is the **bell-clapper deformity** — failure of tunica vaginalis fixation to the posterior testis, allowing the testis to hang and rotate freely within it. It is frequently bilateral.
- A **normal Doppler does not exclude torsion** (intermittent, partial, or early torsion with preserved arterial signal). Do not use a reassuring scan to overrule a convincing history.
- Fix **both** testes at operation — bilateral orchidopexy, because the predisposing deformity is bilateral in a large proportion.

**Mechanism and types**

*Intravaginal* torsion is the adolescent/adult disease and rests on the bell-clapper deformity. *Extravaginal* torsion is perinatal — cord and tunica twist above the tunica attachment because dartos fixation has not yet occurred; it presents as a firm, non-tender, discoloured hemiscrotum at birth and is usually already non-viable.

Ischaemia is progressive: venous outflow obstructs first (congestion, swelling), then arterial inflow fails. Hence an early scan may still show arterial flow in a testis already in trouble, and the degree of twist (180°–720°+) determines speed of infarction as much as elapsed time does.

**Intermittent torsion.** Recurrent, self-limiting episodes of severe unilateral pain lasting minutes to hours, often with a horizontal lie between episodes. Not benign — it marks a testis at high risk of a completed, unwitnessed torsion. **Refer for elective bilateral orchidopexy.**

**Management sequence**

1. Nil by mouth, analgesia (opioid), IV access, bloods, consent.
2. Urgent urology/surgical referral — the clock starts at symptom onset, not at presentation, so quantify it.
3. Manual detorsion may be attempted while awaiting theatre — classically rotating the testis medial-to-lateral, "opening a book" — but it is a temporising manoeuvre only and does not substitute for exploration, since residual twist is common.
4. Scrotal exploration: assess viability after detorsion and warm packs. Viable → **bilateral orchidopexy** (three-point fixation). Non-viable → orchidectomy plus contralateral orchidopexy. Offer a prosthesis at a later date.
5. ⚑ Fix the contralateral side even when the affected testis was salvaged — the deformity, not the event, is what recurs.

**Working the past-paper stem: a 20-year-old with sudden severe testicular pain and swelling**

Make each inference explicit; this is what earns marks in a short-answer paper.

- *Age 20* — squarely in the peak incidence window for intravaginal torsion. Epididymo-orchitis is possible but the prior probability of a surgical emergency here is what dictates action.
- *Sudden onset* — vascular occlusive events start instantaneously; infection does not. A stem that says "sudden" or "woke him from sleep" is signalling torsion.
- *Severe pain with swelling* — consistent with venous congestion of an ischaemic testis.
- *What to say next*: examine for a high-riding, transversely lying, diffusely tender testis with an absent cremasteric reflex; ask about prior self-limiting episodes (intermittent torsion), trauma, and urethral discharge/dysuria (which would raise epididymo-orchitis but does not exclude torsion).
- *What to do*: this is a clinical diagnosis — nil by mouth, analgesia, and **immediate surgical exploration**. Do **not** order an ultrasound if it will delay theatre, and do **not** be reassured by preserved Doppler flow. Give the reason: irreversible ischaemic injury accrues from symptom onset, and salvage falls steeply after ~6 h.
- *Answer to "definitive management"*: scrotal exploration with detorsion and **bilateral orchidopexy** (orchidectomy if non-viable, with contralateral fixation regardless).
- Imaging earns its place only when the presentation is genuinely atypical or delayed and the surgeon judges torsion unlikely — never as a routine step to "confirm" a clinically obvious case.

## 3.3 Epididymo-orchitis

**The short version**

- ⚑ Organism follows **sexual history, not age**: in anyone sexually active — of **any** age — *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* remain the most likely cause (Australian STI Management Guidelines). **Coliforms** (*E. coli*, *Proteus*) are suggested by **insertive anal sex, recent instrumentation or catheterisation, or outflow obstruction** — the old "over 35 = coliforms" split is wrong and misses chlamydia in a sexually active 40-year-old.
- Insertive anal intercourse at any age shifts the likely organism to enteric Gram-negatives — ask the question rather than assuming from age alone.
- Australian first-line for the sexually acquired picture: **ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat (in 1% lignocaine) plus doxycycline 100 mg PO BD for 14 days**.
- Enteric/urinary picture: a 14-day course of an agent with Gram-negative cover — e.g. **trimethoprim or cefalexin**, guided by urine culture; MSHC lists cefalexin 500 mg BD or amoxicillin+clavulanate 875/125 mg BD for 14 days.
- STI-related cases require **contact tracing of sexual partners from the preceding 6 months**; urinary-source cases do not.

**Assessment**

Gradual unilateral scrotal pain and swelling, maximal in the epididymis early, with fever, dysuria, discharge or LUTS. Scrotum warm and erythematous, testis lies normally, cremasteric reflex preserved. Investigations: first-pass urine NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhoea; urethral swab for gonococcal culture and sensitivities **before** antibiotics if discharge is present; MSU M/C/S; full STI screen including HIV and syphilis serology.

| Group | Likely organisms | Australian regimen | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sexually active, any age** | *C. trachomatis*, *N. gonorrhoeae*, *M. genitalium* | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat **+** doxycycline 100 mg PO BD × 14 d | • Azithromycin 1 g PO stat repeated at 1 week is the alternative where doxycycline is unsuitable<br>• Notifiable; contact tracing 6 months |
| **Insertive anal sex, instrumentation/catheter, or obstruction** (⚑ not age alone) | *E. coli*, *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas* | 14 d of Gram-negative cover — **cefalexin 500 mg BD** (⚑ trimethoprim is no longer a good empirical choice at ~20% *E. coli* resistance); amoxicillin+clavulanate 875/125 mg BD as an alternative | • Tailor to MSU sensitivities<br>• Investigate the underlying outflow problem |
| Insertive anal sex | Enteric organisms *and* STIs | Cover both — ceftriaxone + doxycycline, review with cultures | ⚑ Age alone misleads here |
| Post-vasectomy / amiodarone / vasculitis | Sterile inflammation | Supportive; consider drug cause | Amiodarone-induced epididymitis is dose-related and resolves on reduction |

**Adjuncts.** Rest, scrotal elevation and support, NSAIDs, abstinence until patient and partners complete treatment. ⚑ Fluoroquinolones appear in older texts as routine first line; in current Australian practice they are reserved, given resistance and tendon/neurological adverse effects.

**When to image.** Not needed in a straightforward case. Image if torsion remains possible (a surgical, not radiological, decision); symptoms fail to settle within 72 h of appropriate antibiotics; a mass is palpable or persists after treatment (**an underlying tumour can masquerade as epididymo-orchitis**); or abscess/pyocele is suspected.

**Mumps orchitis.** Post-pubertal viral orchitis, typically 4–8 days after parotitis but possible without it. Affects a substantial minority of post-pubertal cases, bilateral in a minority of those, causing atrophy in some; complete sterility is uncommon even after bilateral disease. Supportive management only — antibiotics are useless. Notifiable; the exam point is **MMR vaccination**, since cases cluster in the unvaccinated.

## 3.4 Fournier's gangrene

**The short version**

- Necrotising fasciitis of the perineum, genitalia and perianal region — polymicrobial, synergistic, and rapidly fatal.
- **Pain grossly out of proportion to visible findings** is the earliest and most important sign. Crepitus, dusky skin, bullae, malodour and skin anaesthesia are late.
- Surgical emergency: resuscitation + broad-spectrum IV antibiotics + **urgent, aggressive debridement**, with planned re-look and repeat debridement.
- Antibiotics never substitute for the knife. Delay to theatre is the strongest modifiable determinant of death.
- Mortality remains high (roughly 20–40% in contemporary series) despite modern care.

**Risk factors.** Diabetes (commonest), obesity, alcohol misuse, immunosuppression including HIV and chemotherapy, chronic kidney or liver disease, malignancy, older age. Portals of entry: perianal/ischiorectal abscess and colorectal disease, urethral stricture or urinary extravasation, recent instrumentation, genital trauma, pressure injury. ⚑ SGLT2 inhibitors carry a rare labelled association with Fournier's — worth knowing given how widely they are now prescribed in Australia.

**Recognition and management.** Infection tracks along fascial planes far beyond the visible skin change, so cutaneous signs consistently understate the extent. Systemic toxicity — tachycardia, hypotension, fever, delirium, hyperglycaemia — is common. CT showing fascial gas or fluid may support the diagnosis but **must not delay theatre**.

Management: resuscitate, correct glycaemia, blood cultures; broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Gram-positives, Gram-negatives and anaerobes per Therapeutic Guidelines (typically meropenem or piperacillin–tazobactam **plus clindamycin** for exotoxin suppression, plus vancomycin if MRSA is a concern); urgent radical debridement to healthy bleeding tissue; ICU support; planned second look at 24–48 h. Faecal or urinary diversion may be needed; reconstruction follows source control.

## 3.5 Testicular cancer

**The short version**

- The commonest solid malignancy of young men. In Australia around 1,000 new cases a year, peak incidence in the **30–34 year** age band, and **5-year relative survival about 98%** — this is a curable cancer, which is exactly why delay is unforgivable.
- Risk factors: **cryptorchidism (raising the risk in the undescended testis *and* in the normally descended contralateral testis)**, prior testicular germ cell tumour, first-degree family history, GCNIS on biopsy, testicular atrophy/infertility. ⚑ Orchidopexy reduces but does not abolish the risk.
- **A firm, non-tender intratesticular mass is cancer until proven otherwise.** Ultrasound is the first-line test.
- ⚑ **Do not biopsy transscrotally.** Diagnosis and treatment are by **radical inguinal orchidectomy** with high cord ligation — a scrotal approach risks local recurrence and aberrant lymphatic spread to inguinal nodes.
- **AFP is never raised in pure seminoma** — an elevated AFP means non-seminomatous elements regardless of what the histology report says. β-hCG may be raised in seminoma; LDH reflects tumour bulk.
- **Sperm banking is offered BEFORE orchidectomy or any chemo/radiotherapy.**

**Histology**

| | Seminoma | Non-seminomatous GCT (NSGCT) |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Later peak (~35–45 y) | Earlier peak (~20–35 y) |
| Subtypes | Classic, spermatocytic | • Embryonal carcinoma<br>• Yolk sac tumour<br>• Choriocarcinoma<br>• Teratoma<br>• Mixed (commonest) |
| AFP | **Never raised** (if raised → treat as NSGCT) | Raised in yolk sac and embryonal components |
| β-hCG | Raised in up to ~30% | Raised, markedly so in choriocarcinoma |
| Behaviour | Slower, orderly nodal spread; **radiosensitive** | Earlier haematogenous spread; **chemosensitive**, not radiosensitive |

Non-germ-cell: Leydig cell (may secrete androgens/oestrogens → gynaecomastia, precocious puberty), Sertoli cell, and **lymphoma** — commonest testicular malignancy in men over 60, more often bilateral.

**Tumour markers**

- **AFP** — half-life ~5–7 days. Raised by yolk sac and embryonal elements; also by hepatocellular disease. Never by pure seminoma.
- **β-hCG** — half-life ~24–36 hours. Highest in choriocarcinoma; modestly raised in some seminomas (syncytiotrophoblastic cells). Very high levels cause gynaecomastia and thyrotoxicosis via TSH-receptor cross-reactivity.
- **LDH** — non-specific; correlates with tumour burden, used prognostically.
- Measure **before and after** orchidectomy. Markers failing to fall along their half-life, or plateauing/rising, indicate residual or metastatic disease. Normal markers do not exclude cancer — a large minority are marker-negative.

**Diagnosis and staging**

Scrotal ultrasound → markers → **radical inguinal orchidectomy** for histology and local control → **CT chest, abdomen and pelvis** for staging. Brain imaging if very high hCG, multiple lung metastases, or poor-prognosis disease. Consider contralateral biopsy for GCNIS in high-risk men (small testicular volume, cryptorchidism); not routine in men over 40 without risk factors.

**Lymphatic drainage — why it matters.** The testis descends from the retroperitoneum dragging its vessels and lymphatics with it, so it drains to the **para-aortic (retroperitoneal) nodes at the renal vessels — not inguinal nodes**. The *scrotal skin* drains to inguinal nodes. Two consequences: staging CT must cover the retroperitoneum, and palpable inguinal nodes are not the expected finding; and a transscrotal biopsy breaches the barrier between these territories, seeding inguinal nodes and altering both spread pattern and adjuvant treatment fields. That is the reason for the inguinal approach.

**Treatment principles.** Radical inguinal orchidectomy underpins all stages. Stage I seminoma: surveillance preferred (adjuvant carboplatin or radiotherapy as alternatives). Stage I NSGCT: surveillance, or risk-adapted BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) where lymphovascular invasion is present. Metastatic: BEP by IGCCCG risk group, with retroperitoneal lymph node dissection for residual NSGCT masses. Cure exceeds 95% in early disease and remains good with metastases. ⚑ Australian protocols are published through **eviQ**.

**Late effects** (increasingly examinable, since most patients are cured and live decades): hypogonadism and infertility; second malignancies from radiotherapy and etoposide; cisplatin ototoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, nephrotoxicity and Raynaud's; **bleomycin pulmonary fibrosis** (lifelong caution with high-inspired oxygen); cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome.

⚑ **Screening:** Cancer Council Australia does not recommend population screening or routine testicular self-examination, given the low incidence and excellent prognosis. The message is prompt presentation with a new lump, not ritual self-screening.

## 3.6 Painless scrotal swellings

**The short version**

- Two questions sort most of these: **can you get above it?** (no → inguinoscrotal hernia or a hydrocele extending into the cord) and **does it transilluminate?** (yes → fluid).
- Anything **intratesticular and solid is cancer until proven otherwise**; extratesticular and cystic is usually benign.
- ⚑ A hydrocele in a young man may be **secondary** — scan the testis to exclude an underlying tumour before calling it primary.
- ⚑ A **new right-sided varicocele, or one that does not decompress on lying flat**, mandates abdominal imaging for a retroperitoneal mass or renal tumour.
- Ultrasound is the investigation for essentially all of these.

| Lesion | Feel / signs | Transillumination | Get above it? | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrocele | Smooth, fluctuant, testis impalpable within | Yes | Yes (unless cord hydrocele) | US in young men to exclude tumour; surgery for symptoms |
| Varicocele | "Bag of worms", worse standing/Valsalva, decompresses supine | No | Yes | Left common and benign; **right-sided/new/non-decompressing → CT abdomen** |
| Epididymal cyst / spermatocele | Cystic nodule **separate from and above** the testis | Yes | Yes | Reassure; excise only if symptomatic |
| Inguinoscrotal hernia | Cough impulse, reducible, bowel sounds | No | **No** | Surgical repair |
| Tumour | Firm, craggy, **intratesticular**, non-tender | No | Yes | Urgent US + markers + urology |

**Hydrocele.** Fluid between the layers of the tunica vaginalis. In infants usually communicating (patent processus vaginalis); most resolve by 12–24 months. In adults usually idiopathic, but **secondary** hydrocele accompanies tumour, torsion, infection and trauma — hence imaging the young man rather than reassuring him. Repair by Jaboulay or Lord's procedure; aspiration alone recurs.

**Varicocele.** Dilated pampiniform plexus veins. **Left-sided predominance (~90%)** is anatomical: the left testicular vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle (higher pressure, longer column, valve incompetence), while the right drains obliquely into the IVC; "nutcracker" compression of the left renal vein between aorta and SMA contributes. Dull dragging ache, sometimes ipsilateral atrophy and impaired spermatogenesis — the commonest correctable cause of male infertility. Repair (embolisation or ligation) for pain, adolescent growth arrest, or abnormal semen parameters in an infertile couple. ⚑ Red flag: **right-sided, sudden onset, or failure to empty when supine** — suggests venous obstruction by a retroperitoneal or renal mass; image the abdomen.

**Epididymal cyst / spermatocele.** Extratesticular, transilluminate, at the epididymal head and palpably separate from the testis; a spermatocele contains spermatozoa (opalescent aspirate). Benign; excision risks epididymal obstruction, so avoid in men wanting fertility.

## 3.7 Male infertility and erectile dysfunction

**The short version**

- Infertility work-up is of the **couple**: two semen analyses ≥2–3 weeks apart, morning testosterone with LH/FSH, and a scrotal examination looking for varicocele, absent vas or small testes.
- High FSH with small testes = primary testicular failure. Low/normal FSH with azoospermia = obstruction (consider CBAVD and cystic fibrosis testing).
- **ED is a marker of cardiovascular risk** — endothelial dysfunction in the small penile arteries typically precedes coronary events by a few years. Every man with new ED gets a cardiovascular risk assessment.
- ⚑ **PDE5 inhibitors are absolutely contraindicated with nitrates** — the combination causes profound hypotension. Standard separation is ≥24 h for sildenafil/vardenafil and ≥48 h for tadalafil.
- Reversible contributors: drugs (β-blockers, thiazides, SSRIs, antiandrogens), smoking, alcohol, diabetes, hypogonadism, depression, pelvic surgery/radiotherapy.

Retained early-morning erections and a situational pattern point to psychogenic ED; gradual global loss suggests organic disease. Check HbA1c, lipids, morning testosterone, and prolactin if testosterone is low. First line: risk-factor modification plus a PDE5 inhibitor; second line intracavernosal alprostadil or vacuum device; third line penile prosthesis. Warn about priapism (>4 h is an emergency requiring aspiration). In infertility, treat the treatable — varicocele, hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism, surgically correctable obstruction — and otherwise refer for ICSI with surgical sperm retrieval.

# Urology Notes — Section 4: Stones, Urinary Infection and Obstruction
*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic), incl. the March 2025 UTI revision; EAU Urolithiasis and Urological Infections; RACGP/AJGP; WA KEMH obstetric UTI guideline; Vic Health UTI pharmacist protocol. Scope: urolithiasis, urinary infection, prostatitis and obstructive uropathy in adults, written-finals depth, Australian practice.*

## 4.1 Urolithiasis — types, risk and presentation

**The short version**
- Calcium stones (oxalate ± phosphate) ≈80% of all stones; uric acid ~5–10%; struvite ~5–10%; cystine ~1%.
- **Uric acid is radiolucent on plain KUB; cystine is poorly radiopaque ("ground-glass"), not truly radiolucent.** The genuinely radiolucent group is uric acid, ammonium urate, xanthine, 2,8-dihydroxyadenine and drug stones — but almost all, including uric acid, are visible on non-contrast CT — ⚑ **indinavir stones are the exception and are radiolucent even on CT**. "Radiolucent" is an X-ray statement, not a CT one.
- Struvite = infection stone, formed only when a **urease-producing** organism alkalinises the urine: *Proteus mirabilis* (classic), *Klebsiella*, *Providencia*, *Morganella*, *Serratia*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*, *Ureaplasma urealyticum*. **Not** *E. coli* — it does not make urease.
- Pain is colicky loin-to-groin because the ureter and the testis/labium share T11–L2 afferents (embryological co-origin); the referred site migrates caudally as the stone descends.
- Three points of narrowing: **pelviureteric junction (PUJ) → crossing of the iliac vessels at the pelvic brim → vesicoureteric junction (VUJ)**. The VUJ is narrowest and the commonest lodging point.

**Composition and drivers**

| Stone | Share | Radiology | Typical driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate | ~70–75% | Radio-opaque, very dense | • Hypercalciuria (idiopathic, commonest) • Hyperoxaluria • Hypocitraturia • Low urine volume |
| Calcium phosphate | ~5–10% | Radio-opaque | • Alkaline urine • Distal (type 1) RTA • Primary hyperparathyroidism |
| Uric acid | ~5–10% | **Radiolucent** | • Persistently acid urine (pH <5.5) • Gout, high purine intake • Metabolic syndrome/T2DM (impaired ammoniagenesis) • Tumour lysis, chronic diarrhoea/ileostomy |
| Struvite (Mg-ammonium-phosphate, "triple phosphate") | ~5–10% | Radio-opaque, often **staghorn** | Chronic infection with urease producer; alkaline urine pH >7 |
| Cystine | ~1% | **Radiolucent** on plain film ("faint/ground-glass" at best) | Autosomal recessive cystinuria; young, recurrent, family history |

**Risk factors.** Low fluid intake and hot occupational exposure (the dominant Australian risk); high sodium, high animal protein, low dietary calcium; obesity, T2DM, gout; primary hyperparathyroidism; distal RTA; sarcoidosis; IBD, coeliac disease and bariatric surgery (**enteric hyperoxaluria** — unabsorbed fat binds gut calcium, freeing oxalate for absorption); medullary sponge kidney; horseshoe kidney and PUJ obstruction (stasis); drugs — topiramate, acetazolamide, indinavir, ceftriaxone, excess vitamin C.

**Presentation.** Sudden severe unilateral flank pain, waxing/waning, radiating to groin/testis/labium, patient writhing (contrast peritonitis, where the patient lies rigid). Nausea/vomiting near-universal. Haematuria ~85% — **its absence does not exclude a stone**. Distal/VUJ stones cause urgency, frequency and dysuria, mimicking cystitis. Fever is *not* part of uncomplicated colic — see 4.3.

⚑ In anyone >50 with new "renal colic", especially with hypotension or a pulsatile mass, **exclude a leaking AAA** before anchoring on stone — a recurring exam and coronial theme.

## 4.2 Imaging and acute management

**The short version**
- **Non-contrast CT KUB is the investigation of choice** — sens ~95%, spec ~95%; low-dose protocols preserve accuracy at much lower dose.
- Ultrasound (± KUB film) is preferred first in **pregnancy, children, and young women** and where repeated imaging is anticipated — it detects hydronephrosis, not mid-ureteric stones.
- **NSAIDs are first-line analgesia, superior to opioids** for renal colic (Level 1 evidence). Opioids are the add-on, not the starter.
- Tamsulosin (medical expulsive therapy) is worth offering for **distal ureteric stones 5–10 mm**; benefit for stones <5 mm is negligible.
- Stone size predicts passage: <5 mm mostly pass; ≥10 mm rarely do.

**Imaging pathway.** CT KUB, ideally low-dose, within 24 h. It confirms the stone, gives size, position, Hounsfield density (predicts ESWL success) and degree of obstruction, and picks up alternatives (AAA, appendicitis, ovarian pathology). ⚑ IVP/IVU is obsolete. Plain KUB alone is only useful for follow-up of a stone already known to be opaque. Ultrasound first in pregnancy (note physiological right hydronephrosis of pregnancy is a confounder), in children, and reasonably in young non-pregnant women where gynaecological causes compete; if US is non-diagnostic, low-dose CT or MR urography can follow.

**Analgesia — the high-yield point.** NSAID unless contraindicated: parenteral or rectal routes act fastest, and rectal indomethacin/diclofenac is common Australian ED practice; oral ibuprofen or diclofenac suffices for milder pain. Add paracetamol (opioid-sparing). Reserve opioids (titrated IV fentanyl/morphine) for NSAID contraindication or breakthrough. ⚑ Avoid NSAIDs in AKI, significant CKD, hypovolaemia, active peptic ulcer; lowest dose/shortest course in cardiovascular disease. Add an antiemetic (metoclopramide or ondansetron) and rehydrate to euvolaemia — but **forced high-volume IV fluids do not flush stones out**.

**Medical expulsive therapy.** Tamsulosin 400 microgram daily relaxes distal ureteric smooth muscle. Large pragmatic RCTs (SUSPEND) found no overall benefit, but meta-analysis restricted to **distal stones 5–10 mm** shows faster expulsion with less analgesic use. EAU position: offer as one option for distal stones 5–10 mm; ⚑ **off-label in Australia** — warn about postural hypotension and intraoperative floppy iris if cataract surgery is planned.

**Passage vs intervention**

| Stone size | Spontaneous passage | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| <5 mm | High (~70–90%) | Trial of passage, oral analgesia, strain urine, review 2–4 weeks |
| 5–10 mm | Intermediate (~50%) | Trial of passage + MET if distal; image at 4 weeks |
| >10 mm | Low | Plan definitive intervention |

**Indications for urgent intervention (decompression by ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy):**
- **Infection above an obstruction** — see 4.3
- AKI from obstruction; obstructed solitary or transplanted kidney; bilateral obstruction/anuria
- Pain uncontrolled despite adequate analgesia
- Failure to progress after ~4 weeks (risk of irreversible functional loss beyond ~4–6 weeks of complete obstruction)

**Definitive options.** ESWL — renal stones <20 mm and selected proximal ureteric stones; avoid in pregnancy, coagulopathy, active UTI, distal obstruction. Ureteroscopy + laser (Ho:YAG or thulium fibre) — the workhorse for ureteric stones and increasingly renal stones (flexible URS/RIRS); best single-session stone-free rate. PCNL — renal stones >20 mm and staghorn calculi. ⚑ **Struvite/staghorn stones must be cleared completely** — residual fragments harbour organisms and the stone regrows.

## 4.3 ⚑ THE INFECTED OBSTRUCTED KIDNEY — urological emergency

**The short version**
- **Fever (or any sepsis physiology) + an obstructed upper tract = emergency decompression, within hours, not days.**
- Antibiotics alone will not sterilise pus behind an obstruction — no drug delivery past the block. This is a closed-space abscess.
- Decompress by **retrograde ureteric stent OR percutaneous nephrostomy** — both are equally effective; choose on local expertise, patient stability and anatomy.
- **Do not attempt to treat the stone at the same sitting.** Definitive stone treatment is deferred until sepsis has resolved and the antimicrobial course is complete.
- Delay kills: mortality rises steeply with each hour of untreated pyonephrosis.

**Recognition.** Renal colic *plus* fever, rigors, tachycardia, hypotension, delirium or rising lactate. Neutrophilia, raised CRP, AKI. ⚑ Urinalysis may be bland if the obstructed side cannot drain into the bladder — clean urine does not exclude it. CT shows the obstructing stone with perinephric stranding; a dilated system with debris/pus is pyonephrosis.

**Immediate management (in parallel, not sequence):**
1. Resuscitate — IV access, fluids, sepsis bundle, blood and urine cultures **before** antibiotics.
2. Empirical IV antibiotics with Gram-negative cover per local antibiogram/TG for urinary-source sepsis; narrow to susceptibilities.
3. **Call urology immediately for decompression.** Nephrostomy is favoured if unstable, coagulopathic, or the stone is large/impacted; stenting if endoscopic access is straightforward.
4. ICU if shocked; monitor for post-obstructive diuresis after drainage (4.8).
5. Definitive stone treatment later, electively.

⚑ The rule extends beyond stones: fever + hydronephrosis from any cause (malignant compression, retroperitoneal fibrosis, blocked stent) is the same emergency.

## 4.4 Prevention and metabolic workup

**The short version**
- Recurrence ~50% at 10 years — prevention is the point of the follow-up visit.
- Fluid is the single highest-yield intervention: drink to a **urine output of 2–2.5 L/day** (roughly 2.5–3 L intake, more in heat/manual work); urine should be pale.
- ⚑ **Do not restrict dietary calcium.** Normal calcium intake (~1000–1200 mg/day, i.e. 2–3 dairy serves) *reduces* calcium oxalate stone risk by binding oxalate in the gut. Low-calcium diets increase oxalate absorption and increase stones.
- Restrict **sodium** (**NaCl 4–5 g/day**, i.e. <100 mmol sodium) and **animal protein**; moderate oxalate only if hyperoxaluric.
- Send every first stone for **composition analysis**; full metabolic workup for high-risk and recurrent formers.

**Dietary detail.** Sodium restriction matters because natriuresis drags calcium into the urine — high salt generates hypercalciuria. Animal protein raises uric acid, lowers urinary citrate and lowers urine pH — all pro-lithogenic. Moderate high-oxalate foods (spinach, rhubarb, beetroot, nuts, chocolate, strong tea) only in documented hyperoxaluria, and take calcium **with** meals so it binds oxalate in the lumen — calcium *supplements* between meals do not confer the same protection and may raise risk. Avoid excess vitamin C (oxalate precursor); citrus juice raises urinary citrate.

**Drug therapy**

| Agent | Indication | Mechanism / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Thiazide (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide, indapamide) | Recurrent calcium stones with hypercalciuria | ↑ distal tubular Ca reabsorption → ↓ urinary Ca. Requires low-salt diet to work. Watch hypokalaemia (worsens hypocitraturia), hyponatraemia, gout |
| Potassium citrate | Hypocitraturia, uric acid stones, cystinuria, distal RTA | Alkalinises urine and raises citrate (a crystallisation inhibitor). Target urine pH **7.0–7.2 for oral chemolysis (dissolution)** of an existing uric acid stone; **6.2–6.8 for prevention** ⚑ — do not conflate the two/prevention. Caution in CKD/hyperkalaemia |
| Allopurinol | Uric acid stones with hyperuricosuria/hyperuricaemia; calcium oxalate with hyperuricosuria | ↓ uric acid production. Alkalinisation is the primary measure for uric acid stones — allopurinol is adjunct |
| Antibiotics ± complete stone clearance | Struvite | Sterilising the tract without clearing the stone fails |
| Tiopronin, high fluid, alkalinisation | Cystinuria | Aim urine volume **>3 L/day and pH >7.5** |

**Who gets a full metabolic workup** (fasting bloods — creatinine/eGFR, calcium ± ionised, phosphate, urate, electrolytes, PTH if hypercalcaemic — plus 1–2 24-hour urines for volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, urate, sodium, creatinine, pH, and cystine screen if suspected):
- Recurrent stone formers; bilateral or residual stone burden
- Children and young adults (<25 years)
- Non-calcium stones — **uric acid, struvite, cystine** — at first presentation
- Solitary kidney, CKD, nephrocalcinosis, family history
- Malabsorption/bariatric surgery, or a systemic driver (hyperparathyroidism, gout, sarcoid, RTA)

Everyone else: stone analysis, UEC/calcium/urate, urinalysis and culture, imaging follow-up.

## 4.5 UTI in adults — definitions and Australian antibiotic therapy

**The short version**
- **Uncomplicated** = acute cystitis in a non-pregnant, non-immunocompromised adult with a structurally and functionally normal tract. Everything else — pregnancy, catheters, stones, obstruction, transplant, poorly-controlled diabetes, instrumentation — is **complicated**.
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** = significant bacteriuria without urinary symptoms. ⚑ **Treat only in pregnancy and before an invasive urological procedure breaching the mucosa. Otherwise do not treat** — including the elderly, diabetics, catheterised patients, and residents of aged care.
- Commonest organism *E. coli* (~75–85%); then *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus*, and *Staph. saprophyticus* in young sexually active women.
- ⚑ **Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic, March 2025) made nitrofurantoin first-line for acute cystitis and demoted trimethoprim** because of *E. coli* trimethoprim resistance (~20% in Australia). Older notes saying "trimethoprim 300 mg nocte for 3 days, first-line" are out of date.

**Regimens — non-pregnant adults**

| Situation | Therapy | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Acute cystitis, non-pregnant woman | **1st: nitrofurantoin 100 mg orally 6-hourly**; 2nd: fosfomycin 3 g orally single dose; 3rd: trimethoprim 300 mg orally nocte | Nitrofurantoin **5 days**; fosfomycin single dose; trimethoprim **3 days** |
| Acute cystitis, man | Same agent choices, guided by culture; men are by definition complicated — always culture, and consider prostatic involvement | Longer than in women (typically ~7 days; if prostatitis suspected, treat as 4.7) |
| Cystitis/asymptomatic bacteriuria in **pregnancy** | Choose by susceptibility: nitrofurantoin 100 mg 6-hourly, cefalexin 500 mg 12-hourly, amoxicillin 500 mg 8-hourly (if susceptible), or amoxicillin+clavulanate 875+125 mg 12-hourly | **5 days** (some protocols 5–7); ⚑ **send test-of-cure** |
| Acute pyelonephritis, non-pregnant adult, not septic, tolerating oral | ⚑ TG 2025 now permits **initial oral therapy**; amoxicillin+clavulanate **875+125 mg orally 8-hourly** is a listed option (⚑ 8-hourly for pyelonephritis, not the 12-hourly used for cystitis), otherwise culture-directed. ⚑ Ampicillin/amoxicillin are **no longer** recommended as empirical combination therapy in non-pregnant adults (E. coli resistance) | Total ~10–14 days (shorter courses acceptable with fluoroquinolone-class agents; follow current TG) |
| Acute pyelonephritis, septic / vomiting / pregnant | IV therapy: gentamicin-based regimen, or ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily; piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV 8-hourly for severe/resistant risk. In **pregnancy**, amoxicillin 2 g IV 6-hourly + gentamicin retains a role (group B strep cover) | IV until afebrile/improving, then oral switch; **total 10–14 days** |

⚑ Pregnancy-specific traps: **avoid trimethoprim in the first trimester** (folate antagonist — if unavoidable, give folic acid 5 mg daily); **avoid nitrofurantoin at term (≥37 weeks) or if delivery is imminent** (neonatal haemolysis); avoid fluoroquinolones. Screen **all** pregnant women for asymptomatic bacteriuria with an MSU at the first antenatal visit or 12–16 weeks — dipstick is not adequate — because untreated ASB progresses to pyelonephritis and is linked to preterm birth and low birth weight.

⚑ Nitrofurantoin caveats: no therapeutic tissue levels in renal parenchyma or prostate — **useless for pyelonephritis or prostatitis**, cystitis only. Avoid at low eGFR (commonly cited <45 mL/min/1.73 m²) and in G6PD deficiency.

**Diagnosis.** In a young woman with classic dysuria/frequency/urgency and no vaginal discharge, empirical treatment without culture is acceptable. MSU culture is mandatory in men, pregnancy, children, recurrent or failed treatment, pyelonephritis and catheterised patients. Nitrite is specific but insensitive (nitrate-reducing Enterobacterales only); leucocyte esterase sensitive, non-specific. ⚑ Sterile pyuria → chlamydia/urethritis, renal TB, stones, tumour, interstitial nephritis, partly-treated UTI.

**Recurrent UTI** (≥2 in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months). Confirm each episode by culture. Non-antibiotic measures first: fluids, avoid spermicide/diaphragm, post-coital voiding, treat constipation. **Vaginal oestrogen** post-menopause (strong recommendation). **Methenamine hippurate** is non-inferior to daily antibiotic prophylaxis (ALTAR) in a normal tract. Cranberry and D-mannose: weak/contradictory evidence. ⚑ TG 2025 prioritises **patient-initiated self-start treatment** and **post-coital single-dose prophylaxis** over continuous daily prophylaxis. Image ± cystoscopy for stones, incomplete emptying, fistula or tumour if haematuria between episodes, a persistent single organism (*Proteus* → stone), or failure to respond.

**Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI).** ⚑ **Do not send urine and do not treat an asymptomatic catheterised patient.** Every long-term catheter is colonised/biofilmed within days; culturing it yields a positive result that invites unnecessary antibiotics and drives resistance. Diagnose CAUTI only with systemic features (fever, rigors, delirium, suprapubic/loin pain, unexplained sepsis) *plus* no other source; sample from a freshly changed catheter, change or remove the catheter as part of treatment, treat per culture. Cloudy or malodorous urine alone is not an indication.

## 4.6 Pyelonephritis and renal/perinephric abscess

**The short version**
- Fever + rigors + loin pain + **costovertebral angle tenderness**, usually with cystitis symptoms; nausea/vomiting common.
- Admit for: haemodynamic instability, vomiting/unable to tolerate oral, pregnancy, obstruction or single kidney, immunosuppression, failure of outpatient therapy at 48–72 h, significant comorbidity or social isolation.
- **Image (CT with contrast unless AKI, else US) if: septic, no improvement by 48–72 h, known/suspected stone, single kidney, diabetes, or recurrent same-side infection** — you are looking for obstruction, abscess or emphysematous change.
- Persistent fever beyond 72 h on appropriate antibiotics = complication until proven otherwise.

Renal abscess arises by ascending infection (Gram-negatives) or haematogenous seeding (*Staph. aureus* — IVDU, endocarditis, skin sepsis). Perinephric abscess sits between capsule and Gerota's fascia, presenting insidiously with prolonged fever, weight loss and a flank mass; may track to psoas. Antibiotics plus **drainage** — small abscesses (<3 cm) often resolve on antibiotics alone; larger need percutaneous drainage; surgery if refractory.

⚑ **Emphysematous pyelonephritis.** Necrotising gas-forming infection of the renal parenchyma, **>90% in diabetics**, usually poorly controlled; *E. coli* then *Klebsiella*. Often also obstructed. CT is diagnostic (gas in parenchyma/collecting system/perinephric tissue) and classifies severity (Huang–Tseng). Management has shifted ⚑ from immediate nephrectomy to **resuscitation + broad-spectrum IV antibiotics + glycaemic control + percutaneous drainage**, with nephrectomy reserved for failure of that approach; mortality has fallen from historical 40–90% to roughly 10–20%.

## 4.7 Prostatitis

**The short version**
- **Acute bacterial prostatitis**: fever, rigors, perineal/low back pain, dysuria, obstructive voiding, exquisitely tender boggy prostate on PR.
- ⚑ **Do not perform prostatic massage in acute prostatitis** — it is painful and can precipitate bacteraemia. Do a gentle PR to assess tenderness only.
- Organisms are the uropathogens (*E. coli* dominant); in sexually active men <35 also consider *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *N. gonorrhoeae*.
- ⚑ **Nitrofurantoin does not penetrate the prostate** — never use it here. Choose an agent with prostatic penetration (trimethoprim; a fluoroquinolone where justified by susceptibility) after IV therapy if severely unwell.
- Duration is **long: 2–4 weeks for acute, 4–6 weeks (or longer) for chronic bacterial prostatitis** — short courses relapse.

Investigations: MSU culture, blood cultures if febrile, STI NAAT in younger men; PSA is unhelpful acutely (it rises). Complications: acute retention (⚑ prefer **suprapubic** catheterisation — urethral instrumentation through an inflamed prostate risks bacteraemia) and prostatic abscess (suspect if fever persists >36–48 h on appropriate antibiotics; image and drain).

**Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS, NIH category III)** is far commoner than chronic bacterial prostatitis: ≥3 months of pelvic/perineal pain with voiding and sexual dysfunction, **no positive cultures**, no fever. Repeated antibiotic courses are not the answer — manage multimodally (UPOINT domains): alpha-blocker, pelvic floor physiotherapy, neuromodulators (amitriptyline, pregabalin), and address anxiety/depression. Distinguishing category II (chronic *bacterial* — recurrent UTIs with the same organism, responds to a long antibiotic course) from category III is the examinable discrimination.

## 4.8 Obstructive uropathy and hydronephrosis

**The short version**
- Obstruction is a **reversible** cause of AKI — always consider it, and the test is a **renal tract ultrasound**.
- Think obstruction when: anuria or fluctuating urine output, palpable bladder, known stones/pelvic malignancy/prior pelvic radiotherapy, single kidney, or an unexplained AKI with bland urinalysis.
- **Bilateral hydronephrosis (or unilateral in a single kidney) is needed to cause AKI**; unilateral obstruction with a normal contralateral kidney gives pain, not a rising creatinine.
- Below the bladder → catheterise. Above the bladder → stent or nephrostomy.
- After relieving obstruction, watch for **post-obstructive diuresis**.

**Causes**

| Level | Causes |
|---|---|
| Upper tract (kidney/ureter) | • Stone • PUJ obstruction • Transitional cell carcinoma • Extrinsic compression: cervical/ovarian/colorectal/prostate malignancy, lymphadenopathy • Retroperitoneal fibrosis • Ureteric injury post-pelvic surgery • Blocked/migrated stent |
| Bladder outlet and below | • BPH (commonest in men) • Prostate cancer • Urethral stricture • Phimosis/meatal stenosis • Blocked catheter • Neurogenic bladder • Constipation, pelvic mass, anticholinergic drugs |

**Consequences.** Acute complete obstruction causes a transient rise then fall in renal blood flow, tubular dysfunction and, beyond ~4–6 weeks, irreversible parenchymal loss with cortical thinning. Partial chronic obstruction may present with polyuria/nocturia (concentrating defect) and hyperkalaemic distal RTA rather than anuria. ⚑ Beware the false-negative ultrasound: obstruction can exist **without** hydronephrosis early, in volume depletion, and in retroperitoneal fibrosis or malignant encasement where the ureter cannot dilate — if suspicion is high, proceed to CT.

**Post-obstructive diuresis.** After relief of bilateral or solitary-kidney obstruction, urine output can exceed 200 mL/h — initially physiological (offloading retained solute and water), pathological if tubular dysfunction drives ongoing salt and water loss. Measure hourly urine output, weigh daily, check UEC and magnesium at least 12-hourly, and replace roughly 50–75% of losses with crystalloid — replacing 100% perpetuates the diuresis. Watch hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia and hypovolaemic collapse. ⚑ Transient **haematuria ex vacuo** after decompressing a chronically distended bladder does not require clamping the catheter (risks re-obstruction) — drain completely and monitor.

## 5.3 Confusable pairs

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| Torsion vs epididymo-orchitis | Torsion: instantaneous onset, vomiting, high-riding transverse testis, absent cremasteric reflex, normal urinalysis, afebrile → theatre. EO: builds over days, dysuria/discharge/fever, pyuria, normal lie, reflex preserved → antibiotics. Prehn's sign decides nothing |
| Torsion vs torted appendix testis | Whole testis exquisitely tender with abnormal lie vs a 3–5 mm tender upper-pole nodule and blue dot, normal lie, well child aged 7–12 |
| Seminoma vs NSGCT | Seminoma: later peak, **AFP never raised**, β-hCG up to 30%, orderly nodal spread, radiosensitive. NSGCT: younger, AFP/β-hCG raised, early haematogenous spread, chemosensitive (BEP), not radiosensitive |
| Hydrocele vs varicocele vs epididymal cyst | Hydrocele transilluminates, testis impalpable within. Varicocele does **not** transilluminate, worse standing/Valsalva, empties supine. Epididymal cyst transilluminates and is **separate from and above** the testis |
| BPH vs prostate cancer | BPH: smooth, symmetrical, rubbery, sulcus preserved, years of voiding symptoms. Cancer: hard, craggy, asymmetric, sulcus lost — refer regardless of PSA; usually asymptomatic when curable, so LUTS discriminates poorly. Suspicious extras: bone pain, weight loss, haematuria, renal failure |
| Storage vs voiding LUTS | Storage (frequency, urgency, nocturia, urge leak) = the bladder. Voiding (hesitancy, weak stream, straining, terminal dribble) = obstruction or a weak detrusor. Obstruction causes storage symptoms *secondarily* via detrusor hypertrophy |
| Overactive bladder vs BPH obstruction | Urgency dominant, normal stream, low PVR = OAB. Voiding-predominant, raised PVR, large gland = obstruction. **PVR decides** — an antimuscarinic in an obstructed man precipitates retention |
| Acute vs chronic retention | Acute: agonising, tender palpable bladder, immediate relief, normal creatinine. Chronic: painless, very large volume, enuresis, ± hydronephrosis and renal impairment. High- vs low-pressure chronic retention is decided by upper tracts and creatinine |
| Renal colic vs pyelonephritis vs ruptured AAA | Colic: writhing, afebrile, haematuria. Pyelonephritis: fever, rigors, CVA tenderness, pyuria. AAA: >50 y, hypotension, pulsatile mass — exclude before anchoring on stone. Colic **plus** fever is a third entity: infected obstructed kidney |
| Uncomplicated vs complicated UTI | Uncomplicated = cystitis in a non-pregnant, non-immunocompromised woman with a normal tract. Complicated = men, pregnancy, catheter, stones, obstruction, transplant, instrumentation, poorly controlled diabetes → culture, longer course, find the cause |
| Asymptomatic bacteriuria you treat vs do not | Treat **only** in pregnancy and before an invasive urological procedure breaching mucosa. Do not treat the elderly, diabetics, catheterised patients or aged-care residents |
| Cystitis vs pyelonephritis vs prostatitis | Cystitis: dysuria/frequency, afebrile, 5 days nitrofurantoin. Pyelonephritis: fever, rigors, loin pain, 10–14 days, **nitrofurantoin useless**. Prostatitis: fever, perineal pain, boggy tender prostate, 2–4 weeks with a prostate-penetrating agent, no massage |
| RCC vs upper tract UC vs angiomyolipoma | RCC: enhancing solid **parenchymal** mass → partial/radical nephrectomy. UTUC: **filling defect in the collecting system**, Lynch/aristolochic acid → nephroureterectomy with bladder cuff. AML: macroscopic fat, tuberous sclerosis, benign |
| Bladder cancer vs radiation cystitis | Both give haematuria and irritative symptoms after pelvic radiotherapy — but radiotherapy is itself a risk factor for urothelial cancer, so the history never excuses skipping cystoscopy |
| Alpha-blocker vs 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor | Alpha-blocker: relief in days–2 weeks, any gland size; postural hypotension, retrograde ejaculation, IOFS. 5-ARI: shrinks the gland, needs 3–6 months, only for larger glands, **halves PSA**, teratogenic |
| Stress vs urge incontinence | Stress: leak *at the moment of* cough/lift, no urgency, parity/menopause or post-prostatectomy → pelvic floor training first. Urge: leak *preceded by* urgency, triggered by key-in-door → bladder training then mirabegron/antimuscarinic |
| Testicular cancer vs epididymo-orchitis | Cancer: painless, firm, **intratesticular**, afebrile. EO: painful, tender, epididymal first, febrile, pyuria. A mass persisting after antibiotics, or failure to settle by 72 h, means image — tumours masquerade as epididymo-orchitis |
| Fournier's vs simple scrotal cellulitis | Fournier's: pain grossly out of proportion, systemic toxicity, crepitus, dusky skin, bullae, skin anaesthesia, diabetic/immunosuppressed → theatre. Cellulitis: pain matches the erythema, responds to antibiotics. CT must not delay theatre |

## 5.4 The must-not-miss list

| Emergency | Time-critical action |
|---|---|
| **Testicular torsion** | Straight to theatre — salvage near-universal <6 h, falling by 12–24 h, rare beyond 24 h. Imaging must not delay. Bilateral orchidopexy |
| **Infected obstructed kidney** | Fever + obstruction = decompress within **hours** (stent or nephrostomy) plus IV antibiotics after cultures; defer stone treatment |
| **Fournier's gangrene** | Resuscitate, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics ± clindamycin, **urgent radical debridement**, planned re-look at 24–48 h. Antibiotics never substitute for the knife |
| **High-pressure chronic retention with renal failure** | Catheterise and drain completely; monitor for post-obstructive diuresis; correct electrolytes; function usually recovers |
| **Cauda equina syndrome** | Senior review + urgent **whole-spine MRI**. Retention is a *late* sign and a normal PVR does not exclude it; add corticosteroid if metastatic cord compression |
| **Priapism** | **Ischaemic** (low-flow): painful, rigid, no flow — a compartment syndrome; aspirate ± intracavernosal phenylephrine, treat by **4 h**; prolonged episodes cause fibrosis and ED. **Non-ischaemic** (high-flow, post-traumatic): painless, partly rigid, not an emergency — observe/embolise |
| **Paraphimosis** | Foreskin trapped behind the glans with constriction and oedema — reduce the same hour (compression, ice, local anaesthetic); dorsal slit if reduction fails |
| **Penile fracture** | Tunica albuginea tear during intercourse — crack, detumescence, aubergine deformity. Urgent exploration and repair; retrograde urethrogram if blood at the meatus |
| **Post-obstructive diuresis** | Hourly output, daily weights, frequent electrolytes; replace only part of losses (5.5) — replacing 100% perpetuates it |

## ⚠ Inconsistencies found in Sections 1–4

1. **How much to replace in post-obstructive diuresis.** Section 2.4 says "no more than about 75% of the preceding hour's output"; Section 4.8 says "roughly 50–75% of losses". Same intent (deliberate mild negative balance), but the two files should carry one figure. 5.5 quotes the Section 4 range.
2. **POD monitoring interval.** Section 2.4 says electrolytes and creatinine "every 8–24 h"; Section 4.8 says UEC and magnesium "at least 12-hourly".
3. **Age at which PSA is offered to higher-risk men.** Section 1.5 (2026 guideline) says offer 2-yearly **from age 45**; Section 2.2, quoting the RACGP Red Book 2024, says from 50, or **40–45** with significant family history. Section 1 is the safer answer for the paper, but the starting ages differ.
