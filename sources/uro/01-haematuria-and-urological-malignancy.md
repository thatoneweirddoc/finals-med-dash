# Urology Notes — Section 1: Haematuria and Urological Malignancy
*Sources: 2026 Guidelines for the Early Detection of Prostate Cancer in Australia (PCFA/RACGP-endorsed), RACGP Red Book 10th edn, Cancer Council Australia Optimal Care Pathways, EAU Guidelines (NMIBC, MIBC, RCC, UTUC, Prostate Cancer) 2025, WA Health Urology Referral Access Criteria, eviQ. Scope: assessment of haematuria and the four urological malignancies most likely to be examined — bladder, renal, upper tract urothelial and prostate.*

## 1.1 Haematuria

**The short version**
- Visible haematuria (VH) in an adult is malignancy until proven otherwise — full upper + lower tract workup regardless of age, even if it settles and even if a UTI was cultured.
- Non-visible haematuria (NVH) is defined on **microscopy** (≥3 RBC/hpf), not on dipstick alone. Dipstick positive → confirm with MC&S before acting.
- Transient causes must be excluded/treated first: UTI, menstruation, vigorous exercise, recent instrumentation, sexual activity. Recheck 6–12 weeks after treating UTI.
- Standard workup = upper tract imaging (CT IVP/CT urography, or US if young/renal impairment/contrast contraindication) **plus** cystoscopy. Cytology is optional and is a poor rule-out test.
- Proteinuria, dysmorphic RBCs/red cell casts, hypertension or rising creatinine = **nephrological**, not urological — refer renal.

### Definitions and the dipstick trap

Dipstick detects haem peroxidase activity, so it turns positive for myoglobin and free haemoglobin as well as intact RBCs — rhabdomyolysis and haemolysis give "blood +++" with a bland microscopy. Beetroot, rifampicin, phenazopyridine and porphyria discolour urine with no dipstick positivity at all. Sequence in any stem: dipstick → formal MC&S → decide.

NVH is persistent if present on ≥2 of 3 specimens. ⚑ The old "symptomatic vs asymptomatic NVH" split from UK guidance has largely given way to **risk-stratified** pathways: the question is not "is it symptomatic" but "how many malignancy risk factors, and how many RBC/hpf".

### When investigation is *not* warranted

| Situation | Why | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Culture-proven UTI | Inflamed urothelium bleeds | Treat, then **repeat microscopy 6–12 weeks post-treatment**. Persistent haematuria → investigate |
| Menstruation | Contamination | Repeat MSU ≥7 days after menses ends |
| Vigorous exercise (running, contact sport) | Bladder wall trauma, RBC trauma | Repeat after 48–72 h abstinence from exercise |
| Recent catheter/cystoscopy/TURP | Iatrogenic | Repeat after healing interval |
| Known glomerular disease with stable function, proteinuria, dysmorphic RBCs | Not a urothelial problem | Renal referral, not cystoscopy |

⚑ A cultured UTI in a patient with **visible** haematuria does **not** excuse imaging and cystoscopy. Assuming "it was just the UTI" in a 65-year-old smoker is the commonest way bladder cancer is missed — a favourite short-answer trap. Conversely, the truly low-risk NVH patient (<50, <10 pack-years, <10 RBC/hpf, no other risk factors, no LUTS) can reasonably be managed by shared decision-making with repeat urinalysis within 6 months.

Risk factors lowering the threshold to investigate: age >50, male sex, smoking (dose-dependent, dominant modifiable risk), aromatic amine exposure, cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide, pelvic radiotherapy, chronic IDC, analgesic nephropathy, aristolochic acid, schistosomiasis, prior urothelial cancer.

### Investigation pathway

1. **MSU MC&S** — exclude infection, look for dysmorphic RBCs/casts.
2. **Bloods** — UEC/eGFR, FBC, coagulation if anticoagulated, PSA in men where relevant, urine ACR.
3. **Upper tract imaging**
   - **CT IVP / CT urography** — reference standard: renal parenchymal masses, stones, and critically the pelvicalyceal and ureteric urothelium on the delayed excretory phase. Preferred for VH, age >50, or any risk factors.
   - **US KUB** — acceptable for younger patients, renal impairment, contrast allergy, pregnancy. Materially less sensitive for small stones and urothelial lesions, essentially blind to ureteric TCC; a normal US does not exclude upper tract disease in a high-risk patient.
4. **Flexible cystoscopy** — reference standard for bladder and urethra, required for all VH. Imaging cannot exclude flat CIS (velvety erythematous patch, endoscopically subtle).
5. **Urine cytology** — good specificity and reasonable sensitivity for **high-grade** UC and CIS, poor for low-grade papillary tumours. ⚑ An adjunct only; negative cytology never rules out cancer. Most useful when CIS is suspected.

⚑ Anticoagulation does **not** explain haematuria. Warfarin/DOAC use in a patient with VH is an indication to investigate, not to reassure — a significant proportion have an underlying lesion unmasked by the anticoagulant.

### Causes by age

| Age band | Commonest / must-not-miss |
|---|---|
| Children | • Post-streptococcal GN • IgA nephropathy • Alport syndrome • Hypercalciuria • Wilms tumour • HUS |
| Young adults (<40) | • UTI • Stones • Exercise • IgA nephropathy (synpharyngitic VH) • Thin basement membrane disease • Sickle cell trait (papillary necrosis) |
| 40–60 | • Stones • UTI • **Urothelial carcinoma — risk climbing** • BPH • RCC |
| >60 | • **Bladder urothelial carcinoma** • RCC • Prostate cancer/BPH • Radiation cystitis • Anticoagulant-unmasked lesion |

### Referral urgency (Australian access criteria pattern)

- **Category 1 (≤30 days):** visible haematuria at any age; NVH with a lesion, upper tract abnormality or hydronephrosis on imaging.
- **Category 2 (≤90 days):** persistent NVH with normal imaging.
- Pre-referral work should already include MSU MC&S and upper tract imaging.

## 1.2 Bladder cancer

**The short version**
- >90% urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma; painless visible haematuria is the presentation until proven otherwise.
- Smoking is the dominant risk factor; aromatic amines the classic occupational one; *Schistosoma haematobium* causes **squamous** cell carcinoma.
- TURBT is both diagnostic and therapeutic — it must sample **detrusor muscle** to allow T-staging.
- The pivotal split is **non-muscle-invasive (Ta/T1/CIS)** vs **muscle-invasive (≥T2)**: intravesical therapy + surveillance vs radical treatment.
- Intravesical BCG is for intermediate/high-risk NMIBC (especially CIS and T1 high grade); a single immediate intravesical chemotherapy instillation within 24 h of TURBT reduces recurrence in low-risk disease.

### Risk factors and histology

| Exposure | Histology | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Smoking | Urothelial | ~50% of cases; risk falls but never normalises after cessation |
| Aromatic amines (β-naphthylamine, benzidine) — dye, rubber, leather, painting, aluminium, hairdressing | Urothelial | Latency of decades — take an occupational history |
| Cyclophosphamide / ifosfamide (acrolein) | Urothelial | Mesna reduces haemorrhagic cystitis; malignancy risk persists |
| Pelvic radiotherapy | Urothelial | Also causes radiation cystitis |
| Chronic irritation — long-term IDC, recurrent stones, exstrophy | **Squamous** | Catheter + haematuria in spinal cord injury |
| *Schistosoma haematobium* | **Squamous** | Egypt/sub-Saharan Africa; migrant history |
| Urachal remnant (bladder dome) | **Adenocarcinoma** | Rare, mucinous |
| Aristolochic acid, Lynch syndrome, phenacetin | Urothelial (upper tract) | See 1.4 |

### Presentation

Painless VH (~80%). Irritative LUTS — frequency, urgency, dysuria with sterile pyuria — should raise **CIS**, which is flat, high-grade and prone to progression; recurrent culture-negative "UTIs" in an older smoker is the same trap in different clothing. Advanced: pelvic pain, obstructive uropathy from ureteric orifice involvement, leg oedema, bone pain. Field change is intrinsic — the whole urothelium from renal pelvis to urethra is at risk, hence lifetime surveillance even after superficial disease.

### Diagnosis and staging

Cystoscopy → **TURBT** under anaesthetic, with bimanual EUA before and after resection to assess a palpable mass. Resect to include detrusor: a specimen with no muscle cannot be staged. Blue-light cystoscopy with hexaminolevulinate improves CIS detection; mapping biopsies where cytology is positive but the bladder looks normal. Staging: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with urography for muscle-invasive disease; bone scan if bone pain or raised ALP.

T-stages: **Ta** non-invasive papillary; **Tis** flat CIS; **T1** invades lamina propria; **T2** detrusor muscle; **T3** perivesical fat; **T4** adjacent organs/pelvic sidewall.

### Non-muscle-invasive disease (Ta, T1, CIS)

Roughly 75% of new bladder cancers. Management is stratified by grade, stage, size, multifocality, recurrence rate and presence of CIS.

| Risk group | Typical features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Solitary, primary, Ta low grade, <3 cm, no CIS | • Single immediate intravesical chemo (mitomycin C, gemcitabine or epirubicin) **within 24 h** of TURBT, ideally 2 h • Surveillance cystoscopy |
| Intermediate | Multifocal/recurrent low-grade Ta, or fitting neither low nor high | • BCG induction (6 weekly) + maintenance 3-weekly at 3, 6, 12 months = **1 year** • Or intravesical chemo ≤1 year |
| High | Any T1, any high grade, CIS, multiple adverse features | • **Re-TURBT** (2–6 weeks) for T1, incomplete resection or absent muscle • BCG induction + maintenance **1–3 years** (3-weekly at 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36 months) • Early radical cystectomy if very high risk |

⚑ Immediate instillation is contraindicated with bladder perforation or extensive resection — extravasated mitomycin causes severe chemical peritonitis.

**Intravesical BCG.** Live attenuated *M. bovis*; a non-specific immunostimulant recruiting a local Th1 response that clears residual tumour. Superior to intravesical chemotherapy for preventing recurrence *and* progression in high-risk disease. Adverse effects: irritative LUTS and low-grade fever (common, self-limiting), granulomatous prostatitis, contracted bladder, and rarely **BCGosis** — disseminated infection with sepsis-like illness, hepatitis or pneumonitis, treated with antituberculous therapy. Do not instil after traumatic catheterisation, or with active haematuria or UTI. BCG-unresponsive disease → radical cystectomy.

**Surveillance.** Cystoscopy-based and lifelong, front-loaded: 3 months post-resection for all, then intervals extended by risk; high-risk disease also gets periodic upper tract imaging and cytology. No blood test substitutes for cystoscopy.

### Muscle-invasive disease (≥T2)

- **Neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy** (dose-dense MVAC or gemcitabine–cisplatin) before radical cystectomy — roughly a 5% absolute gain in 5-year OS in pooled trial data. Needs adequate renal function and performance status; carboplatin is **not** an equivalent substitute in the curative setting.
- **Radical cystectomy** + bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy — cystoprostatectomy in men, anterior exenteration in women. Diversion by ileal conduit (commonest), orthotopic neobladder, or continent cutaneous diversion.
- **Trimodal therapy** (maximal TURBT + concurrent chemoradiotherapy) is the bladder-preserving alternative for selected patients — solitary tumour, no hydronephrosis, no extensive CIS, complete resection — and for those unfit for cystectomy.
- Metastatic: platinum-based chemotherapy; checkpoint inhibitors and antibody–drug conjugates (enfortumab vedotin) in combination/later lines — check eviQ.

## 1.3 Renal cell carcinoma

**The short version**
- Arises from proximal tubular epithelium; **clear cell** is the commonest subtype and the one linked to VHL.
- The classic triad (flank pain + haematuria + palpable mass) occurs in <10% and signals advanced disease — over half are now **incidentalomas** on imaging done for something else.
- Paraneoplastic syndromes are heavily examined: polycythaemia (EPO), hypercalcaemia (PTHrP), hypertension (renin), **Stauffer syndrome** (non-metastatic hepatic dysfunction, reversible after nephrectomy).
- Surgery is the only curative modality — **partial nephrectomy** wherever technically feasible; RCC is intrinsically chemo- and radio-resistant.
- **New left-sided varicocele that does not decompress when supine** = left renal vein obstruction → image the kidney.

### Risk and subtypes

Smoking, obesity, hypertension, acquired cystic disease of dialysis; inherited syndromes — von Hippel–Lindau (clear cell, bilateral/multifocal, plus phaeochromocytoma and haemangioblastomas), hereditary papillary RCC (MET), Birt–Hogg–Dubé (chromophobe/oncocytoma, fibrofolliculomas, pneumothorax), tuberous sclerosis (angiomyolipoma).

| Subtype | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear cell | ~70–75% | VHL pathway; most likely to metastasise; most IO/TKI-responsive |
| Papillary | ~10–15% | Type 1 (MET) better prognosis than type 2 |
| Chromophobe | ~5% | Best prognosis; overlaps morphologically with benign oncocytoma |
| Collecting duct / medullary | Rare | Medullary RCC: sickle cell trait, aggressive |

### Presentation and paraneoplastic syndromes

Incidental detection dominates because CT and US are done so freely — that is *why* the classic triad is now rare, and the exam wants that reasoning. Symptomatic: haematuria, flank pain, weight loss, FUO, anaemia (commoner than polycythaemia despite the teaching), new varicocele, IVC syndrome from tumour thrombus.

| Syndrome | Mediator | Clue in a stem |
|---|---|---|
| Polycythaemia | Ectopic erythropoietin | Raised Hb/Hct with a renal mass |
| Hypercalcaemia | PTHrP (or lytic mets) | High Ca²⁺, **suppressed** PTH |
| Hypertension | Renin | New/refractory HTN with renal mass |
| **Stauffer syndrome** | IL-6 / GM-CSF | Deranged LFTs (↑ALP, ↑transaminases, ↑PT) with **no liver metastases**; resolves after nephrectomy |
| AA amyloidosis, anaemia, pyrexia | Inflammatory cytokines | "FUO" workup |

**Left varicocele.** The left gonadal vein drains into the left renal vein; the right drains straight into the IVC. Left renal vein tumour thrombus therefore obstructs left gonadal drainage. A varicocele that is new, right-sided, or fails to decompress with recumbency mandates cross-sectional imaging.

### Staging and management

Diagnosis is radiological: multiphase contrast CT showing an enhancing solid mass (>15–20 HU enhancement). Biopsy is not routine before nephrectomy for a classic mass, but is used before ablation, before systemic therapy, and where lymphoma/metastasis/abscess is a differential.

| T stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| T1a | ≤4 cm, confined to kidney |
| T1b | >4 to ≤7 cm, confined |
| T2a | >7 to ≤10 cm, confined |
| T2b | >10 cm, confined |
| T3a | Renal vein or its segmental branches, pelvicalyceal system, or perinephric/renal sinus fat — within Gerota's fascia |
| T3b | IVC **below** the diaphragm |
| T3c | IVC **above** the diaphragm, or invades IVC wall |
| T4 | Beyond Gerota's fascia, including contiguous ipsilateral adrenal involvement |

Management:
- **Partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing)** preferred for T1 wherever feasible — equivalent oncological outcomes, better renal function, fewer downstream CKD/cardiovascular events. ⚑ A genuine shift from older texts making radical nephrectomy the default.
- **Radical nephrectomy** for large, central or locally advanced tumours. ⚑ The ipsilateral adrenal is spared unless involved or radiologically abnormal; routine adrenalectomy is no longer standard. Lymphadenectomy is not routinely therapeutic.
- **Active surveillance / percutaneous ablation** (RFA, cryotherapy) for small renal masses in elderly or comorbid patients.
- **Metastatic:** cytoreductive nephrectomy in selected patients; systemic therapy is now **checkpoint inhibitor combinations** (dual IO, or IO + VEGF TKI), not the old interferon/IL-2 era. Metastasectomy for oligometastatic disease.
- ⚑ "Chemo- and radio-resistant" holds for conventional cytotoxics (high P-glycoprotein expression) but RCC is *not* treatment-resistant — it is among the most immunotherapy-sensitive solid tumours. Radiotherapy retains a palliative role for bone and brain metastases.

Metastatic pattern: lung (cannonball lesions), bone (**lytic**, expansile, hypervascular — bleed briskly at surgery), liver, brain. Notorious for late relapse, so surveillance runs for years.

## 1.4 Upper tract urothelial carcinoma

**The short version**
- Uncommon (~5–10% of urothelial cancers) but disproportionately examined because of its associations and its surgery.
- Risk: smoking, aromatic amines, **Lynch syndrome** (MSH2 especially), aristolochic acid/Balkan endemic nephropathy, analgesic (phenacetin) nephropathy.
- Presents with visible haematuria, or flank pain from clot colic/obstruction; a filling defect in the collecting system on CT urography.
- Standard treatment is **radical nephroureterectomy with excision of a bladder cuff**; kidney-sparing endoscopic management for low-risk, low-grade, unifocal small tumours, solitary kidney or CKD.
- Lifelong bladder surveillance follows, because ~20–50% develop subsequent bladder tumours (field change plus seeding).

Diagnosis: CT urography (delayed-phase filling defect), selective ureteric cytology/washings, ureteroscopy with biopsy. Suspect Lynch syndrome and refer for germline testing if young, or with personal/family history of colorectal, endometrial, gastric or ovarian cancer. The bladder cuff must be excised — retained distal ureter carries a high recurrence rate. A single post-operative intravesical chemotherapy instillation reduces subsequent bladder recurrence. ⚑ Platinum chemotherapy is best given **neoadjuvantly** in high-risk disease, because post-nephroureterectomy eGFR often falls below the threshold for cisplatin.

## 1.5 Prostate cancer

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Australia does not run a population screening programme**, but the 2026 national guidelines now *recommend offering* two-yearly PSA to men aged 50–69 after shared decision-making — a shift from the more neutral "if the patient requests it after discussion" framing of 2016.
- ⚑ **DRE is no longer recommended as a routine adjunct to PSA in primary care.** It remains part of specialist assessment.
- ⚑ **mpMRI before biopsy** is now standard; biopsy for PI-RADS 4–5, or PI-RADS 3 with PSA density ≥0.15.
- Grading is by **ISUP grade group** (1–5); clinically significant disease = **grade group ≥2**.
- Bone metastases are characteristically **osteoblastic/sclerotic**, spreading via Batson's venous plexus to the axial skeleton.

### PSA testing — the Australian position

| Group | Recommendation | PSA action threshold (repeat in 1–3 months, then refer if confirmed) |
|---|---|---|
| 45–49, average risk | Not routinely offered; test if the man raises it, after discussion | ≥1.0 µg/L |
| 50–69, average risk | **Offer 2-yearly** after shared decision-making | ≥3.0 µg/L |
| 45–49, higher risk | **Offer 2-yearly from age 45** | ≥1.0 µg/L |
| 50–69, higher risk | Offer 2-yearly | ≥2.0 µg/L |
| ≥70 | Individualised; only if life expectancy >7 years and after documented discussion | ≥5.5 µg/L |

"Higher risk" = at least double population risk: first-degree relative with prostate cancer (especially diagnosed young), Black sub-Saharan African ancestry, or pathogenic **BRCA2** variant.

PSA is organ-specific but **not cancer-specific** — it rises with BPH, prostatitis, UTI, retention, recent ejaculation, cycling and instrumentation, and falls roughly by half on 5α-reductase inhibitors (double the measured value to interpret). The harms of testing are overdiagnosis and overtreatment of indolent grade group 1 disease; mpMRI-first pathways and active surveillance exist to blunt exactly that.

### Diagnosis

Confirmed elevated PSA → urology referral → DRE + **mpMRI** reported with PI-RADS. Biopsy is targeted (usually **transperineal** — lower sepsis rate than transrectal) to PI-RADS 4–5, or PI-RADS 3 with PSA density ≥0.15 µg/L/mL. Negative mpMRI with low PSA density permits monitoring rather than immediate biopsy. Most cancers arise in the **peripheral zone** (palpable on DRE); BPH is transition zone.

### Grading and risk stratification

| ISUP grade group | Gleason score |
|---|---|
| 1 | ≤6 (3+3) |
| 2 | 7 (**3+4**) |
| 3 | 7 (**4+3**) |
| 4 | 8 (4+4, 3+5, 5+3) |
| 5 | 9–10 |

⚑ Grade group is now the reporting standard; "Gleason 7" alone is inadequate because 3+4 and 4+3 behave differently.

| Risk (localised) | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Low | ISUP 1 **and** PSA <10 **and** cT1–T2a ⚑ (EAU says cT1–2; the cT2a ceiling is the D'Amico/NCCN version, and it is the one that keeps the intermediate row below coherent) |
| Intermediate | ISUP 2–3, or PSA 10–20, or cT2b–c (favourable vs unfavourable subdivided by grade group and PSA) |
| High | ISUP 4–5, **or** PSA >20, **or** cT3–4/N+ |

Staging beyond DRE/mpMRI: bone scan ± CT for intermediate-unfavourable and high-risk disease. ⚑ PSMA PET/CT is now widely used in Australia for primary staging and biochemical recurrence, with superior sensitivity to conventional imaging.

### Management by risk

| Risk / setting | Options |
|---|---|
| Low risk, ISUP 1 | **Active surveillance** — serial PSA, repeat mpMRI and biopsy; treat on progression. Distinguish from *watchful waiting* (symptom-directed palliation in men unfit for radical treatment) |
| Intermediate risk | Radical prostatectomy ± pelvic node dissection, **or** radical RT (EBRT ± brachytherapy) with short-course ADT if unfavourable |
| High risk / locally advanced | EBRT + **long-course ADT** (2–3 years), or surgery within a multimodal plan; adjuvant/salvage RT for adverse pathology or PSA relapse |
| Metastatic hormone-sensitive | ADT (LHRH agonist/antagonist or orchidectomy) **plus** an ARPI and/or docetaxel — ⚑ ADT alone is no longer adequate first-line |
| Castration-resistant | ARPIs, taxanes, radium-223 (bone-dominant), ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA, PARP inhibitors for BRCA/HRR mutations |

⚑ LHRH **agonists** cause an initial testosterone surge — cover with an antiandrogen or use a GnRH antagonist if there is a risk of tumour flare (impending cord compression, severe bone pain, obstruction).

### Complications of treatment

- **Radical prostatectomy:** ED (nerve-sparing dependent), stress incontinence, anastomotic stricture, loss of ejaculate.
- **Radiotherapy:** radiation proctitis with rectal bleeding, radiation cystitis, urethral stricture, delayed-onset ED, small second-malignancy risk.
- **ADT:** flushing, loss of libido, ED, sarcopenia and weight gain, gynaecomastia, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance, cardiovascular risk, mood/cognitive effects, and **osteoporosis** — baseline and periodic DXA, calcium, vitamin D, exercise and bone-protective therapy.

### Metastatic pattern

Lumbar spine, pelvis, ribs, proximal femora — **osteoblastic** on plain film/CT, intense bone-scan uptake, raised **ALP with normal or near-normal calcium** (unlike the lytic, hypercalcaemic pattern of myeloma or RCC). Spread via Batson's vertebral venous plexus bypasses the caval circulation, explaining axial predominance. Consequences: cord compression, pathological fracture, bone pain.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Visible haematuria in an adult, even once, even with a positive urine culture | Full urological workup: upper tract imaging + cystoscopy |
| Microscopy threshold defining non-visible haematuria | >3 RBC per high-power field |
| Dipstick "blood" positive, microscopy shows no RBCs | Myoglobinuria or haemoglobinuria |
| Haematuria + proteinuria + red cell casts + hypertension | Glomerular — refer nephrology |
| Interval to recheck urine after treating a UTI | 6–12 weeks |
| Urine cytology's blind spot | Low-grade papillary tumours (good for high-grade/CIS) |
| Sterile pyuria + irritative LUTS + normal-looking bladder in a smoker | Bladder CIS |
| Schistosoma haematobium bladder cancer histology | Squamous cell carcinoma |
| Timing of single post-TURBT intravesical chemotherapy | Within 24 h (ideally 2 h); withhold if perforation |
| BCG maintenance duration, high-risk NMIBC | Up to 3 years |
| TURBT specimen with no detrusor muscle | Cannot stage — re-resect |
| Deranged LFTs with a renal mass and no liver metastases | Stauffer syndrome |
| New left varicocele that doesn't empty when lying flat | Left renal vein obstruction — image for RCC |
| RCC T1a cut-off | ≤4 cm |
| Preferred surgery for a 3 cm renal mass | Partial (nephron-sparing) nephrectomy |
| PSA action threshold, average-risk man aged 50–69 | ≥3.0 µg/L, repeat in 1–3 months |
| Routine DRE alongside PSA in general practice (2026 guidance) | Not recommended |
| Biopsy indication for a PI-RADS 3 lesion | PSA density ≥0.15 µg/L/mL |
| Gleason 4+3 = which grade group | Grade group 3 |
| Bone metastasis appearance in prostate cancer | Osteoblastic/sclerotic, raised ALP, calcium usually normal |
| Man on finasteride with PSA 2.0 | Interpret as ~4.0 — double the value |
| Upper tract urothelial carcinoma standard operation | Radical nephroureterectomy with bladder cuff excision |
