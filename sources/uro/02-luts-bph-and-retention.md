# Urology Notes — Section 2: LUTS, Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia and Urinary Retention

*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines, Australian Prescriber, Australian Medicines Handbook, RACGP Red Book 10th ed (2024) and AJGP, PCFA/Cancer Council PSA guideline update (2026), TGA mesh actions, Continence Foundation of Australia, USANZ, EAU male LUTS/incontinence/neuro-urology guidelines (2024–25), ICS terminology. Scope: male LUTS, BPH pharmacology and surgery, acute and chronic retention, incontinence in both sexes, neurogenic bladder.*

---

## 2.1 LUTS — classifying the symptom, then classifying the patient

**The short version**
- LUTS is a symptom complex, not a diagnosis: **storage** (frequency, urgency, nocturia, urge incontinence), **voiding** (hesitancy, weak/intermittent stream, straining, terminal dribble), **post-micturition** (incomplete emptying, post-micturition dribble).
- The split points to different organs: voiding → outflow obstruction (prostate, stricture) or underactive detrusor; storage → the bladder itself (detrusor overactivity, infection, stone, CIS); post-micturition → urethral pooling.
- ⚑ "Prostatism" is obsolete. Men get storage symptoms without prostates and women get identical voiding symptoms. Say LUTS.
- IPSS: 7 items, 0–5 each, total 0–35 — **mild ≤7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35** — plus a separate 0–6 quality-of-life ("bother") item.
- **Nocturia is its own problem with its own differential.** Work through nocturnal polyuria, global polyuria, reduced capacity and primary sleep disorder before blaming the prostate.

### Why the storage/voiding split earns its keep

Obstruction produces voiding symptoms directly but storage symptoms *indirectly*: chronic outflow resistance causes detrusor hypertrophy and secondary overactivity. Hence the common finals trap — a man with BPH whose dominant complaint is urgency and frequency. Treating him purely as "overactive bladder" risks precipitating retention; treating him purely as obstruction may not fix the bother he presented with. Establish whether he empties (PVR) before choosing. Conversely, storage symptoms with normal flow, no residual and a small prostate are probably not prostatic — idiopathic detrusor overactivity, UTI, bladder stone, bladder pain syndrome.

### Separating the causes on history alone

| Pattern | Discriminating features on history |
|---|---|
| **BPH / outflow obstruction** | Gradual onset over years; voiding-predominant (hesitancy, weak stream, straining, terminal dribble, double voiding); nocturia with *normal-sized* voids; no haematuria or weight loss |
| **Overactive bladder** | Storage-predominant; **urgency is the defining symptom** — sudden, difficult to defer; small-volume frequency; urge incontinence; often triggered (key in the door, running water); stream normal |
| **Prostate cancer** | Usually **asymptomatic** when curable — LUTS is a poor screening symptom. Suspicious: new bone pain, weight loss, haematuria/haematospermia, rapid progression, hard/nodular prostate, renal failure from bilateral ureteric involvement |
| **Neurological cause** | Abrupt onset; leg weakness/numbness, saddle sensory change, faecal incontinence, new ED; back pain; known MS, Parkinson disease, diabetes, prior spinal surgery; **reduced sensation of bladder filling** |
| **Polyuria (systemic)** | Large-volume voids day *and* night; thirst; the volume, not the bladder, is abnormal |
| **UTI / stone / CIS** | Rapid onset, dysuria, haematuria, suprapubic pain, sterile pyuria (CIS, TB) |
| **Urethral stricture** | Younger man; prior instrumentation, catheterisation, trauma or urethritis; spraying stream; poor alpha-blocker response |

### Polyuria: the causes to name

- **Diabetes mellitus** — osmotic diuresis; polyuria + polydipsia + weight loss; check BGL/HbA1c.
- **Diuretics** — especially a loop taken in the afternoon; a timing problem masquerading as a bladder problem.
- **Obstructive sleep apnoea** — nocturnal hypoxia and raised intrathoracic pressure raise ANP, causing genuine nocturnal polyuria. Ask about snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence, obesity; screen with STOP-Bang.
- **Hypercalcaemia** — nephrogenic DI; polyuria, constipation, confusion, bone pain, stones.
- **Diabetes insipidus** (cranial or nephrogenic, incl. lithium), **primary polydipsia**, **hypokalaemia**.
- Peripheral oedema of any cause (heart failure, venous insufficiency, dihydropyridines): daytime third-spaced fluid is mobilised when supine.

### ⚑ The exam stem: "an older man waking several times a night to pass urine"

Nocturia alone does not equal BPH; a stem giving *only* nocturia tests whether you generate a differential rather than pattern-match to prostate. Reason in four steps:

1. **Is total 24-h output high?** >40 mL/kg/24 h = global polyuria → diabetes mellitus, DI, primary polydipsia, hypercalcaemia. These men are polyuric by day as well.
2. **Is output normal but nocturnally weighted?** Nocturnal polyuria index = night volume ÷ 24-h volume; **>33% in older adults (>20% in the young)** = nocturnal polyuria → OSA, heart failure, peripheral oedema, evening diuretic, evening fluid/alcohol/caffeine.
3. **Is nocturnal volume normal but split into small voids?** Reduced functional capacity → detrusor overactivity, BPH with significant residual, bladder stone/tumour, chronic UTI.
4. **Is he waking for another reason and voiding opportunistically?** Insomnia, pain, OSA arousals, restless legs — nocturia is a *consequence* here.

The highest-yield investigation is a **3-day frequency–volume (bladder) diary** with fluid intake — it separates all four groups and costs nothing. Add PVR, urinalysis and renal function. Only then does the prostate belong in the answer.

## 2.2 Assessment

**The short version**
- Everyone: abdominal exam (palpable bladder), meatus/genitalia, **DRE**, and a focused neurological exam if any red flag.
- Everyone: **urinalysis** ± MSU, **renal function**, **PVR**, plus a **frequency–volume diary** if storage- or nocturia-predominant.
- PSA only after discussing consequences; ⚑ **defer ~6 weeks after UTI/prostatitis, instrumentation or catheterisation**, and avoid immediately post-ejaculation. Vigorous massage raises it; gentle DRE has minimal effect — conventionally take blood first anyway.
- Image the upper tracts for renal impairment, large residual, haematuria, recurrent UTI, stones or suspected malignancy — not routinely.
- Refer urgently: retention with renal failure, any visible haematuria, suspicious DRE, any neurological red flag.

### Examination and DRE

Palpate for a distended bladder (dull, arising from the pelvis, cannot get below it). Inspect the meatus for stenosis and the foreskin for phimosis. DRE assesses anal tone, faecal loading (a reversible cause of retention) and the prostate.

| DRE finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Smooth, symmetrical, rubbery, enlarged, sulcus preserved | Benign hyperplasia |
| Hard, craggy, nodular, asymmetric, sulcus lost, fixed | Suspicious for carcinoma — refer regardless of PSA |
| Exquisitely tender, boggy, warm, systemically unwell | Acute prostatitis — ⚑ **do not massage** (bacteraemia); treat and defer PSA |
| Absent/reduced anal tone, loss of perianal sensation | Cauda equina/cord lesion until proven otherwise |

⚑ DRE size correlates poorly with symptom severity and only crudely with true volume (it underestimates). Size matters for *drug choice* (5-ARI benefit) more than for diagnosis.

### Investigations

| Test | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Urinalysis ± MSU | Infection, haematuria, glycosuria | Highest yield per dollar; glycosuria reframes the consultation |
| UEC/eGFR | Obstructive nephropathy | Impaired + LUTS → image tracts, consider high-pressure chronic retention |
| PSA | Prostate cancer risk; also predicts BPH progression | See caveats below |
| PVR (bladder scan) | Emptying failure | Measure **within ~10 min of voiding**; repeat before acting on one high value |
| 3-day frequency–volume diary | Polyuria vs nocturnal polyuria vs small capacity | Often diagnostic on its own |
| Uroflowmetry | Objective flow; needs voided volume ≥150 mL | Cannot distinguish obstruction from a weak detrusor |
| Renal tract ultrasound | Hydronephrosis, residual, stones, bladder wall | For renal impairment, large residual, haematuria, recurrent UTI, stones |
| Urodynamics | Obstruction vs detrusor underactivity | Specialist; pre-surgery if ambiguous or neurogenic |
| Cystoscopy ± CT urography | Haematuria, stricture, stone, tumour | All **visible** haematuria warrants urology referral |

**PVR thresholds** — no universal cut-off. Broadly: <100 mL normal, 100–200 mL equivocal (repeat), >200–300 mL clinically significant, persistently >300–400 mL is retention warranting drainage and specialist input. Interpret against voided volume: 150 mL residual after a 200 mL void is far worse than after an 800 mL void.

**Uroflowmetry** — Qmax below roughly 10 mL/s in an adequate void suggests impaired emptying; above ~15 mL/s makes obstruction less likely. A weak detrusor gives an identical trace, so a low Qmax alone should not commit a man to surgery.

**PSA caveats.** PSA rises with prostate volume, age, UTI/prostatitis, retention, catheterisation or cystoscopy, biopsy, recent ejaculation and prolonged cycling; it falls with 5-ARIs. ⚑ **Australian practice (RACGP Red Book 2024): offer the *opportunity to discuss* the benefits and harms — the Red Book does not recommend offering the test itself, and a PSA should not be ordered without informed consent to screening. Where the man consents, 2-yearly from age 50 (40–45 with significant family history) after informed discussion; ≥3.0 µg/L is repeated in 1–3 months and, if confirmed, referred for urological assessment with mpMRI before biopsy.** ⚑ **The 2026 Australian update supersedes this for the paper — see §1.5 for the risk-stratified table (offer from 45 in higher-risk men, threshold ≥1.0 µg/L at 45–49).** It adds risk-stratified thresholds (lower for higher-risk men, higher in men ≥70) and **conditionally recommends against routine DRE as a PSA adjunct in primary care** — DRE stays part of specialist assessment and is still mandatory when symptoms are suspicious.

**Urgent referral triggers:** retention with renal impairment or bilateral hydronephrosis; any visible haematuria; hard/nodular prostate; suspected cauda equina or cord compression; urosepsis; failed trial of void; symptomatic bladder stones.

## 2.3 BPH management

**The short version**
- Mild symptoms, low bother → **watchful waiting plus behavioural change**. A legitimate exam answer, not a cop-out.
- **Alpha-blocker** = fastest relief (days to weeks), independent of prostate size. Tamsulosin is uroselective; prazosin needs titration and causes first-dose hypotension.
- ⚑ **Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome** — ask about planned cataract surgery *before* prescribing tamsulosin, and tell the ophthalmologist if already started. Stopping pre-operatively does not reliably prevent it.
- **5-ARI** shrinks the gland — benefit only in larger prostates (~>30–40 mL), takes **3–6 months**, **halves PSA**, so ⚑ **double the measured PSA after ≥6 months**; any rise on therapy is suspicious.
- Surgery (TURP is the benchmark) for refractory symptoms, or absolute indications: refractory retention, recurrent UTI, recurrent haematuria, bladder stones, obstructive renal impairment.

### Conservative and behavioural

Education and reassurance; fluid moderation with evening restriction; less caffeine and alcohol; timed and double voiding; treat constipation; review drugs (diuretic timing, anticholinergics, opioids, decongestants); bladder training for storage symptoms; weight loss. Many men with mild symptoms stay stable without drugs.

### Pharmacotherapy

| Class | Examples (Australian) | Mechanism | Onset | Key adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha1-blocker, non-selective** | Prazosin (low starting dose, first dose at night, titrate) | Relaxes prostatic and bladder-neck smooth muscle (dynamic component) | Days–2 weeks | • First-dose and postural hypotension, syncope • Dizziness, headache • Titration, multiple daily doses |
| **Alpha1a-selective** | Tamsulosin MR 400 microgram daily; silodosin | As above, prostate-selective | Days–2 weeks | • **IOFS** (a large proportion of tamsulosin users) • Retrograde/absent ejaculation • Dizziness, nasal congestion • Less hypotension than prazosin, but still possible |
| **5-alpha-reductase inhibitor** | Finasteride 5 mg daily (type 2); dutasteride 500 microgram daily (types 1+2) | Blocks testosterone → DHT; gland volume falls ~20–25% | **3–6 months** | • Reduced libido, ED, ejaculate volume • Gynaecomastia • **PSA falls ~50%** • Teratogenic — women of childbearing potential must not handle broken tablets |
| **Combination** | Tamsulosin + dutasteride (fixed-dose, PBS-listed) | Symptom relief plus disease modification | Immediate + delayed | Additive sexual adverse effects; for larger glands with progression risk |
| **Antimuscarinic** | Oxybutynin, solifenacin, darifenacin, tolterodine | Blocks detrusor M3 receptors | 2–4 weeks | • Dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision • **Cognitive impairment/delirium in older men** — oxybutynin worst • ⚑ Add for storage symptoms only if PVR is not elevated |
| **Beta3 agonist** | Mirabegron 25 or 50 mg daily | Detrusor relaxation during storage | 4–8 weeks | • Hypertension — monitor BP, avoid if uncontrolled • Fewer cognitive effects; preferred in the frail elderly |
| **PDE5 inhibitor** | Tadalafil 5 mg daily | Smooth muscle relaxation; treats LUTS and ED together | 2–4 weeks | • Headache, flushing, dyspepsia • **Contraindicated with nitrates**; additive hypotension with alpha-blockers |

**Choosing.** Voiding-predominant, modest gland → alpha-blocker. Large gland (>30–40 mL) or high PSA with progression risk → add a 5-ARI, counselling that benefit takes months. Persistent storage symptoms on an alpha-blocker with a low PVR → add mirabegron or low-dose antimuscarinic, re-check PVR. Concurrent ED → consider tadalafil.

### Surgery

**TURP** remains the reference procedure. Absolute indications: refractory or recurrent retention, obstructive renal impairment, recurrent UTI, recurrent gross haematuria of prostatic origin, bladder calculi. Relative: bothersome symptoms failing medical therapy.

| TURP complication | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Retrograde ejaculation** | Commonest lasting effect, in the majority of men — consent explicitly, especially if fertility matters |
| **TUR syndrome** | ⚑ Dilutional hyponatraemia from absorption of hypotonic **glycine** irrigant through open venous sinuses. Confusion, restlessness, headache, nausea, visual disturbance ("glycine blindness"), bradycardia/hypertension then collapse, seizures. Risk rises with resection >~60 min or large glands. Stop resecting, support, restrict fluid, correct sodium cautiously; hypertonic saline only for severe neurological features. ⚑ **Now uncommon — bipolar TURP and laser techniques use normal saline irrigation**; the classic syndrome is monopolar |
| Bleeding/clot retention | Transfusion or return to theatre; continuous bladder irrigation post-op |
| Infection/urosepsis | Prophylaxis per local protocol |
| Stricture, bladder neck contracture | Late; poor stream returning after initial improvement |
| Incontinence; ED | Uncommon (sphincter injury); ED less predictable but still consent |
| Re-treatment | A proportion need repeat surgery within ~10 years |

**Alternatives.** TUIP for small glands; **HoLEP**/anatomical enucleation for very large glands (size-independent, low re-treatment); photoselective vaporisation for anticoagulated men; open or robotic simple prostatectomy. ⚑ **Ejaculation-preserving minimally invasive options** — prostatic urethral lift (UroLift), water vapour thermal therapy (Rezūm), Aquablation, prostatic artery embolisation — are increasingly available in Australia; name these when a stem stresses a younger man wanting antegrade ejaculation preserved. Durability data are shorter than for TURP.

## 2.4 Acute urinary retention

**The short version**
- Sudden, **painful** inability to void with a palpable tender bladder. Painless distension points to **chronic** retention or a **neurological** lesion.
- Causes: BPH (commonest in men), constipation, UTI/prostatitis, clot, stricture, post-operative, and **drugs** — anticholinergics, opioids, alpha-agonist decongestants, TCAs, antipsychotics, antihistamines.
- Manage: immediate catheterisation with **documented drained volume**, treat the precipitant, check UEC, start an alpha-blocker, plan a trial of void.
- ⚑ **Rapid complete drainage is appropriate** — routine clamping to decompress slowly is no longer recommended; haematuria ex vacuo and hypotension are uncommon and self-limiting.
- Watch for **post-obstructive diuresis** after large-volume drainage.

### Clinical picture and causes

Acute retention is agonising: restless patient, palpable tender bladder, instant relief on drainage. Precipitants worth naming: recent surgery (especially spinal/pelvic anaesthesia), a new opioid or anticholinergic, an OTC decongestant, alcohol binge, constipation, immobility, UTI or prostatitis, sacral zoster.

**Painless retention** should change your answer. It means either (a) chronic retention, where the bladder has slowly accommodated large volumes, or (b) an afferent neurological lesion so distension is not felt — cauda equina, cord compression, MS, diabetic cystopathy. Painless retention plus back pain, saddle numbness or leg weakness is cauda equina until MRI says otherwise.

### Immediate management

1. Analgesia; confirm distension clinically ± bladder scan.
2. **Catheterise** (urethral; suprapubic if urethral access fails or is contraindicated) and **record the drained volume** — that number drives everything else.
3. Urinalysis/MSU, UEC; defer PSA (retention itself raises it).
4. Treat the precipitant: relieve constipation, stop the offending drug, treat infection.
5. Start an **alpha-blocker** at least 24–48 h before catheter removal — it improves trial-of-void success.
6. Renal tract ultrasound if renal function is impaired, the drained volume was large, or chronic retention is suspected.

### Post-obstructive diuresis (POD)

Defined as urine output **≥200 mL/h for two consecutive hours**, or **>3 L in 24 h**, after relief of obstruction. Mechanism: accumulated urea/sodium osmotic load plus acquired tubular resistance to ADH and aldosterone. Risk rises with drained volumes over roughly 1–1.5 L, prolonged obstruction, older age and renal impairment.

Management: admit rather than discharge; hourly urine output, daily weights, orthostatic BP; electrolytes (Na, K, Mg, PO4) and creatinine **4–6 hourly while the output is brisk**, relaxing to 12-hourly as it settles. Most POD is *physiological* and self-limits within 24 h — over-replacing fluid perpetuates it. When replacement is needed give **roughly 50–75% of the preceding period's losses** as crystalloid, allowing a deliberate mild negative balance, and encourage oral intake. Diuresis beyond ~48 h with dilute urine and high urinary sodium is pathological — involve renal.

### Trial of void (TWOC) and chronic retention

Catheter removal is usually attempted after a few days on an alpha-blocker; success is likelier with smaller drained volumes, younger age and a reversible precipitant. Failure means re-catheterisation and urological referral (surgery, or long-term catheter/clean intermittent self-catheterisation).

⚑ **High-pressure chronic retention** is the entity not to miss: painless, large-volume (often >800–1000 mL) retention with **bilateral hydronephrosis and renal impairment**, classically with **nocturnal enuresis** in an older man ("the man who wets the bed"). Intravesical pressure stays high throughout the cycle and is transmitted to the upper tracts. Catheterise, monitor closely for POD (high risk), correct electrolytes, refer — renal function usually improves substantially after drainage. Low-pressure chronic retention has a compliant bladder, normal upper tracts and normal creatinine, and is far less urgent.

## 2.5 Urinary incontinence

**The short version**
- Four types: **stress** (leak on cough/exertion), **urge** (leak preceded by urgency), **overflow** (dribbling with high PVR), **functional** (bladder works; mobility/cognition/access do not). Mixed is common.
- Assess all: history and diary, urinalysis, **PVR**, cough stress test, pelvic/DRE examination, medication review.
- **Supervised pelvic floor muscle training for ≥3 months is first-line for stress incontinence** in women and post-prostatectomy in men.
- Urge: bladder training, then antimuscarinic or mirabegron, then botulinum toxin or sacral neuromodulation.
- ⚑ Exclude overflow before starting an antimuscarinic — it will make it worse.

**Stress** — sphincter/pelvic floor weakness. Risk factors: parity and vaginal delivery, obesity, chronic cough, menopause; in men, radical prostatectomy. Management: weight loss, treat cough/constipation, supervised pelvic floor training, continence pessary; topical vaginal oestrogen for atrophy. Surgery: **synthetic mid-urethral sling**, autologous fascial sling, colposuspension, urethral bulking; male sling or artificial urinary sphincter post-prostatectomy. ⚑ **Australian divergence:** after the TGA's 2017 review, transvaginal mesh for *prolapse* and mini-slings were cancelled from the ARTG, while **mid-urethral slings for stress incontinence remain available** under tightened conditions, mandated informed consent and credentialling — expect a counselling stem, not "mesh is banned". Duloxetine is not TGA-approved for this indication.

**Urge** — detrusor overactivity. Bladder training, less caffeine, timed voiding, weight loss; then an antimuscarinic (solifenacin, darifenacin — more M3-selective, less cognitively burdensome than oxybutynin) or **mirabegron**, preferred in older patients. Third-line: intradetrusor botulinum toxin (repeat ~6–9 monthly; counsel about needing self-catheterisation), sacral neuromodulation, posterior tibial nerve stimulation.

**Overflow** — chronic retention from obstruction or an underactive detrusor (diabetes, drugs, neurological disease). Relieve obstruction and drain, usually with **clean intermittent self-catheterisation**; never an antimuscarinic.

**Functional** — the answer for a cognitively impaired or immobile inpatient. Fix the environment: bedside commode, accessible toilet, lighting, prompted voiding, clothing, review sedatives and diuretics. Indwelling catheters are not a treatment for incontinence.

## 2.6 Neurogenic bladder — and the must-not-miss

**The short version**
- Lesion site predicts bladder behaviour. **Suprapontine** (stroke, Parkinson, dementia): overactive detrusor, coordinated sphincter → urgency, urge incontinence. **Suprasacral cord** (trauma, MS): overactive detrusor **with detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia** → high pressures, hydronephrosis, renal failure. **Sacral/infrasacral** (cauda equina, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, pelvic surgery): acontractile bladder → retention and overflow.
- Suprasacral lesions threaten the kidneys — surveillance plus pressure-lowering management (antimuscarinic/botulinum toxin with CISC).
- **Cauda equina and cord compression are the emergencies.** Retention with back pain is a neurosurgical problem until proven otherwise.

**Cauda equina red flags:** new bilateral or progressive leg neurology, saddle/perineal sensory loss, painless retention or loss of the sensation of bladder filling, urinary or faecal incontinence, reduced anal tone, new erectile dysfunction, severe back pain. ⚑ Retention is a *late* sign — waiting for it before imaging is the classic error, and a normal PVR does not exclude early cauda equina. Action: immediate senior review, urgent **whole-spine MRI**, neurosurgical/spinal referral. If metastatic cord compression, add high-dose corticosteroid per protocol and involve radiation oncology.

Cord injury also produces **spinal shock** acutely: an areflexic acontractile bladder with retention for weeks to months before detrusor overactivity emerges — catheterise, assess urodynamically later. **Autonomic dysreflexia** (severe hypertension, headache, flushing above and pallor below a lesion at or above T6) is usually triggered by a blocked catheter or distended bladder or rectum; sit the patient up and relieve the trigger.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Total IPSS 8–19 | Moderate symptoms |
| Nocturnal urine >33% of 24-h volume in an older adult | Nocturnal polyuria |
| 24-h urine >40 mL/kg | Global polyuria — think diabetes, DI, primary polydipsia, hypercalcaemia |
| BPH drug that must be flagged before cataract surgery | Tamsulosin (intraoperative floppy iris syndrome) |
| Man on finasteride, PSA 2.4 µg/L at 12 months | True value ≈4.8 — double it, then act |
| Time to benefit: 5-ARI vs alpha-blocker | 3–6 months vs days to ~2 weeks |
| Confusion, hyponatraemia and visual disturbance during prostate resection | TUR syndrome (glycine absorption, monopolar TURP) |
| Commonest lasting adverse effect of TURP | Retrograde ejaculation |
| Painless retention + back pain + saddle numbness | Cauda equina syndrome — urgent whole-spine MRI |
| Urine output ≥200 mL/h for 2 consecutive hours after catheterisation | Post-obstructive diuresis; replace ≤75% of the previous hour's output |
| Older man, nocturnal enuresis, creatinine 240, bilateral hydronephrosis | High-pressure chronic retention |
| Storage symptoms with PVR 400 mL | Do not give an antimuscarinic — this is overflow |
| Australian PSA action threshold in a usual-risk man aged 50–69 | ≥3.0 µg/L — repeat in 1–3 months, then refer with mpMRI |
| Recent UTI or catheterisation before a planned PSA | Defer ~6 weeks |
| Wet only on coughing, sneezing and lifting | Stress incontinence — 3 months supervised pelvic floor training first |
| Cord lesion at T6, sudden headache and severe hypertension | Autonomic dysreflexia — check for blocked catheter/full bladder |
| Preferred OAB drug in a frail patient with memory concerns | Mirabegron (monitor BP) |
