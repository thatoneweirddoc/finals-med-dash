# ENT Notes — Section 3: The Nose and Paranasal Sinuses
*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic) and Australian Prescriber 2023; ASCIA Allergic Rhinitis Clinical Update 2024; RACGP "An update on epistaxis" 2015; SA Health ENT Clinical Prioritisation Criteria; RCH Melbourne CPG Periorbital/Orbital Cellulitis; EPOS 2020; TGA montelukast advisory. Scope: written-finals rhinology — anatomy, epistaxis, acute and chronic rhinosinusitis and complications, allergic rhinitis, nasal obstruction and trauma, sinonasal and nasopharyngeal malignancy, anosmia.*

## 3.1 Applied anatomy

**The short version**
- Anterior septum carries a confluence of vessels (**Little's area / Kiesselbach's plexus**) — site of >90% of nosebleeds.
- Posterior bleeding is **sphenopalatine artery** territory (terminal maxillary a., ECA), entering behind the middle turbinate.
- Dual carotid supply: ECA (sphenopalatine, greater palatine, superior labial) + ICA (ethmoidals via ophthalmic a.) — hence ECA embolisation cannot address an ethmoidal bleed.
- **Ostiomeatal complex (OMC)** in the middle meatus is the common drainage path for frontal, maxillary and anterior ethmoid sinuses — block it and all three obstruct.
- **Olfactory cleft** sits high between superior septum and middle turbinate; obstructive anosmia follows polyp or oedema there.

**Little's area.** Anteroinferior septal mucosa: thin, adherent to cartilage, dried by airflow, reachable by a finger. Contributors: anterior ethmoidal (ophthalmic, i.e. **ICA**), sphenopalatine septal and greater palatine branches (maxillary/**ECA**), superior labial (facial/ECA). The RACGP review attributes ~80% of nasal vault supply to the sphenopalatine and greater palatine arteries.

**Posterior supply.** The sphenopalatine artery emerges at the posterior end of the middle turbinate. Bleeding is brisk, poorly visualised with a headlight, and drains into the pharynx — the target of **endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation**, controlling ~98% of posterior epistaxis.

**Ostiomeatal complex.** Maxillary ostium, ethmoidal infundibulum, uncinate process, ethmoid bulla, hiatus semilunaris. Mucociliary clearance beats *towards the natural ostium* regardless of gravity — an inferior accessory ostium does not drain the maxillary sinus. Obstruction → stasis, falling intrasinus O₂, ciliary dysfunction → bacterial colonisation; hence the rationale for FESS (restore ostial patency, don't strip mucosa). ⚑ Posterior ethmoid and sphenoid drain **superiorly** (superior meatus, sphenoethmoidal recess) and are *not* OMC — sphenoid disease can present with a normal middle meatus and isolated vertex/retro-orbital headache.

**Olfactory cleft.** Fibres pierce the cribriform plate — fracture or FESS injury risks CSF rhinorrhoea and meningitis. Conductive loss (polyps, CRS) often responds to steroid; sensorineural loss (post-viral, head injury) does not.

## 3.2 Epistaxis

**The short version**
- >90% anterior, from Little's area; posterior bleeds affect older patients, are brisker, look bilateral and run down the throat.
- Hypertension is **rarely the direct cause** — treat the bleed, don't blame the BP.
- Anticoagulants/antiplatelets raise severity and recurrence, not usually causation.
- **Unilateral epistaxis + progressive unilateral obstruction in an adolescent male = JNA until excluded — image, refer, NEVER biopsy.**
- First aid: sit up, lean forward, pinch the **soft cartilaginous** part continuously ≥10 min.

### Anterior vs posterior

| Feature | Anterior | Posterior |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | >90% | ~10% |
| Typical patient | • Children, young adults • Digital trauma, dry air | • Older, arteriopathic • Anticoagulant/antiplatelet |
| Source | Little's area / Kiesselbach's | Sphenopalatine branches, posterolateral wall |
| Presentation | • Unilateral drip • Visible bleeding point | • Blood from both nares and down the oropharynx • Haematemesis, melaena |
| Control | Pressure ± cautery | Fails first aid; needs posterior tamponade, SPAL or embolisation |
| Risk | Nuisance | Aspiration, hypovolaemia, admission |

### Causes
- **Local**: digital trauma (commonest in children), nose blowing, dry/heated or air-conditioned air, fracture, foreign body, steroid spray aimed at the septum, cocaine.
- **Mucosal/structural**: septal deviation with turbulent airflow over the spur; **septal perforation** (margins crust and rebleed — prior cautery/surgery, cocaine, trauma, GPA); rhinitis, rhinosinusitis.
- **Systemic**: anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, thrombocytopenia, liver disease, haemophilia/von Willebrand, **hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia** (recurrent bleeds, lip/tongue/finger telangiectasia, family history, iron deficiency).
- **Neoplastic**: JNA, sinonasal SCC, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, inverted papilloma.
- ⚑ **Hypertension**: associated with more severe bleeds and underlying vasculopathy but "rarely the direct cause". Don't withhold haemostasis to chase a BP number — acute readings reflect anxiety and pain.

### The must-not-miss: juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA)
Benign, locally destructive, highly vascular tumour arising near the sphenopalatine foramen/pterygopalatine fossa, almost exclusively in **adolescent males**. Triad: recurrent **unilateral** epistaxis, progressive **unilateral obstruction**, nasopharyngeal mass. Later: facial swelling, proptosis, cranial neuropathy.
- **Do not biopsy** — catastrophic haemorrhage. Diagnosis is radiological (contrast CT/MRI: avidly enhancing mass, widened sphenopalatine foramen/pterygopalatine fossa).
- Preoperative embolisation then endoscopic or open excision, tertiary centre.
- ⚑ SA Health prioritisation lists suspected JNA as **Category 2 (≤90 days)** and suspected tumour on imaging as **Category 1 (≤30 days)** — but active heavy bleeding is an ED problem.

### Stepwise management
1. **ABC and resuscitate** if brisk — IV access, FBC, group and hold, coagulation profile if anticoagulated or bleeding heavily.
2. **First aid** — sit upright, lean **forwards** (reduces aspiration), pinch the **cartilaginous** part (not the bony bridge — the RACGP review specifically criticises pinching over the nasal bones), continuously **≥10 minutes** without releasing to check. Spit blood out; ice is comfort only.
3. **Topical vasoconstrictor ± anaesthetic** — co-phenylcaine (lignocaine 5% + phenylephrine 0.5%) or oxymetazoline pledget, then re-examine with light and suction; clot must be cleared to see the point.
4. **Chemical cautery** — silver nitrate to the vessel and immediately around it, seconds only, one side. ⚑ Avoid **bilateral septal cautery at one sitting** (perforation risk). Emollient ointment after; avoid picking, hot showers, heavy lifting.
5. **Anterior packing** — tampon, inflatable pack (may stay ~3–4 days) or ribbon gauze, along the **floor** of the nose directed posteriorly, not upwards. Consider antibiotic cover if prolonged.
6. **Posterior packing / balloon** — Foley or posterior balloon plus anterior pack; needs **admission with monitoring** (vagal response, hypoxia, pressure necrosis) and ENT.
7. **Surgical/radiological** — endoscopic **SPA ligation** (~98% control, increasingly preferred over prolonged packing), anterior ethmoidal ligation for high bleeds, or **embolisation** (stroke/blindness risk up to ~4%).

⚑ **Tranexamic acid**: the **NoPAC** RCT found topical TXA did **not** reduce the need for anterior packing versus placebo (44% vs 41%). Don't cite it as a guideline-backed step; systemic TXA retains a role in coagulopathy/HHT on specialist advice.

### The anticoagulated patient
- Establish drug, indication, last dose, thrombotic risk (mechanical valve, recent VTE/stent) — reversal is a trade-off, not a reflex.
- **Warfarin**: check INR; mechanical measures first. **Vitamin K 5–10 mg IV + 4-factor PCC (Beriplex) 50 IU/kg** (capped at 100 kg; 25–50 IU/kg for lesser bleeding) only for life-threatening bleeding or markedly raised INR — ⚑ **no FFP**. **What changed:** the **3-factor Prothrombinex-VF** was discontinued around **June 2024** and replaced by Beriplex (4-factor), and FFP — which existed only to supply the missing factor VII — is no longer required; most texts still print "Prothrombinex + FFP", so recognise that as the **old** answer. Full treatment: **Critical Care §3.14**.
- **DOACs**: last dose and renal function. **Idarucizumab** (dabigatran) for life-threatening haemorrhage — unchanged. ⚑ For **factor Xa inhibitors**, Australian reversal is now **PCC-based (Beriplex)**: **andexanet alfa was removed from the ARTG on 20 May 2026**, so the older "andexanet first" answer is superseded. See **Critical Care §3.14**.
- **Antiplatelets**: no useful reversal; platelets only for critical bleeding.
- ⚑ Most epistaxis on anticoagulants is controlled by pressure, cautery or packing **without** reversal — treat the nose, don't reflexively stop the drug.

## 3.3 Acute rhinosinusitis (ARS)

**The short version**
- ≥2 symptoms, one being **nasal blockage or discharge**, ± facial pain/pressure, ± reduced smell, for <12 weeks.
- Overwhelmingly **viral**; Therapeutic Guidelines: antibiotics "required rarely".
- Bacterial suggested by ≥3 of: fever >38°C, **double sickening**, unilateral disease, severe pain, raised ESR/CRP (EPOS 2020).
- Symptomatic care is the answer: analgesia, saline irrigation, intranasal corticosteroid; topical decongestant **3–5 days max**.
- When antibiotics are justified: **amoxicillin 500 mg orally 8-hourly for 5 days** (adult, Australian).

**Viral vs bacterial.** No single feature separates them — purulence and fever alone do **not** mean bacterial infection, and green mucus is not an antibiotic indication (it reflects neutrophil myeloperoxidase). ⚑ The commonest exam trap here. Peaking then improving by day 5–7 = viral; persisting **beyond ~10 days without improvement**, **worsening after initial improvement (double sickening)**, or severe from onset with high fever and unilateral facial pain = consider bacterial.

**Symptomatic management**
| Measure | Comment |
|---|---|
| Analgesia | Paracetamol ± NSAID — most useful single intervention for facial pain |
| Saline irrigation | Low risk; evidence strongest in children |
| Intranasal corticosteroid | Small effect in post-viral ARS (EPOS 2020) — reasonable, not mandatory |
| Topical decongestant | Symptom relief only; ⚑ **3–5 days** max — beyond this, **rhinitis medicamentosa** (rebound congestion, receptor downregulation) |
| Oral decongestant, antihistamine | Little benefit unless coexisting AR; pseudoephedrine has cardiovascular and misuse concerns |
| Steam, mucolytics | Comfort only |

**When antibiotics are indicated (Australia).** Reserve for symptoms persisting **beyond 10 days** without improvement, high fever >3 days, biphasic illness, severe systemic illness, immunocompromise, or suspected complication. First line **amoxicillin 500 mg orally 8-hourly for 5 days** (children by weight); doxycycline is an adult alternative in non-immediate penicillin hypersensitivity (not children or pregnancy). ⚑ Australia does **not** default to amoxicillin–clavulanate as some US guidance does, and 5-day courses match longer ones.

## 3.4 Complications of sinusitis

**The short version**
- Complications track anatomy: through the **lamina papyracea** to the orbit, through frontal bone to Pott's puffy tumour, via valveless veins to cavernous sinus and cranium.
- **Preseptal (periorbital)** cellulitis = lid swelling and erythema with a **normal globe**.
- **Orbital (postseptal)** cellulitis = **proptosis, painful or restricted eye movements, reduced acuity, diplopia, RAPD** — these discriminators are the exam answer.
- Suspected postseptal disease → urgent **contrast CT orbits and sinuses ± brain**, IV antibiotics, same-day ENT and ophthalmology.

**Chandler classification**: I preseptal → II orbital cellulitis → III **subperiosteal abscess** → IV orbital abscess → V **cavernous sinus thrombosis**.

| Complication | Recognition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Preseptal cellulitis | Lid oedema/erythema; movements full, acuity normal, no proptosis | Oral antibiotics, review 24–48 h; admit if young, unwell or eye not assessable |
| Orbital cellulitis | Proptosis, painful/restricted movements, ↓acuity, RAPD, chemosis | Emergency: IV ceftriaxone/cefotaxime (± vancomycin if MRSA), contrast CT, ENT + ophthalmology, surgery if abscess |
| Subperiosteal abscess | Usually medial (ethmoids); globe displaced laterally/inferiorly | Drain if large, non-medial, visual compromise, or IV failure |
| Cavernous sinus thrombosis | **Bilateral** eye signs, III/IV/V₁–V₂/VI palsies, headache, swinging fever, meningism | MRI/MRV; IV antibiotics; anticoagulation on specialist advice; high mortality |
| Pott's puffy tumour | **Doughy forehead swelling** with frontal sinusitis, usually adolescent | CT + MRI (intracranial extension common); prolonged IV antibiotics + drainage |
| Intracranial extension | Meningitis, epidural/subdural empyema, frontal abscess — headache, vomiting, seizures, ↓GCS | MRI; urgent neurosurgery |

⚑ In children, unilateral eye swelling with fever after a URTI is the classic stem. If the child will not open the eye, or acuity and movements cannot be documented, treat as postseptal and image.

## 3.5 Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS)

**The short version**
- Same symptom pair as ARS but **≥12 weeks**, plus objective endoscopic or CT evidence (polyps, middle meatal mucopus/oedema, OMC or sinus mucosal change).
- Phenotype by polyps: **CRSsNP** vs **CRSwNP**; also primary vs secondary (CF, primary ciliary dyskinesia, immunodeficiency, GPA/EGPA).
- Backbone: **long-term intranasal corticosteroid + saline irrigation** — high-quality evidence, larger effect in CRSwNP.
- FESS only after failure of ≥8 weeks of adequate medical therapy with CT change.
- **Samter's triad / AERD** = nasal polyps + asthma + NSAID sensitivity; aggressive recurrent polyps.

**Medical management.** Topical corticosteroid (spray, or added to large-volume irrigation in specialist practice), daily isotonic saline irrigation, and 1–2 short oral corticosteroid courses per year for uncontrolled disease (mainly CRSwNP). Prolonged low-dose macrolide is used by some specialists in CRSsNP with normal IgE. Treat coexisting AR and asthma. Investigate secondary causes if disease is unilateral, paediatric, refractory, or systemic features are present (ANCA, eosinophilia, bronchiectasis).

**Surgery.** FESS follows documented failure of medical therapy — EPOS frames this as ≥8 weeks of adequate treatment plus CT change (Lund–Mackay ≥1) and persistent burden (SNOT-22 ≥20). It restores drainage and improves topical delivery; not curative in eosinophilic disease, and polyps recur without ongoing topical steroid. ⚑ Type 2 **biologics** (dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab) are now established for severe recurrent CRSwNP — older texts offer only revision surgery.

**Samter's triad / AERD.** Polyposis + asthma + hypersensitivity to aspirin and other COX-1 inhibitors, driven by dysregulated arachidonic acid metabolism (cysteinyl leukotriene excess), **not** IgE. Adult onset; polyps recur rapidly post-surgery; asthma often severe. Reaction = bronchospasm and profuse rhinorrhoea within an hour or two. Avoid COX-1 inhibitors (selective COX-2 usually tolerated after specialist assessment); maximal topical therapy, LTRA, surgery, and **aspirin desensitisation** in selected patients.

## 3.6 Allergic rhinitis (AR)

**The short version**
- Classify by timing (seasonal/perennial/occupational) and by ARIA (intermittent vs persistent; mild vs moderate–severe).
- Signs: pale boggy turbinates, clear rhinorrhoea, allergic shiners, transverse nasal crease, conjunctival injection.
- **Intranasal corticosteroid is first-line for persistent or moderate–severe disease** (ASCIA wording), and also for antihistamine failure. It is widely taught as the single most effective agent, but ASCIA itself does not make that superiority claim.
- Technique: **contralateral hand, aimed away from the septum**, no hard sniffing.
- "One airway": AR and asthma are one inflammatory process at two levels — always ask about wheeze.

**Classification.** Intermittent = <4 days/week **or** <4 weeks; persistent = ≥4 days/week **and** ≥4 weeks. Moderate–severe = disturbed sleep or impaired daily activity/school/work.

**Management ladder (ASCIA)**
| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| Allergen minimisation | Dust mite measures, pet exclusion, pollen strategies — modest alone |
| Saline | Safe adjunct, especially children and pregnancy |
| **Intranasal corticosteroid** | First line for persistent/moderate–severe; days to full effect, so **regular use, not PRN**; irritation and epistaxis are the main adverse effects |
| Non-sedating oral antihistamine | Mild intermittent symptoms or add-on. ⚑ **Sedating antihistamines discouraged** — poor sleep quality, next-day impairment, driving risk, paradoxical effects in children |
| Intranasal antihistamine | Onset ~30 min — useful as rescue |
| Combination INCS + antihistamine spray | Better than either alone; moderate–severe or poorly controlled disease |
| Montelukast (LTRA) | Second line; no added benefit over INCS alone. ⚑ **Boxed warning for serious neuropsychiatric effects** — this is an **FDA** action (2020); the TGA has issued safety advice rather than a boxed warning, so do not attribute a "TGA boxed warning" in an answer (mood change, sleep disturbance, suicidal ideation) |
| Decongestants | Topical ≤3–5 days only — rhinitis medicamentosa |
| Systemic corticosteroid | Short course, rarely. ⚑ Depot IM corticosteroid for hay fever is **no longer acceptable practice** in Australia |
| Immunotherapy (SCIT/SLIT) | Uncontrolled despite optimal pharmacotherapy, intolerable side effects, wish to reduce medication, unavoidable or occupational allergen. ~50% symptom/medication reduction; disease-modifying over 3–5 years |

**Spray technique** — worth a mark: shake, clear the nose, head slightly forward, insert just inside the nostril with the **opposite hand** so the tip points **laterally towards the same-side eye, away from the septum**, spray and breathe gently in — no hard sniffing. Reduces epistaxis and perforation, improves deposition.

**One airway.** 50–80% of people with asthma have AR; treating the nose improves asthma control. Conversely, poorly controlled asthma with nasal polyps should raise AERD.

## 3.7 Nasal obstruction and structural causes

**The short version**
- Bilateral, alternating, worse supine = mucosal/turbinate/rhinitic. Fixed unilateral = structural or sinister.
- ⚑ **Persistent unilateral obstruction in an adult — especially with bloody or foul discharge, facial numbness or epiphora — is a red flag for sinonasal neoplasm; nasoendoscopy required.**
- Child with **unilateral offensive purulent discharge** = nasal foreign body until proven otherwise.
- **Septal haematoma** after trauma needs urgent drainage — cartilage necrosis → saddle nose.

| Cause | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Septal deviation | Fixed unilateral obstruction, often post-traumatic | Septoplasty if symptomatic after medical therapy |
| Turbinate hypertrophy | Bilateral, fluctuating, worse supine and with rhinitis | INCS first; turbinate reduction if refractory |
| Adenoidal hypertrophy (children) | Mouth breathing, snoring, hyponasal speech, OSA, recurrent otitis media with effusion | Adenoidectomy ± tonsillectomy if OSA or morbidity |
| Nasal polyps | Bilateral, painless, insensate pale masses, hyposmia | INCS, treat as CRSwNP. ⚑ A **unilateral** polyp is not CRSwNP — image and biopsy (inverted papilloma, malignancy); in a child exclude encephalocoele/glioma first |
| Foreign body (child) | Unilateral **foul-smelling purulent/bloody** discharge over days–weeks | Removal; button battery is an emergency (perforation within hours) |
| Choanal atresia | Bilateral: neonatal cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying. Unilateral: later rhinorrhoea | Catheter fails to pass; CT; surgical repair |
| Septal haematoma | Post-trauma obstruction, boggy fluctuant cherry-red septal swelling, pain | **Urgent incision, drainage, quilting + antibiotics** — untreated → cartilage necrosis, septal abscess, **saddle nose** |

## 3.8 Nasal trauma and fracture

**The short version**
- Clinical diagnosis; **plain radiographs are not indicated** for isolated nasal fracture.
- Examine the **septum every time** for haematoma — the time-critical finding.
- Review at ~5–7 days once swelling settles to judge deformity; closed reduction usually within ~10–14 days, before the fracture sets.
- Exclude CSF rhinorrhoea, other facial fractures, orbital and cervical spine injury.

Assess mechanism, epistaxis, obstruction, prior nasal shape (photographs help) and external deviation. Clear rhinorrhoea suggests **CSF leak** (anterior skull base fracture — urgent referral, do not pack blindly); diplopia or infraorbital numbness suggests orbital/zygomatic fracture (image); check occlusion. ⚑ Referral is driven by deformity and the timing window, not imaging; refer children earlier (faster remodelling, growth-centre injury).

## 3.9 Nasopharyngeal carcinoma and sinonasal malignancy

**The short version**
- Markedly higher incidence in **southern Chinese** populations (also Southeast Asian, Inuit); strongly **EBV**-associated (undifferentiated non-keratinising type); male predominance.
- Arises in the **fossa of Rosenmüller**, obstructing the Eustachian tube.
- ⚑ **Unilateral serous otitis media in an adult mandates nasopharyngeal examination** — the classic first sign.
- Painless upper cervical **neck node** is a very common presentation; cranial neuropathies (VI most often) in ~20%.
- Diagnosis: nasoendoscopy + biopsy, MRI for local/skull-base staging, EBV serology/plasma EBV DNA. Treatment: radiotherapy ± concurrent cisplatin.

Other features: blood-stained discharge, unilateral obstruction, headache, hearing loss, aural fullness. Trotter's triad — unilateral conductive deafness, ipsilateral V₃ pain/numbness, palatal immobility. **Sinonasal malignancy** (SCC commonest; adenocarcinoma with hardwood dust; esthesioneuroblastoma; melanoma) presents late with unilateral obstruction, epistaxis, facial pain or numbness, epiphora, proptosis or a palatal mass. Inverted papilloma is benign but locally aggressive with malignant transformation risk.

## 3.10 Anosmia

**The short version**
- Split causes into **conductive** (airflow can't reach the olfactory cleft) and **sensorineural** (epithelium or central pathway).
- Commonest: sinonasal disease (CRSwNP, allergic rhinitis), **post-viral**, and head injury (shearing of fibres at the cribriform plate — often permanent).
- Post-viral anosmia recovers gradually over months in most; olfactory training is the mainstay of therapy.
- ⚑ Image (**MRI**) when loss is **unilateral**, progressive, associated with neurological signs or headache, or when there is no sinonasal or viral explanation.

Also: ageing, smoking, drugs, Parkinson and Alzheimer disease (early hyposmia is characteristic), olfactory groove meningioma, Kallmann syndrome (congenital anosmia + hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism). CT sinuses if inflammatory sinonasal disease; MRI brain/olfactory bulbs if central or neoplastic. A short corticosteroid course is diagnostically useful — improvement implies a reversible conductive component.
