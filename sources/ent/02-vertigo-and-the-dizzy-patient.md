# ENT Notes — Section 2: Vertigo and the Dizzy Patient
*Sources: Bárány Society diagnostic criteria (BPPV 2015, Ménière's 2015, vestibular migraine 2012/2022, PPPD 2017), ICHD-3, Therapeutic Guidelines (Australia), Australian Prescriber/RACGP AJGP dizziness articles, RACGP HANDI (Epley), AAO-HNSF Ménière's CPG 2020, Cochrane reviews (HINTS 2023, Ménière's pharmacotherapy 2023), Stroke Foundation Australia Living Clinical Guidelines. Scope: adult dizziness for written finals — timing/trigger classification, peripheral causes, and the central mimics you cannot afford to miss.*

## 2.1 The approach to the dizzy patient

**The short version**
- "Dizzy" is a symptom word, not a diagnosis. Old teaching split it four ways: **vertigo** (illusion of movement), **presyncope** (about to faint), **disequilibrium** (unsteady on the feet, symptom lives in the legs not the head), **non-specific lightheadedness**.
- ⚑ That symptom-quality approach is now known to perform badly — patients describe the same pathology in different words on the same day, and symptom quality does not reliably separate peripheral from central.
- The examinable replacement is **TiTrATE**: **Ti**ming, **Tr**iggers, **A**nd **T**argeted **E**xamination. Classify into four vestibular syndromes, then apply the exam that belongs to that syndrome.
- Written stems are built out of timing and triggers because those are the discriminating variables. **Duration of a single attack is the single highest-yield question** — it usually narrows the answer to one or two options before you read the exam findings.

**The four syndromes**

| Syndrome | Defining pattern | Typical causes | Targeted exam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triggered episodic vestibular syndrome (t-EVS) | Brief attacks, always provoked by a specific head/body movement or position; asymptomatic between | • BPPV (commonest)<br>• Orthostatic hypotension<br>• SCDS (sound/pressure-triggered) | Dix–Hallpike, supine roll test, lying-to-standing BP |
| Spontaneous episodic vestibular syndrome (s-EVS) | Attacks arise out of the blue, minutes–hours, well between | • Vestibular migraine<br>• Ménière's<br>• Vertebrobasilar TIA<br>• Panic/arrhythmia | Interictal exam usually normal — diagnosis is historical; audiogram, ECG |
| Acute vestibular syndrome (AVS) | Continuous vertigo, nausea, gait unsteadiness and nystagmus for **days**, worse on movement but not caused by it | • Vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis<br>• Posterior circulation stroke<br>• Demyelination, Wernicke's | **HINTS** (+ hearing), gait, full neuro |
| Chronic vestibular syndrome | Months of dizziness/unsteadiness | • PPPD<br>• Vestibular schwannoma<br>• Bilateral vestibular failure<br>• Cerebellar degeneration, polypharmacy | Romberg, gait, dynamic visual acuity, audiogram, MRI |

**Why duration is so discriminating.** Each disorder has a physiologically constrained attack length: otoconia settle within seconds; hydrops distends and equilibrates over tens of minutes to hours; an infarcted or inflamed vestibular nerve gives a fixed tone imbalance resolving only as central compensation occurs over days. Duration maps almost one-to-one onto mechanism.

**Two traps.** (1) *Positional worsening ≠ positional triggering* — every vestibular disorder feels worse on head movement; BPPV differs because between provocations the patient is symptom-free. Constant vertigo "worse on turning the head" is AVS. (2) *Frequency ≠ duration* — "vertigo for two weeks" may be daily 10-second attacks (BPPV) or 14 continuous days (AVS). Ask the length of one attack.

## 2.2 The duration table

**The short version**
- **Seconds (<1 min), positionally triggered** → BPPV.
- **Minutes, spontaneous** → vertebrobasilar TIA; vestibular migraine can also sit here.
- **20 minutes to 12 hours, with aural symptoms** → Ménière's disease.
- **Days, continuous** → vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis, or posterior circulation stroke.
- **Constant / chronic (weeks–months)** → vestibular schwannoma, bilateral vestibular failure, PPPD, cerebellar disease.

| Attack duration | Most likely | Key discriminators in the stem |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds to <1 min | **BPPV** | • Triggered by rolling over, lying down, looking up, bending<br>• Well between attacks<br>• **No hearing loss, no tinnitus, no neurology**<br>• Dix–Hallpike positive |
| Seconds | Orthostatic hypotension; SCDS | • On standing (with BP drop) or on loud noise/straining |
| Minutes (2–30) | **Vertebrobasilar TIA** | • Vascular risk factors, sudden onset<br>• Diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, visual field loss<br>• Isolated vertigo can still be TIA — recurrent stereotyped attacks are a stroke warning |
| Minutes to hours (5 min – 72 h, variable) | **Vestibular migraine** | • Migraine history, photophobia/phonophobia during attack, head-motion intolerance<br>• Duration deliberately variable |
| **20 min – 12 h** | **Ménière's disease** | • Fluctuating low-frequency SNHL, tinnitus, aural fullness, unilateral |
| Days (24 h – weeks, continuous) | **Vestibular neuritis** (no hearing loss) / **labyrinthitis** (hearing loss) | • Preceding viral illness<br>• Unidirectional horizontal nystagmus<br>• **Abnormal** head impulse to the affected side |
| Days (continuous) | **Posterior circulation stroke** | • **Normal** head impulse, direction-changing or vertical nystagmus, skew<br>• Cannot walk unaided; other posterior fossa signs |
| Constant / chronic | Vestibular schwannoma; bilateral vestibular failure (aminoglycoside); PPPD; cerebellar degeneration | • Schwannoma: asymmetric SNHL + mild imbalance<br>• Bilateral failure: oscillopsia when walking, worse in the dark<br>• PPPD: worse upright and in visually busy environments |

**The past-paper stem.** *"Episodes of severe spinning vertigo lasting under a minute, brought on by rolling over in bed."* Sub-minute + positional trigger + otherwise well = **BPPV**, and the next step is **Dix–Hallpike**, not imaging and not a vestibular sedative.

## 2.3 Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

**The short version**
- Otoconia dislodged from the utricular macula float free in a semicircular canal (**canalithiasis**); ~85–90% posterior canal, usually the dependent ear.
- History: brief spinning triggered by **rolling over in bed, lying down, sitting up, looking up, bending forward**; each attack <1 min; residual "off" feeling between; **no** cochlear or neurological symptoms.
- **Dix–Hallpike** is diagnostic for posterior canal; positive = **latency, upbeat-torsional (geotropic) nystagmus, <1 min duration, fatigability**, with matching vertigo.
- Treatment is **canalith repositioning — Epley**. ⚑ **Vestibular sedatives are not the treatment**; they suppress symptoms without moving the otoconia and impede central compensation.
- Horizontal canal variant (~5–15%): triggered by rolling supine; diagnosed on **supine roll test**; treated with **BBQ/Lempert roll**.

**Pathophysiology.** Free otoconia move under gravity when the canal plane is reoriented, dragging endolymph and deflecting the cupula — hence the latency (particles must start moving), the crescendo–decrescendo course, and reversal of nystagmus on sitting up. Less commonly otoconia adhere to the cupula (**cupulolithiasis**), giving nystagmus with little latency that persists while the position is held.

**Dix–Hallpike technique.** Patient seated so the head will overhang. Turn the head 45° toward the test ear, then lie them rapidly supine with the neck extended ~20–30° below horizontal. Eyes open, watch ≥30 s. Positive for **posterior canal BPPV** of the dependent ear:

| Feature | Expected finding |
|---|---|
| Latency | ~1–5 s (up to ~20 s) before nystagmus starts |
| Direction | **Upbeating with torsional component, top pole beating toward the dependent (affected) ear** |
| Duration | Builds then fades, typically <30 s, always <1 min |
| On sitting up | Nystagmus reverses direction, milder |
| Repetition | **Fatigues** on repeated testing |
| Symptoms | Reproduces the patient's vertigo, often with marked nausea |

Cautions: cervical instability or significant neck disease, severe carotid disease, recent neck/spinal surgery — use a side-lying (Semont-type) positional test instead.

**Epley (canalith repositioning).** Sequential 90° rotations moving the otoconia round the posterior canal into the utricle, holding each position until symptoms settle (~30 s, or 1–2 min in HANDI-style protocols): (1) Dix–Hallpike position on the affected side → (2) rotate head 90° to the opposite side, still extended → (3) roll body onto that shoulder, head facing ~45° floorward → (4) sit up slowly, chin tucked. Repeat if still positive. Most are clear after one or two manoeuvres versus roughly half resolving spontaneously over weeks. Post-procedure positional restrictions add little; Brandt-Daroff exercises are second-line self-treatment.

**Red flags against BPPV.** Hearing loss, headache, focal neurology, non-fatiguing downbeat or purely torsional nystagmus, or positional nystagmus without vertigo → central positional vertigo (posterior fossa/cerebellar lesion); image.

## 2.4 Vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis

**The short version**
- AVS: sudden continuous vertigo, nausea/vomiting, gait instability and nystagmus lasting **days**, peaking within hours, then slowly settling over 1–3 weeks with residual motion sensitivity for longer.
- **Neuritis = vestibular nerve only** (no cochlear symptoms). **Labyrinthitis = neuritis + hearing loss ± tinnitus** (cochlea involved).
- Nystagmus is **unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, fast phase beating away from the affected ear, suppressed by visual fixation** and worse with fixation removed (Frenzel lenses/ophthalmoscope).
- Management: symptomatic vestibular sedative for the **first 24–72 hours only**, antiemetic, fluids, then **early vestibular rehabilitation**.
- ⚑ Corticosteroids improve caloric test recovery but evidence for better patient-centred outcomes is weak — not routine.

**Assessment.** Head impulse is **abnormal (corrective saccade) toward the affected ear** — the reassuring finding. Patients can usually walk, veering to the affected side. Sudden SNHL here demands audiometry and urgent ENT input (and consider AICA infarct — see 2.5).

**Pharmacology.**

| Drug class | Example | Role | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antihistamine (H1) | Promethazine, cinnarizine | Acute vertigo/nausea, **≤72 h** | Sedation, anticholinergic effects; impairs central compensation if continued |
| **Dopamine D2 antagonist** ⚑ (not an antihistamine) | Prochlorperazine | Acute vertigo/nausea, **≤72 h** | Sedation; **dystonia and other extrapyramidal effects, especially in the young and the elderly** — this is why its profile differs from promethazine's |
| Antiemetic | Metoclopramide, ondansetron | Vomiting control | Dystonia (metoclopramide) |
| Benzodiazepine | Diazepam | Severe distress, very short term | Dependence; strongly impairs compensation |

⚑ The single most examinable pharmacological point: **prolonged vestibular suppressants delay central compensation and worsen long-term outcome**. Stop them and start exercises.

**Vestibular rehabilitation.** Gaze-stabilisation and habituation exercises as soon as vomiting settles — good evidence, and the definitive treatment for persisting symptoms. No improvement by 4–6 weeks → reassess for a central cause or evolving PPPD.

## 2.5 Ruling out central causes — the high-stakes bit

**The short version**
- Around 3–5% of ED dizziness presentations turn out to be stroke, and dizziness/vertigo features in a large proportion of posterior circulation strokes, and a minority present with **isolated** vertigo — indistinguishable from neuritis without a structured exam.
- **HINTS** applies **only to AVS** (continuous, ongoing vertigo with nystagmus) — never to BPPV, never to a well patient between episodes.
- ⚑ Counter-intuitive but examinable: a **NORMAL head impulse test in AVS points to a CENTRAL lesion**. The peripheral case is the abnormal one.
- Peripheral pattern = **HI abnormal, unidirectional horizontal nystagmus, no skew**. Any deviation from all three → central.
- **CT is inadequate for the posterior fossa**; **MRI with DWI** is required and can be falsely negative in the first 24–48 h, particularly for small brainstem infarcts.

**HINTS**

| Component | Peripheral (reassuring) | Central (worrying) |
|---|---|---|
| **H**ead **I**mpulse | **Abnormal** — catch-up saccade on rapid head thrust toward affected ear (VOR failed) | **Normal** — eyes stay locked on target (VOR intact, so the lesion is above the labyrinth) |
| **N**ystagmus | Unidirectional, horizontal(-torsional), fast phase always the same way, **suppressed by fixation** | **Direction-changing** on gaze to either side, **vertical** or **pure torsional**, not fixation-suppressed |
| **T**est of **S**kew | Absent — no vertical refixation on alternate cover test | **Present** — vertical corrective movement (skew deviation, brainstem) |

**HINTS-plus** adds bedside hearing: **new unilateral hearing loss in AVS is a central red flag**, because AICA territory infarction takes out the labyrinth (via the internal auditory/labyrinthine artery) and the brainstem together. ⚑ Do not assume hearing loss = benign labyrinthitis.

Performance: with trained examiners HINTS is more sensitive for stroke than early MRI-DWI. ⚑ Accuracy falls substantially in non-specialist hands — a "normal HINTS" from an untrained examiner does not exclude stroke.

**Other central red flags (any one → image and admit)**
- Sudden onset, severe headache or **neck pain** (vertebral dissection — young patient, neck trauma/manipulation).
- **Inability to stand or walk unaided** — strongest single bedside discriminator; neuritis patients veer, cerebellar stroke patients fall.
- Other neurology: diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, hemisensory change, limb ataxia, Horner's.
- Vascular risk factors, AF, age >50 with new isolated vertigo.
- Recurrent stereotyped attacks in a vasculopath (vertebrobasilar TIA — a stroke warning).

**Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome — PICA/vertebral territory**

| Structure infarcted | Sign |
|---|---|
| Vestibular nuclei | Vertigo, nystagmus, vomiting |
| Inferior cerebellar peduncle | Ipsilateral limb ataxia, fall toward the lesion |
| Spinal trigeminal nucleus | **Ipsilateral** facial pain/temperature loss |
| Spinothalamic tract | **Contralateral** body pain/temperature loss (crossed sensory pattern) |
| Nucleus ambiguus | Dysphagia, hoarseness, absent gag |
| Descending sympathetics | Ipsilateral **Horner's** (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) |

Note motor power and dorsal column sensation are typically spared. **AICA** infarction gives the same vertigo plus **ipsilateral SNHL and facial weakness** — the "vertigo with deafness" stroke.

**Imaging.** CT detects haemorrhage but misses most acute posterior fossa infarcts (beam-hardening artefact, small lesions). **MRI-DWI** is the investigation of choice; false-negative rates in the first 24–48 h for small brainstem infarcts are appreciable, so a negative early scan with a central exam does not exclude stroke — repeat imaging or admit. Add CTA/MRA if dissection or vertebrobasilar stenosis is suspected. Manage on the standard Australian acute stroke pathway — time-critical reperfusion assessment applies posteriorly too.

## 2.6 Ménière's disease

**The short version**
- Quartet: **episodic vertigo 20 min – 12 h**, **fluctuating low-frequency SNHL**, **tinnitus**, **aural fullness** — unilateral at onset.
- **Definite** MD requires ≥2 spontaneous vertigo episodes of 20 min–12 h **plus audiometrically documented** low-to-mid frequency SNHL in the affected ear before/during/after an episode, plus fluctuating aural symptoms, not better explained otherwise.
- **Probable** MD: ≥2 episodes of vertigo/dizziness lasting **20 min – 24 h** with fluctuating aural symptoms — audiometric documentation not required.
- Pathology: endolymphatic hydrops. Hearing loss starts low-frequency and fluctuating, becomes fixed and pan-frequency over years; vertigo attacks eventually burn out.
- **Tumarkin otolithic crises**: sudden drop attacks without warning or LOC — late feature, major injury/driving risk.

**Documented hearing loss.** The Bárány audiometric definition requires bone-conduction thresholds worse in the affected ear by ≥30 dB at **two contiguous frequencies below 2000 Hz**. Practical point: **you cannot diagnose definite Ménière's without an audiogram** — the correct "next investigation" in a stem.

**Management ladder**

| Step | Intervention | Evidence position |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reduce dietary salt; limit caffeine, alcohol, nicotine; sleep and stress hygiene | Widely recommended, low-quality evidence; harmless and first-line in Australian practice |
| 2 | Vestibular sedative/antiemetic **for the acute attack only** | Symptomatic; not prophylaxis |
| 3 | **Betahistine** | ⚑ Long-standing first-line, but the BEMED RCT found no difference vs placebo in attack rate and Cochrane rates the evidence very low certainty. Still commonly prescribed in Australia; be able to say the evidence is weak |
| 4 | Thiazide ± K-sparing diuretic (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide–amiloride) | Low-certainty evidence; still used |
| 5 | **Intratympanic corticosteroid** | Non-ablative, hearing-sparing; reasonable before ablation |
| 6 | **Intratympanic gentamicin** (chemical labyrinthectomy) | Effective for intractable vertigo; **risk of hearing loss** — not initial therapy |
| 7 | Surgery: endolymphatic sac decompression, vestibular neurectomy, labyrinthectomy | Refractory disease; labyrinthectomy destroys hearing |
| Adjunct | Vestibular rehab, hearing aids, tinnitus support, **driving advice** | Rehab helps interictal imbalance, not the attacks |

Driving: notify the licensing authority per Austroads *Assessing Fitness to Drive* where attacks are unpredictable or Tumarkin crises occur.

## 2.7 Vestibular migraine

**The short version**
- ⚑ Now recognised as one of the **commonest causes of recurrent spontaneous vertigo**, plausibly more common than Ménière's, and consistently under-diagnosed.
- Criteria: ≥5 episodes of moderate/severe vestibular symptoms lasting **5 minutes to 72 hours**, a current or past history of migraine (with or without aura), and **≥50% of episodes accompanied by ≥1 migrainous feature**.
- Migrainous features: migrainous headache (≥2 of unilateral, pulsating, moderate/severe, aggravated by routine activity); **photophobia and phonophobia**; **visual aura**.
- Vertigo need **not** occur with headache — that is precisely why it is missed.

Clues: lifelong motion sickness, head-motion intolerance, visual-motion sensitivity (supermarket aisles), recognised migraine triggers, normal audiogram. Overlap with Ménière's is real; the discriminator is **documented fluctuating low-frequency SNHL** (Ménière's) versus **normal hearing plus migrainous features** (VM). Manage on migraine principles — triggers, acute treatment, prophylaxis (propranolol, amitriptyline, candesartan, topiramate) if frequent, plus vestibular rehabilitation.

## 2.8 Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)

**The short version**
- Benign Schwann cell tumour of the vestibular division of CN VIII, in the internal auditory meatus/cerebellopontine angle.
- **Asymmetric/unilateral progressive SNHL** ± **unilateral tinnitus** with **disproportionately mild imbalance** — slow growth allows central compensation, so true rotatory vertigo is uncommon.
- **Absent/reduced corneal reflex** (CN V) is a classic early CPA sign as the tumour enlarges; later facial numbness, then facial weakness (CN VII is relatively resistant).
- Investigation of choice: **MRI internal auditory meati with gadolinium**. Audiogram first, MRI regardless if asymmetry is unexplained.
- **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas = neurofibromatosis type 2** until proven otherwise.

Any **unexplained asymmetric SNHL, unilateral tinnitus or sudden SNHL** warrants MRI to exclude a schwannoma — a high-yield "next investigation" answer. Large tumours give cerebellar signs, raised ICP and hydrocephalus. Management: serial MRI surveillance for small non-growing tumours, stereotactic radiosurgery, or microsurgery.

## 2.9 Other causes worth a line

| Condition | Recognition cue | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Orthostatic hypotension / drugs** | Lightheaded **on standing**, seconds, resolves on sitting | Drop ≥20 systolic / ≥10 diastolic mmHg within 3 min. Culprits: antihypertensives, α-blockers, nitrates, diuretics, TCAs, antipsychotics, dopaminergics, alcohol. Review meds in every older dizzy patient |
| **Bilateral vestibular failure** | Gentamicin exposure; **oscillopsia** when walking, worse in the dark or on uneven ground | Bilaterally abnormal head impulse; Romberg positive eyes closed. Rehab, not drugs |
| **Cervicogenic dizziness** | Non-spinning unsteadiness with neck pain and restricted range | Diagnosis of exclusion — exclude dissection and central causes first |
| **Perilymph fistula** | Vertigo ± hearing loss after barotrauma, straining, head injury or ear surgery | Symptoms on Valsalva/pressure; conservative, then surgical repair |
| **Superior semicircular canal dehiscence** | ⚑ **Tullio phenomenon** (loud sound → vertigo/oscillopsia), pressure-induced vertigo (Hennebert), **autophony**, hearing own footsteps/eye movements | Low-frequency **conductive** loss on audiogram but **normal tympanometry and intact acoustic reflexes** (a "conductive" loss with a normal middle ear). Diagnose on high-resolution temporal bone CT with reformats; VEMP thresholds low |
| **PPPD** | Non-spinning dizziness/unsteadiness **most days for ≥3 months**, worse **upright**, with **motion**, and in **complex visual environments** | Precipitated by a preceding vestibular/medical/psychological event. Treat with vestibular rehabilitation, SSRI/SNRI and CBT — **not** vestibular sedatives |

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Spinning <1 min triggered by rolling over in bed | BPPV — posterior canal |
| Positive test showing latency, upbeat-torsional nystagmus, fatigable | Dix–Hallpike (posterior canal BPPV) |
| Definitive treatment of posterior canal BPPV | Epley canalith repositioning manoeuvre |
| Drug class that is NOT the treatment for BPPV and impedes compensation | Vestibular sedatives (prochlorperazine, antihistamines, benzodiazepines) |
| Continuous vertigo for days, normal hearing, unidirectional nystagmus, abnormal head impulse | Vestibular neuritis |
| Same picture but with new hearing loss (peripheral) | Labyrinthitis — but exclude AICA infarct |
| Maximum duration of vestibular sedatives in acute vestibular syndrome | 24–72 hours |
| Normal head impulse test in acute vestibular syndrome | Suggests a CENTRAL lesion (stroke) |
| Direction-changing, vertical or pure torsional nystagmus | Central cause |
| Ipsilateral facial pain/temperature loss + contralateral body loss + Horner's + hoarseness | Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome — PICA/vertebral |
| Acute vertigo with ipsilateral deafness and facial weakness | AICA territory infarct |
| Imaging required for suspected posterior circulation stroke | MRI with DWI (CT inadequate; DWI may be falsely negative <48 h) |
| Vertigo 20 min–12 h with fluctuating low-frequency SNHL, tinnitus, aural fullness | Ménière's disease |
| Investigation required to call Ménière's "definite" | Audiogram documenting low-to-mid frequency SNHL |
| Sudden drop attack without warning or loss of consciousness in Ménière's | Tumarkin otolithic crisis |
| Ablative intratympanic drug for intractable Ménière's vertigo (risks hearing) | Gentamicin |
| Recurrent vertigo 5 min–72 h with photophobia/phonophobia and migraine history | Vestibular migraine |
| Unilateral progressive SNHL + tinnitus with minimal imbalance; absent corneal reflex | Vestibular schwannoma — MRI internal auditory meati |
| Bilateral vestibular schwannomas | Neurofibromatosis type 2 |
| Vertigo provoked by loud noise, with autophony and low-frequency conductive loss but intact acoustic reflexes | Superior semicircular canal dehiscence (Tullio phenomenon) |
| Non-spinning dizziness ≥3 months, worse upright and in busy visual environments | PPPD |
| Oscillopsia on walking, worse in the dark, after gentamicin | Bilateral vestibular failure |
