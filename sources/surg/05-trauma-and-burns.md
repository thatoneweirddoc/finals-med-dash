# Surgery Notes — Section 5: Trauma and Burns
*Sources: EMST/ATLS 10th ed (RACS), ANZBA/EMSB and NSW ACI burns guidance, National Blood Authority PBM Guidelines Module 1, CRASH-2 and CRASH-3, Canadian CT Head Rule, Canadian C-Spine Rule, NEXUS, ANZCOR. Scope: written-finals depth on resuscitation of the injured patient — primary and secondary survey, thoracic and haemorrhagic emergencies, head and spine injury, abdominopelvic trauma, burns, compartment syndrome, crush injury.*

## 5.1 The primary survey

**The short version**
- **<C>ABCDE**: catastrophic external haemorrhage first (tourniquet/packing), then Airway with C-spine control, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure.
- **Treat what you find before moving to the next letter** — a tension pneumothorax found at "B" is decompressed there, not after "C".
- **Noisy breathing is obstructed breathing.** Gurgling = liquid → suction. Snoring = tongue → jaw thrust and adjunct. Stridor = laryngeal → definitive airway early.
- Reassess from the top whenever the patient deteriorates.
- Secondary survey (head-to-toe plus **AMPLE**) begins only once the primary survey is complete and the patient is not deteriorating.

**<C> — catastrophic haemorrhage.** Exsanguinating compressible limb bleeding kills faster than an obstructed airway: direct pressure, wound packing, proximal tourniquet tightened until bleeding stops (note the time). ⚑ Tourniquets are standard Australian prehospital equipment; the teaching that they inevitably cost the limb is obsolete.

**A — Airway with C-spine protection.** Ask a question — a clear answer in a normal voice means patent airway, ventilation and cerebral perfusion. Look for blood, vomit, teeth, foreign body. Escalate: suction → jaw thrust (not head tilt) → oro/nasopharyngeal airway → supraglottic device → **definitive airway** by RSI with manual in-line stabilisation; cricothyroidotomy is the failed-airway escape. Indications: apnoea, failure to maintain patency, **GCS ≤8**, aspiration risk, expanding neck haematoma, facial burns/inhalation injury, predicted deterioration in transfer.

> **Exam point.** Gurgling is an **airway** problem, not a breathing problem. The answer is *suction the airway*, then reassess and secure it — before oxygen titration, IV access or imaging. Gurgling means fluid the patient cannot clear: the immediate precursor to aspiration.

**B — Breathing.** Expose the chest: inspect (asymmetry, wounds, flail segment, tracheal shift), palpate (surgical emphysema), percuss (hyper-resonant vs stony dull), auscultate. High-flow O₂, SpO₂, capnography.

**C — Circulation.** Control bleeding; find where it has gone — **"on the floor and four more": chest, abdomen, pelvis/retroperitoneum, long bones**. Two large-bore (14–16G) cannulae, IO if access fails; crossmatch, VBG/lactate, coags, β-hCG. Activate MTP early.

**D — Disability.** GCS, pupils, lateralising signs, **BSL** (hypoglycaemia mimics head injury). **E — Exposure.** Undress fully, log-roll, then **actively rewarm** — hypothermia is one third of the lethal triad and usually iatrogenic.

**Adjuncts:** ECG/monitoring, oximetry, capnography, ABG/VBG, urinary catheter (contraindicated with suspected urethral injury — blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal haematoma), gastric tube (orogastric if base-of-skull fracture suspected), **chest and pelvis radiographs**, **eFAST**.

**Secondary survey.** Head-to-toe: scalp, tympanic membranes, face, neck, chest, abdomen, perineum, PR/PV where indicated, four limbs, log-roll with spinal palpation, full neurological exam. Plus **AMPLE** — **A**llergies, **M**edications (anticoagulants especially), **P**ast history/pregnancy, **L**ast meal, **E**vents. Mechanism predicts pattern: speed, restraint, ejection, fall height, intrusion.

## 5.2 Immediately life-threatening chest injuries

**The short version**
- Six found at "B": **tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, flail chest with pulmonary contusion, cardiac tamponade, tracheobronchial disruption**.
- **Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis — decompress before imaging.** Requesting a CXR here is a wrong answer.
- ⚑ Adult needle decompression site is now the **5th ICS, just anterior to the mid-axillary line**, not 2nd ICS mid-clavicular.
- Massive haemothorax: **>1500 mL immediately** on ICC, or **>200 mL/hr for 2–4 hrs** → thoracotomy.
- Tamponade: hypotension + distended neck veins + muffled heart sounds; FAST confirms, surgery treats.

| Injury | Clinical signature | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| Tension pneumothorax | • Distress, hypoxia<br>• **Absent** breath sounds + **hyper-resonant**<br>• Distended neck veins, hypotension<br>• Tracheal deviation away (late) | Needle decompression → **ICC** |
| Open ("sucking") wound | Audible air movement through defect | Dressing taped on **three sides**, then ICC at a *separate* site |
| Massive haemothorax | • Absent breath sounds + **stony dull**<br>• Shock, **flat** neck veins | **Blood** concurrently with ICC; thoracotomy per volume criteria |
| Flail chest / contusion | ≥2 ribs in ≥2 places, paradoxical segment; hypoxia from the contusion | Analgesia (regional blocks), O₂, careful fluid, ventilatory support |
| Cardiac tamponade | **Beck's triad**; pulsus paradoxus; PEA arrest | FAST subxiphoid → **surgical decompression**; pericardiocentesis only a bridge |

**Tension pneumothorax — the site change.** ⚑ EMST/ATLS 10th ed moved adult needle decompression from 2nd ICS mid-clavicular to the **5th ICS, just anterior to the mid-axillary line** (the safe-triangle position; some sources say 4th–5th, but 5th is the examinable number). The reason is mechanical: a standard 4.5 cm cannula fails to reach pleura anteriorly in a substantial minority of adults, whereas the lateral wall is thinner and is where the ICC is going anyway. Use a **long (≥5 cm, ideally 8 cm) large-bore cannula**. Decompression is temporising and often fails — **definitive treatment is an ICC**; peri-arrest, finger thoracostomy is faster and more reliable. The anterior route remains acceptable if the lateral chest is inaccessible. Paediatrics: 2nd ICS mid-clavicular still standard.

> **Exam point — the talking patient with reduced breath sounds.** That the patient is *talking* is data: air is moving and the brain is perfused, so this is not peri-arrest tension. Discriminate on percussion and haemodynamics:
> - **Hyper-resonant + normal BP + veins not distended** → simple pneumothorax → ICC, no needle.
> - **Hyper-resonant + hypotension + raised JVP + tracheal shift + escalating distress** → tension → decompress now, no CXR.
> - **Stony dull + hypotension + flat neck veins** → haemothorax.
> Between the two shocked pictures the discriminator is **percussion note plus neck veins**: tension obstructs venous return (veins up), haemothorax empties the tank (veins down). A tension patient who is also hypovolaemic may have flat veins — percussion then decides.

## 5.3 Haemorrhagic shock

**The short version**
- ⚑ **BP is a late sign — it does not fall until class III (~30–40% loss).** Tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure and anxiety come first.
- **Pulse pressure narrows in class II** — the earliest reliable marker.
- Young, fit patients and pregnant women compensate hard then crash; a normal BP in a 20-year-old does not exclude litres of loss.
- ⚑ Australian practice: **permissive hypotension, minimal crystalloid, early balanced blood products (MTP), TXA within 3 hrs.**
- Fix the **lethal triad**: hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy.

| | Class I | Class II | Class III | Class IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood loss | <750 mL (<15%) | 750–1500 mL (15–30%) | 1500–2000 mL (30–40%) | >2000 mL (>40%) |
| Heart rate | <100 | 100–120 | 120–140 | >140 or bradycardia pre-arrest |
| Blood pressure | Normal | **Normal** | **Decreased** | Decreased/unrecordable |
| Pulse pressure | Normal | **Narrowed** | Narrowed | Narrowed |
| Resp rate | 14–20 | 20–30 | 30–40 | >35 |
| Urine output | >30 mL/hr | 20–30 mL/hr | 5–15 mL/hr | Negligible |
| Mental state | Slightly anxious | Mildly anxious | Anxious, **confused** | Confused, **lethargic** |

⚑ The 10th edition cautions that this is a teaching framework, not a diagnostic instrument — beta-blockade, pacemakers, age, hypothermia, alcohol and analgesia decouple pulse and pressure from volume state. Treat the trend, lactate and base deficit.

**Compensation masks loss.** ⚑ A fit 20-year-old holds BP by vasoconstriction until sudden decompensation; in **pregnancy** plasma volume rises ~40–50%, so 1.5 L can be lost before maternal BP moves — foetal distress is often the first sign.

**Damage control resuscitation — three pillars.** (1) **Permissive hypotension**: accept a lower BP (palpable radial pulse, systolic ~80–90 mmHg) until surgical haemostasis, so clot is not dislodged. ⚑ **Exception: traumatic brain injury**, where cerebral perfusion must be protected. (2) **Minimal crystalloid** — ⚑ the old "2 L for everyone" is gone; it dilutes clotting factors, worsens acidosis and cools the patient. (3) **Balanced blood products** — red cells, FFP and platelets in roughly equal ratio via the local **MTP**, fibrinogen/cryoprecipitate guided by level or ROTEM/TEG, plus **ionised calcium** (citrate chelation impairs clotting and contractility).

**Tranexamic acid.** ⚑ Standard of care in Australian major trauma with significant haemorrhage. **CRASH-2**: **1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 hours**, started **within 3 hours of injury** — beyond 3 hours it increased death from bleeding, so the window is examinable both ways. **CRASH-3** enrolled isolated TBI with **GCS ≤12 or intracranial bleeding on CT**, within 3 hours; ⚑ benefit was found in the **GCS 9–15 subgroup**, which is the basis of the practice recommendation — do not quote 9–15 as the trial entry criterion. Some retrieval services use a simplified 2 g bolus — quote CRASH-2 unless told otherwise.

**Lethal triad:** hypothermia → acidosis → coagulopathy, each worsening the others. Countermeasures: fluid/blood warmers, active warming, minimal exposure, correct calcium, fast haemostasis (damage-control laparotomy — pack, control, temporary closure, return once corrected).

**Pelvic binder.** For suspected pelvic fracture with instability, at the level of the **greater trochanters** (not the iliac crests — a common error). Do not spring the pelvis repeatedly; it disrupts clot. Definitive control: angioembolisation or preperitoneal packing ± external fixation.

## 5.4 FAST and imaging choices

**The short version**
- FAST answers one question: **is there free fluid?** Four views — hepatorenal (Morison's), splenorenal, pelvic, subxiphoid pericardial.
- **Positive** FAST in an **unstable** patient = theatre. A **negative** FAST **excludes nothing**.
- FAST misses retroperitoneum, bowel, diaphragm, and solid-organ injury without free fluid.
- **eFAST** adds anterior thoracic views — pneumothorax (loss of lung sliding) and pleural fluid.
- **Unstable = theatre. Stable = CT.** "Too unstable for CT" is a real, examinable category.

The rule carrying most marks: **unstable + positive abdominal FAST → laparotomy**. Unstable with a **negative** FAST means excluding the other sites (chest and pelvic films, external, long bones) ± diagnostic peritoneal aspiration. **Haemodynamically normal, or a transient responder who stabilises** → whole-body **CT trauma series**: more sensitive, grades solid-organ injury for non-operative management, images the retroperitoneum, shows contrast blush for embolisation. A CT scanner is a dangerous place for a bleeding patient.

## 5.5 Head injury

**The short version**
- **Primary** injury happens at impact and cannot be undone; **secondary** injury (hypoxia, hypotension, hypercapnia, hyperthermia, seizure, raised ICP) is what you treat.
- Avoid the two great killers: **SpO₂ <90%** and **systolic <90 mmHg** — one hypotensive episode roughly doubles mortality.
- **Monro–Kellie**: fixed box; compensation by CSF and venous displacement is finite, then ICP rises steeply.
- **Cushing's reflex** (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiration) is **pre-herniation**, not an early warning.
- **Lucid interval → extradural haematoma → middle meningeal artery → urgent CT and neurosurgical referral.**

**GCS (score 3–15).**

| Component | High → low |
|---|---|
| **Eye** (4) | 4 spontaneous · 3 to voice · 2 to pain · 1 none |
| **Verbal** (5) | 5 orientated · 4 confused · 3 words · 2 sounds · 1 none |
| **Motor** (6) | 6 obeys · 5 localises · 4 withdraws · 3 abnormal flexion (decorticate) · 2 extension (decerebrate) · 1 none |

Severity: **mild 13–15, moderate 9–12, severe ≤8**. Record components separately; the **motor score** carries most prognostic weight. Score the **best** response.

**Raised ICP and herniation.** Worsening headache, vomiting, falling GCS, pupillary change (papilloedema late or absent acutely). **Uncal herniation** compresses CN III → **ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil**, **contralateral** hemiparesis (Kernohan's notch gives the false-localising variant). Tonsillar herniation → brainstem compression, apnoea, death.

**The three haemorrhages.**

| | Extradural | Acute subdural | Traumatic SAH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vessel | **Middle meningeal artery** | **Bridging cortical veins** | Small cortical surface vessels |
| Mechanism | Temporal blow ± skull fracture | Acceleration–deceleration; trivial fall in the frail | Significant blunt injury |
| Patient | Young adult | **Elderly, alcohol, anticoagulated, atrophic brain** | Any age |
| Time course | **Lucid interval, then decline over minutes–hours** | Hours (acute); weeks with fluctuating cognition (chronic) | Immediate |
| CT shape | **Biconvex/lentiform, hyperdense** | **Crescentic** over the convexity | Blood in sulci/fissures |
| Boundaries | **Does not cross sutures**; can cross dural reflections | **Crosses sutures**; not falx/tentorium | Neither applies |
| Outcome | Excellent if evacuated early | Worse — reflects brain injury | Depends on associated injury |

> **Exam point — head strike, then headache and falling GCS at 40 minutes.** Textbook **extradural**: initially lucid, arterial bleed from the **middle meningeal artery** stripping dura off the inner table, deterioration accelerating as compensation is exhausted. Chain: <C>ABCDE, prevent hypoxia/hypotension → **urgent non-contrast CT head** → **immediate neurosurgical referral for craniotomy and evacuation**. Reaching theatre before pupillary change usually means a good outcome.

**Canadian CT Head Rule.** Blunt minor head injury, age ≥16, GCS 13–15 with witnessed LOC, amnesia or disorientation. Does **not** apply if anticoagulated, obvious open skull fracture, post-injury seizure, or GCS <13. CT if any of:
- *High risk (neurosurgical intervention):* GCS still <15 **at two hours**; suspected open or depressed skull fracture; any base-of-skull sign (periorbital or mastoid bruising, haemotympanum, CSF otorrhoea/rhinorrhoea); **≥2 vomits**; **age ≥65**.
- *Medium risk:* retrograde amnesia **≥30 min** before impact; dangerous mechanism (pedestrian struck, ejection, fall >1 m or >5 stairs).

**The anticoagulated head injury.** ⚑ The rules above *exclude* anticoagulated patients because their risk is not captured — Australian practice is a **much lower threshold: effectively CT anyone on an anticoagulant with any head strike**, plus observation and consideration of repeat imaging, as these bleeds evolve late.

| Agent | Reversal in traumatic ICH |
|---|---|
| **Dabigatran** | **Idarucizumab** 5 g IV (2 × 2.5 g) — specific antibody fragment; haemodialysis as fallback |
| **Apixaban / rivaroxaban** | **Andexanet alfa** where stocked; otherwise **PCC** — ⚑ note Prothrombinex-VF is a **3-factor** PCC (II, IX, X); **Beriplex P/N** is the 4-factor product Australia is moving to — andexanet availability varies across Australian centres, so PCC is often what is given |
| **Warfarin** | **Vitamin K1 5–10 mg IV + Prothrombinex-VF 50 IU/kg + FFP 150–300 mL, all three together** ⚑ — traumatic ICH is critical-organ bleeding, and FFP is given *with* the PCC (not as a fallback) because Prothrombinex-VF is a **3-factor** product lacking adequate factor VII |
| **Antiplatelets** | Platelet transfusion **not** routine (PATCH harm signal) |

> **Exam point — 70-year-old on dabigatran who hit his head.** Even with GCS 15 and a normal examination: **urgent CT**, coagulation assessment, early haematology/neurosurgical involvement, and **idarucizumab** if intracranial haemorrhage is confirmed. Reversal is not deferred pending the neurosurgical opinion.

**Raised ICP management.** Head up 30°, neutral neck, relieve collar obstruction to venous drainage; sedation and analgesia; normoxia and **normocapnia (PaCO₂ ~35–40 mmHg)** — hyperventilation is a short-term rescue for impending herniation only, since vasoconstriction causes ischaemia; hyperosmolar therapy (**hypertonic saline** or mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg); seizure prophylaxis; treat fever; **CPP = MAP − ICP** ~60–70 mmHg; then surgery — evacuation, EVD, decompressive craniectomy. ⚑ Steroids are **contraindicated** in TBI (CRASH showed increased mortality).

## 5.6 Cervical spine

**The short version**
- Assume injury until cleared in any blunt trauma with a suggestive mechanism.
- ⚑ Immobilisation has shifted from hard collar plus blocks toward **minimal handling and self-restriction of movement**, especially in penetrating trauma and agitated patients where collars harm.
- **NEXUS** and the **Canadian C-Spine Rule** clear the spine clinically in alert, stable patients — the Canadian rule is more specific but has stricter entry criteria.
- CT is the imaging of choice in adults; plain films have no role in significant trauma.
- **Neurogenic shock = hypotension with bradycardia and warm, well-perfused peripheries.**

**NEXUS** — no imaging if **all five** hold: no midline posterior cervical tenderness; no focal neurological deficit; normal alertness; no intoxication; no distracting painful injury.

**Canadian C-Spine Rule** — alert (GCS 15), stable adults. (1) Any **high-risk factor** — **age ≥65**, dangerous mechanism (fall ≥1 m or five stairs, axial load such as diving, high-speed/rollover/ejection crash, motorised recreational vehicle, bicycle collision), or **limb paraesthesiae** → **image**. (2) If none, look for a **low-risk factor** permitting assessment of movement — simple rear-end collision, sitting up in ED, ambulatory since injury, delayed neck pain, no midline tenderness; if none → image. (3) With a low-risk factor, ask for **active 45° rotation each way**; if achieved, no imaging.

**Cord syndromes.**

| Syndrome | Mechanism | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Central cord** | Hyperextension, older spondylotic patient (commonest incomplete syndrome) | Weakness **upper > lower limbs**, distal > proximal, sacral sparing, bladder dysfunction |
| **Brown-Séquard** | Hemisection — penetrating injury, lateral mass fracture | **Ipsilateral** motor and dorsal-column loss; **contralateral** pain/temperature loss |
| **Anterior cord** | Anterior spinal artery infarct, flexion injury | Motor and pain/temperature loss below lesion, **proprioception preserved**; worst prognosis |

**Shock discrimination (high-yield).**

| | Neurogenic | Spinal | Haemorrhagic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Haemodynamic** — lost sympathetic outflow above T6 | **Neurological** — transient flaccid areflexia below the lesion | Volume loss |
| Heart rate | **Bradycardia** | N/A | **Tachycardia** |
| Peripheries | **Warm, vasodilated** | N/A | **Cool, clammy, pale** |
| Treatment | Cautious fluid then **noradrenaline**; atropine | Time; return of bulbocavernosus reflex | **Blood** and haemostasis |

⚑ Assume the hypotensive spinal-injured patient is bleeding until proven otherwise — neurogenic shock is a diagnosis of exclusion. ⚑ High-dose methylprednisolone for acute cord injury is **no longer recommended**.

## 5.7 Abdominal and pelvic trauma

**The short version**
- Blunt trauma injures **spleen and liver**; stab wounds **liver and small bowel**; gunshot **small bowel and colon**.
- **Abdominal examination is unreliable** in the intoxicated, head-injured or distracted patient — image or observe.
- Immediate laparotomy: instability with positive FAST, peritonitis, evisceration, free air, diaphragmatic rupture, gunshot traversing peritoneum, blood on gastric tube/PR.
- **Seat-belt sign** predicts hollow-viscus, mesenteric and lumbar (Chance) fracture injury — normal early CT does not exclude it, so admit and serially examine.
- Pelvic fracture bleeding is mostly **venous plexus and cancellous bone**; binder first, then angioembolisation or packing.

Stable blunt trauma is increasingly **non-operative**: CT-graded splenic and hepatic injuries are observed with serial haematocrit and examination, with angioembolisation for contrast blush. Splenectomy commits the patient to **post-splenectomy sepsis prophylaxis** — pneumococcal, meningococcal and Hib vaccination, antibiotics, medical alert. Stable anterior stab wounds without peritonitis may be managed by wound exploration, CT and serial examination. Retroperitoneal (duodenal, pancreatic) injuries are easily missed — rising lipase and retroperitoneal air are the clues.

## 5.8 Burns

**The short version**
- Stop the burning; **cool running water 20 minutes, effective up to 3 hours after injury**; never ice. Keep the patient warm while cooling the burn.
- Depth is judged on **appearance, blistering, capillary refill, sensation**.
- **Rule of Nines** for adults; **palmar surface ≈1%** for scattered burns; **Lund–Browder** for children.
- ⚑ ANZBA/EMSB fluid formula is **3 mL × kg × %TBSA of Hartmann's over 24 hours from the time of injury**, half in the first 8 hours — not the classic Parkland 4 mL.
- ⚑ **Burns do not cause early hypotension. A shocked burns patient has another injury** until proven otherwise.

**Depth.**

| Depth | Appearance | Blisters | Cap refill | Sensation | Healing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial (epidermal) | Red, dry | **No** | Brisk | **Painful** | 5–7 days, no scar; **excluded from TBSA** |
| Superficial partial | Pale pink, moist | **Yes** | Brisk | **Very painful**, hypersensitive | ~2 weeks, minimal scar |
| Deep partial | Blotchy red/white, moist | Yes, may deroof | **Sluggish/absent** | **Reduced** (blunted pinprick) | >3 weeks, scars — usually grafted |
| Full thickness | White, waxy, leathery, charred; thrombosed vessels | **No** | **Absent** | **Insensate** | Will not heal — excision and grafting |

**TBSA — Rule of Nines (adult):** head and neck **9**; each arm **9**; anterior trunk **18**; posterior trunk **18**; each leg **18**; perineum **1**. ⚑ **Superficial (erythema-only) burns are not counted.** **Palmar surface:** the whole palmar hand **including fingers ≈ 1% TBSA** — the ANZBA/EMSB teaching figure to quote. ⚑ Anatomical meta-analyses put the true value nearer **0.8%** (palm alone ~0.5%); quote 1%, noting the discrepancy if precision is invited. **Children:** head proportionally larger, legs smaller — infants approach **18% head, 14% per leg**; use an age-corrected **Lund–Browder** chart.

> **Worked Rule of Nines — entire anterior torso plus the whole of one arm.**
> Anterior chest 9 + anterior abdomen 9 = **18%**; whole arm (anterior and posterior) = **9%**. **Total = 27% TBSA.**

**Fluid resuscitation.** Start formal resuscitation at **>20% TBSA in adults**, **>10% in children**. ⚑ **ANZBA/EMSB (current ANZ practice): 3 mL × weight (kg) × %TBSA of Hartmann's in the first 24 hours.** Classic **Parkland uses 4 mL** and persists in older texts — quote **3 mL** as current for an Australian paper, saying so if you show both. Rules: **the clock starts at the TIME OF INJURY, not arrival**; **half in the first 8 hours from injury**, the rest over the next 16; children need **maintenance fluid** on top; the formula sets only a starting rate — **titrate to urine output: 0.5 mL/kg/hr adults, 1 mL/kg/hr children**, in 10–20% increments. More is needed with inhalation injury, electrical burns, delayed presentation or dark urine.

> **Worked calculation — 80 kg, 30% TBSA, presenting 1 hour after injury.**
> Total 24-hour volume: **3 × 80 × 30 = 7200 mL** Hartmann's.
> First half: **3600 mL** due within **8 hours of injury**. One hour has already elapsed → deliver over the remaining **7 hours** = **3600 ÷ 7 ≈ 514 mL/hr**.
> Second half: **3600 mL over the next 16 hours ≈ 225 mL/hr**.
> Target urine output: 0.5 × 80 = **40 mL/hr**.
> *(By the older Parkland 4 mL formula this would be 9600 mL total, 4800 mL in the first 8 hours, ≈686 mL/hr over the remaining 7 — quote the ANZBA figure.)*

> **Second worked example — 70 kg, 25% TBSA, arriving 2 hours after injury.**
> **3 × 70 × 25 = 5250 mL.** First half **2625 mL** over the **6 remaining** hours = **≈437 mL/hr**; second half 2625 mL over 16 hours ≈ **164 mL/hr**. Urine target **35 mL/hr**.

**Burns unit referral (ANZBA):** >10% TBSA (>5% children); full-thickness >5%; face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, major joints; **circumferential** limb or chest burns; **inhalation injury**; **electrical** or **chemical** burns; significant comorbidity, extremes of age, pregnancy, associated major trauma; suspected **non-accidental injury**.

**Inhalation injury.** Suspect with enclosed-space fire, facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in sputum, hoarseness or stridor. ⚑ **Intubate early** — laryngeal oedema progresses over hours and later becomes impossible. **Carbon monoxide**: SpO₂ falsely normal (the oximeter cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin) — measure **carboxyhaemoglobin by co-oximetry**, treat with **100% oxygen**. **Cyanide** (burning plastics/wool): profound lactic acidosis with apparently adequate oxygenation and cardiovascular collapse → **hydroxocobalamin**.

**Other management.** **Escharotomy** for circumferential full-thickness burns causing distal ischaemia, or chest burns restricting ventilation — bedside incisions along mid-axial lines; distinct from **fasciotomy** (deep electrical injury). **Analgesia** IV and titrated (IM absorption unreliable). Check **tetanus**; **no prophylactic antibiotics**. Remove rings early. Cling film is acceptable for transfer.

## 5.9 Compartment syndrome

**The short version**
- Interstitial pressure in a closed osseofascial compartment exceeds capillary perfusion pressure → **microvascular ischaemia while the major artery stays open**.
- **Pain out of proportion** and **pain on passive stretch** are the earliest and most reliable signs.
- **Pulses and capillary refill stay normal until very late** — waiting for pulselessness means waiting for a dead limb.
- **Diagnosis is clinical.** Absolute pressure **>30 mmHg**, or **ΔP (diastolic BP − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg**, supports it — measurement must never delay treatment.
- Treatment is **immediate open fasciotomy** of all compartments.

Commonest setting is a **tibial shaft fracture** (also forearm fracture, crush injury, reperfusion after vascular repair, tight casts, burns). Measure pressure only when the patient *cannot* report pain — intubated, obtunded, regional-blocked, very young.

**Six Ps in order:** **Pain** (severe, escalating, opioid-resistant) → **Pain on passive stretch** → **Paraesthesia** (deep peroneal first — first web space) → **Paresis** → **Pallor** → **Pulselessness** (terminal). Pulses persist because arterial systolic pressure far exceeds the compartment pressure that has already collapsed the capillaries.

**Management.** Remove all circumferential casts, splints and dressings to skin. ⚑ **Keep the limb at heart level — do NOT elevate**; elevation reduces inflow and narrows ΔP further. Analgesia, oxygen, normotension, correct hypovolaemia. **Urgent fasciotomy** — the leg needs **all four compartments** released, usually two-incision; wounds left open for delayed closure or grafting. Muscle tolerates ~4–6 hours of ischaemia.

> **Exam point — tibial fracture with severe pain 6 hours later.** Escalating pain despite adequate opioid is compartment syndrome until disproved. Answer: **split the cast to skin, limb at heart level, immediate fasciotomy referral** — not elevate, not more analgesia, not repeat films, and not "measure the pressure first" when the picture is clear.

## 5.10 Rhabdomyolysis and crush injury

**The short version**
- Myocyte necrosis releases myoglobin, potassium, phosphate and CK; myoglobin causes tubular obstruction and direct nephrotoxicity → **AKI**.
- **Tea-coloured urine, dipstick positive for blood with no red cells on microscopy** — the classic discriminator.
- **CK is the diagnostic test** (>5× upper limit; AKI risk rises sharply above ~5000 U/L).
- **Hyperkalaemia is the immediate killer** — ECG and treat before creatinine returns.
- ⚑ **Crush/reperfusion syndrome:** start IV fluid **before** the crushing force is released where possible.

Management: high-volume isotonic crystalloid titrated to **urine output 1–2 mL/kg/hr**; cardiac monitoring; treat hyperkalaemia (calcium gluconate, insulin–dextrose, salbutamol, then definitive removal); watch for hypocalcaemia (calcium deposits in damaged muscle — do not over-correct, it is released later), hyperphosphataemia, and compartment syndrome as muscle swells. ⚑ **Bicarbonate alkalinisation and mannitol are no longer routine** — weak evidence over volume alone. RRT for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, overload or severe AKI.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Gurgling in a trauma patient | Airway — suction immediately, before anything else |
| Absent breath sounds + **hyper-resonant** + hypotension + raised JVP | Tension pneumothorax — decompress, no CXR |
| Absent breath sounds + **stony dull** + flat neck veins | Massive haemothorax — blood + ICC |
| Adult needle decompression site (current) | **5th ICS, just anterior to mid-axillary line**, cannula ≥8 cm |
| ICC output mandating thoracotomy | >1500 mL immediately, or >200 mL/hr for 2–4 hrs |
| Hypotension + raised JVP + muffled heart sounds | Beck's triad — tamponade |
| Shock class at which BP first falls | Class III (30–40%, ~1500–2000 mL) |
| Earliest haemodynamic change in haemorrhage | Narrowed pulse pressure (class II) |
| TXA regimen and window | 1 g over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 hrs, within 3 hrs |
| Pelvic binder position | Level of the **greater trochanters** |
| Negative FAST in an unstable patient | Excludes nothing — chest, pelvis, retroperitoneum, external |
| Lucid interval then rapid decline | Extradural — middle meningeal artery, urgent CT + neurosurgery |
| Head injury on dabigatran / apixaban | **Idarucizumab** / andexanet alfa or prothrombinex |
| Hypotension + **bradycardia** + warm peripheries | Neurogenic shock — exclude bleeding first |
| Upper limbs weaker than lower after hyperextension | Central cord syndrome |
| ANZBA burn formula | 3 mL × kg × %TBSA Hartmann's/24 hrs **from injury**, half in 8 hrs |
| Burn urine targets | 0.5 mL/kg/hr adults; 1 mL/kg/hr children |
| Shocked in the first hours after a burn | Not the burn — find the other injury |
| Normal SpO₂ but drowsy after enclosed-space fire | Carbon monoxide — co-oximetry, 100% O₂ |
| Pain on passive stretch after tibial fracture | Compartment syndrome — fasciotomy |
| ΔP threshold for fasciotomy | Diastolic BP − compartment pressure <30 mmHg |
