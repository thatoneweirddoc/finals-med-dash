# Surgery Notes — Section 1: Perioperative Assessment and Preparation

*Sources: ANZCA PG07(A) and ANZCA/ADS/GESA/NACOS recommendations, Choosing Wisely Australia (ANZCA), ADS–ANZCA perioperative diabetes guidelines and ADS SGLT2i alerts, Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic, AMH, THANZ/IMJ DOAC guidance, RACS VTE guidance, NBA PBM Module 2, ACSQHC, 2024 ACC/AHA perioperative guideline, ERAS Society, Rogers v Whitaker (1992). Scope: pre-operative assessment, risk stratification, medication management, fasting, prophylaxis, consent and ERAS in the adult Australian surgical patient.*

## 1.1 Risk stratification

**The short version**
- ASA describes **systemic disease burden**, not operative risk — a crude descriptor predicting population mortality and nothing about the specific operation.
- ⚑ Routine pre-op bloods/ECG/CXR in a **healthy young patient having intermediate surgery are NOT indicated** — order in response to patient factors, symptoms, disease or the planned procedure (Choosing Wisely Australia/ANZCA).
- Inguinal hernia repair is **intermediate**, not minor — but surgical grade alone does not generate an investigation.
- **<4 METs** (can't climb a flight of stairs) = poor functional capacity, the trigger for further cardiac thought.
- **RCRI** = 6 equally-weighted variables; ≥2 points marks elevated risk.

### ASA physical status classification

| Class | Meaning (paraphrased) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Normal healthy patient; non-smoker, nil/minimal alcohol | Fit 25 y.o. |
| **II** | Mild systemic disease, **no substantive functional limitation** | Controlled HTN or T2DM, smoker, pregnancy, BMI 30–40 |
| **III** | Severe systemic disease with **substantive functional limitation** | Poorly controlled DM/HTN, COPD, BMI ≥40, dialysis, MI/CVA/stent **>3 months ago** |
| **IV** | Severe systemic disease that is a **constant threat to life** | MI/CVA/stent **<3 months ago**, ongoing ischaemia, severe valve disease, sepsis, DIC |
| **V** | Moribund; not expected to survive without the operation | Ruptured AAA, massive trauma, intracranial bleed with mass effect |
| **VI** | Brain-dead; organs being retrieved | — |
| **E** | **Modifier**, not a class (e.g. 2E) — added when delay would increase threat to life or limb; the *inability to safely delay* defines it | Incarcerated hernia |

**ASA does** correlate with mortality, length of stay and complications at population level — useful for audit and resourcing. **ASA does not** score the individual: it ignores the operation, age and airway, and has poor inter-rater reliability. An ASA 1 for emergency laparotomy is at far higher risk than an ASA 3 for cataract surgery.

### The exam scenario: ASA 1, elective inguinal hernia repair — which investigations?

**Answer: essentially none.** ⚑ A healthy young man on no medications, no comorbidity, having intermediate surgery needs **no routine FBC, U&E, coags, BSL, ECG or CXR**. The "pre-op screen" is obsolete. Choosing Wisely Australia/ANZCA is explicit: avoid routine pre-operative bloods, CXR or spirometry; order **in response to patient factors, symptoms and signs, disease, or the planned surgery**. Abnormalities in asymptomatic patients rarely change management, are often false positives, and generate a cascade of testing, delay and anxiety.

What actually triggers a test:

| Investigation | Indicated when |
|---|---|
| FBC | Major surgery/anticipated blood loss; suspected anaemia, menorrhagia, chronic GI loss; CKD, malignancy, chemotherapy, haematological disease |
| U&E | Diuretics, ACEI/ARB, digoxin, lithium, NSAIDs; CKD, diabetes, cardiac failure; major surgery or large fluid shifts |
| Coags | Anticoagulated; suspected bleeding disorder or liver disease. **Not** a screen when the bleeding history is negative |
| BSL/HbA1c | Known or suspected diabetes |
| ECG | Cardiac disease or symptoms (chest pain, syncope, palpitations), arrhythmia; major surgery with cardiac risk factors. Not by age alone in the asymptomatic fit patient |
| CXR | Acute respiratory symptoms, suspected malignancy. Not routine, not because a GA is planned |
| Group & hold | Anticipated transfusion |
| Pregnancy test | Anyone of childbearing potential in whom pregnancy is possible — offer, with consent |

### Surgical risk grading

| Grade | Examples | 30-day cardiac risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Minor** | Skin lesion excision, cataract, dental, breast biopsy, endoscopy | <1% |
| **Intermediate** | **Inguinal hernia repair**, lap chole, TURP, hip/knee arthroplasty, carotid endarterectomy, head & neck | 1–5% |
| **Major** | Open aortic/suprainguinal vascular, open colorectal/hepatic/oesophageal, pneumonectomy, Whipple | >5% |

### Functional capacity — METs

1 MET = resting O₂ consumption (~3.5 mL/kg/min). **4 METs** is the threshold: below it, reserve is poor and further evaluation may be warranted; above it, testing rarely changes management. 1 MET = dress, walk indoors; **4 METs = climb a flight of stairs, walk up a hill**; >10 METs = swimming, running. ⚑ Subjective estimation of METs performs poorly; 2024 ACC/AHA favours the **Duke Activity Status Index** (>34 = low risk).

### Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)

One point each: **high-risk surgery** (intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, **suprainguinal** vascular); **ischaemic heart disease** (prior MI, positive stress test, ischaemic chest pain, nitrates, pathological Q waves); **congestive cardiac failure** (pulmonary oedema, PND, bibasal creps, S3, CXR redistribution); **cerebrovascular disease** (stroke or TIA); **insulin-treated diabetes** (insulin, not diabetes per se); **creatinine >177 µmol/L**.

0 points ≈ very low risk; ≥2 ≈ elevated risk of major cardiac events. RCRI underestimates risk in vascular surgery. **NT-proBNP/BNP** adds discrimination in patients ≥45 with risk factors having major surgery.

## 1.2 Cardiac assessment before non-cardiac surgery

**The short version**
- Test only if the result **would change management** — same indications as the non-surgical setting.
- Active cardiac conditions mean **postpone and treat**.
- Elective surgery: delay **≥60 days after MI**; **≥30 days after bare-metal stent**; **≥6 months after drug-eluting stent** (⚑ 12 months if placed for ACS).
- **Never stop aspirin + P2Y12 within the mandatory DAPT window** — reschedule the surgery instead. Stent thrombosis carries ~20–45% mortality.

### When to investigate further

Investigate only when functional capacity is poor or unknown (**<4 METs**) **AND** clinical risk is elevated **AND** the result would alter management. **Resting TTE** — new/worsening dyspnoea, known or suspected moderate–severe valvular disease, uncharacterised LV dysfunction; not routine before major surgery. **Stress testing** — same indications as in the non-operative patient, not in low-risk patients or those with good exercise tolerance; ⚑ revascularisation purely to "get the patient through" does not improve outcome (CARP) and delays surgery. **Coronary angiography** — standard ACS indications only.

### Active cardiac conditions — delay surgery

Unstable/severe angina or recent ACS; decompensated heart failure (new, worsening, NYHA IV); significant arrhythmia (high-grade AV block, symptomatic bradycardia or VT, SVT with uncontrolled rate); severe symptomatic AS or MS. ⚑ Uncontrolled hypertension is *not* on this list — it predicts peri-operative events poorly, though ≥180/110 warrants reassessment rather than reflex cancellation.

### Timing rules

| Event | Minimum delay for elective surgery | Antiplatelet rule |
|---|---|---|
| Myocardial infarction | **60 days** (risk falls steeply over the first 2 months) | Continue aspirin |
| Balloon angioplasty, no stent | **14 days** | Continue aspirin |
| **Bare-metal stent** | **30 days** | DAPT mandatory 30 days — do not interrupt |
| **Drug-eluting stent, chronic coronary syndrome** | **6 months** (3 months acceptable if surgery cannot be delayed and risk of delay outweighs stent risk) | DAPT mandatory ≥3 months |
| **Drug-eluting stent, placed for ACS** | **12 months** ⚑ | DAPT 12 months |

**Rules that earn marks:** if surgery must proceed inside the window, operate in a centre with 24-hour PCI access, **continue aspirin throughout**, stop only the P2Y12 inhibitor and for the shortest possible time, restart it as soon as haemostasis allows (ideally <5 days). Bridging a P2Y12 inhibitor with a short-acting IV agent (tirofiban, cangrelor) is a specialist decision, not routine. Discuss with the interventional cardiologist and document who was consulted.

## 1.3 Respiratory disease, OSA and smoking

**The short version**
- Post-op pulmonary complications (PPC) rival cardiac complications in frequency and exceed them in attributable length of stay.
- The biggest predictors are **surgical**: upper abdominal/thoracic incision, open approach, operative duration >3 h, emergency surgery.
- **STOP-BANG ≥3** = intermediate risk of OSA, **≥5** = high risk.
- Stop smoking **≥4 weeks** before surgery for a measurable reduction in respiratory and wound complications; stopping at any point is still worth doing.
- Routine spirometry and CXR do not predict PPC and are not indicated in the absence of symptoms.

**Patient risk factors:** age >60, ASA ≥3, COPD (especially recent exacerbation), current smoking, obesity/OSA, heart failure, functional dependence, hypoalbuminaemia, active respiratory infection, pulmonary hypertension.

**Risk reduction that works:** lung expansion (incentive spirometry, CPAP), early mobilisation, analgesia adequate to allow coughing, regional/neuraxial where feasible, minimally invasive approach, quantitative neuromuscular monitoring with full reversal, and deferring elective surgery 4–6 weeks after a significant LRTI.

**STOP-BANG:** **S**noring loudly, **T**iredness, **O**bserved apnoea, **P**ressure (treated HTN), **B**MI >35, **A**ge >50, **N**eck >40 cm, **G**ender male. ≥3 intermediate, ≥5 high. High risk → peri-operative CPAP (own machine), minimise sedatives and long-acting opioids, extended recovery monitoring, avoid major day-case surgery.

**Smoking.** Nicotine and CO effects resolve in 12–24 h; ciliary function and sputum clearance take weeks; wound healing and immune benefits accrue over 4–8 weeks. ⚑ The old claim that stopping within 8 weeks *increases* complications is not supported by contemporary meta-analyses. Offer NRT — no need to stop it peri-operatively.

## 1.4 Medications: stop, continue, or switch

**The short version**
- Most cardiovascular drugs **continue**; most anti-diabetic drugs **stop** on the day. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1RAs have new, specific rules.
- **Aspirin continues** in secondary prevention (always with a stent); stop only in primary prevention or bleeding into a closed space.
- ⚑ **Warfarin bridging is now much more restricted** — BRIDGE showed bridging in AF increases major bleeding without reducing thromboembolism.
- ⚑ **DOACs are not bridged** — interrupt by drug, renal function and procedural bleeding risk.
- ⚑ **SGLT2 inhibitors: cease ≥3 days pre-procedure** because of euglycaemic DKA.

| Drug class | Action | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspirin** | Usually **continue** | Secondary prevention, all stents, vascular surgery. Withhold ~7 days only for intracranial, intramedullary spinal or posterior-chamber eye surgery, or primary prevention (POISE-2: no benefit, more bleeding) |
| **Clopidogrel / prasugrel / ticagrelor** | Stop **5–7 / 7 / 3–5 days** | Clopidogrel and prasugrel irreversible; ticagrelor reversible but has an active metabolite |
| **Warfarin** | Stop **5 days**; INR day before/of; target **<1.5** (⚑ **≤1.4 for neuraxial** — not 1.2) | Urgent reversal: vitamin K ± PCC. ⚑ **Bridge only for genuinely high thrombotic risk** — mechanical **mitral**/caged-ball valve, VTE <3 months, thromboembolism during a prior interruption, severe thrombophilia, recent stroke with very high CHA₂DS₂-VASc. **Do not bridge routine AF** |
| **DOACs** | See below — **no bridging** | By drug, CrCl and bleeding risk |
| **ACEI / ARB** | **Withhold morning dose** | Reduces refractory intra-op hypotension. Reasonable to continue in HFrEF |
| **Beta-blockers** | **Continue** — never stop abruptly | ⚑ Do **not** start de novo on the day (POISE: fewer MIs, excess stroke and death). If newly indicated, start ≥8 days pre-op |
| **CCBs / statins** | **Continue** | Statin rebound if stopped; consider starting in vascular surgery |
| **Diuretics** | Withhold morning dose | Check K⁺; continue in decompensated HF |
| **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapa-, empa-, ertugliflozin) | ⚑ **Cease ≥3 days** — 2 days before **plus** the day of surgery | **Euglycaemic DKA** — acidosis at a near-normal BSL. Check ketones if unwell/nauseated regardless of BSL. Day-stay: stopping on the day may suffice if fasting is brief. Emergency surgery on an SGLT2i → post-op ward able to manage DKA |
| **Metformin / sulfonylureas / DPP-4 inhibitors** | Withhold on the day | Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia while fasting; recheck renal function after major surgery/contrast |
| **GLP-1 / GLP-1–GIP RAs** (semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide) | ⚑ 2025 ADS/ANZCA/GESA/NACOS: **do not routinely cease**; **24 h clear fluid diet**, then standard fasting | Delayed gastric emptying → aspiration. ⚑ Diverges from earlier US (ASA 2023) advice to withhold 1 day (daily)/1 week (weekly). If unprepared: gastric POCUS, regional technique or RSI |
| **Insulin** | Basal continues (see 1.5); halve pre-mixed morning dose; omit short-acting while fasting | Never omit basal in T1DM |
| **Corticosteroids** | **Continue** + stress dose if prednisolone **≥5 mg/day for ≥1 month** or known adrenal insufficiency | Hydrocortisone **100 mg IV at induction**, then 200 mg/24 h infusion while NBM. Double usual oral dose: up to a week (major), 48 h (intermediate), 24 h (minor) |
| **COCP / HRT** | Individualise | Consider stopping COCP 4 weeks before major surgery; otherwise ensure VTE prophylaxis |
| **Lithium** | Withhold 24–72 h before major surgery | Narrow index; toxicity with fluid shifts, NSAIDs, ACEI, diuretics. Check level and U&E |
| **MAOIs** | Continue where possible, **flag to anaesthetics** | Avoid **pethidine** (serotonin syndrome) and indirect sympathomimetics (ephedrine, metaraminol) |
| **SSRIs/SNRIs** | Continue | Platelet dysfunction; hyponatraemia; serotonin syndrome with tramadol/fentanyl |
| **Methotrexate** | **Continue** | Safe at RA doses with stable renal function; withholding provokes flare |
| **Biologics** (TNF inhibitors, rituximab, JAK inhibitors) | Withhold — time surgery for the **end of a dosing interval** | Infection and wound healing; JAK inhibitors ~3–7 days |
| **Herbal supplements** | Stop ~**7 days** | The "G"s bleed — garlic, ginger, ginkgo, ginseng. St John's wort induces CYP450; kava/valerian sedate |

### DOAC interruption (no bridging)

| Drug | Renal function | Low bleeding-risk procedure | High bleeding-risk procedure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban | CrCl ≥30 | Omit **1 day** (last dose 2 days pre-op) | Omit **2 days** (last dose 3 days pre-op) |
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban | CrCl <30 | Extend by ≥1 day; seek haematology advice | Extend by ≥1–2 days |
| Dabigatran | CrCl >50 | Omit **1 day** | Omit **2 days** |
| Dabigatran | CrCl 30–50 | Omit **2 days** | Omit **4 days** |

Restart at **~24 h** after low-bleeding-risk and **48–72 h** after high-bleeding-risk surgery, once haemostasis is secure; prophylactic LMWH may cover the interval if VTE risk is high. Neuraxial blockade counts as high bleeding risk.

**⚑ Exam scenario — 71 y.o. on apixaban for non-valvular AF, elective right hemicolectomy.** Right hemicolectomy is major intra-abdominal surgery = **high bleeding risk**. Check CrCl; **omit apixaban for 2 clear days** (last dose 3 days pre-op, ~48–72 h of interruption); **do not bridge** — a DOAC's onset and offset are fast enough that bridging only adds bleeding; no routine coagulation testing (INR/APTT do not reflect DOAC level); post-op VTE prophylaxis per protocol; **restart apixaban 48–72 h post-op** once haemostasis is confirmed, with prophylactic LMWH in the interim.

## 1.5 Diabetes in the perioperative period

**The short version**
- Measure **HbA1c** within 3 months of elective surgery; **delay elective surgery if HbA1c ≥75 mmol/mol (9%)** and optimise.
- Put diabetic patients **first on the list** to minimise fasting.
- ⚑ **In type 1 diabetes, basal insulin is NEVER omitted** — omission causes DKA regardless of BSL and regardless of fasting.
- Target BSL **5–10 mmol/L** peri-operatively; monitor hourly.
- A VRIII is added *alongside* basal insulin, not instead of it.

**Pre-operative.** HbA1c, U&E, ECG if indicated. Look for complications with anaesthetic implications: autonomic neuropathy (gastroparesis → aspiration; silent ischaemia), nephropathy, stiff-joint syndrome (difficult intubation), CAD. Hypoglycaemia unawareness shifts the target upward (~10 ± 2.5 mmol/L).

**Day of surgery.** Withhold all non-insulin agents that morning (SGLT2i already ceased 3 days). Continue **basal insulin at usual dose and time** (−20% if recent nocturnal hypoglycaemia); halve pre-mixed morning dose; omit short-acting while fasting. Hourly BSL from admission.

**VRIII indications:** prolonged fasting (>1 missed meal) or major surgery; BSL rapidly rising and/or >12 mmol/L, **>12 for over an hour despite a correction bolus, or >15** (⚑ the ">10 twice" trigger is specific to cardiothoracic/ICU patients) or persistently >10; T1DM having more than a short day-case procedure; emergency surgery with poor control, DKA/HHS; ICU/cardiothoracic with BSL >10 twice.

**⚑ Absolute rule:** run subcutaneous long-acting basal **alongside** the VRIII. If basal is stopped, any interruption (tissued line, pump alarm, ward transfer) leaves a T1DM patient with **no circulating insulin** and DKA follows within hours. Give a substrate infusion (glucose with potassium) with the VRIII and overlap 30–60 min with subcutaneous insulin before ceasing.

**⚑ Exam scenario — 45 y.o. T1DM for bowel resection who has taken no insulin.** T1DM is **absolute** insulin deficiency. Omitting insulin while fasting does not protect against hypoglycaemia — it removes suppression of lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis, FFAs are converted to ketone bodies, and **DKA** develops, potentially at a BSL that looks acceptable (euglycaemic DKA); fasting, bowel prep and surgical stress accelerate it. Action: **BSL, VBG, ketones and electrolytes now**; do not proceed until ketoacidosis is excluded or treated; start a **VRIII with glucose and potassium** and restart basal; treat established DKA with fluid resuscitation and a fixed-rate infusion before elective surgery. If genuinely emergent, resuscitate and operate concurrently with senior input.

**Hyperglycaemia** >10 mmol/L: subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin by weight and diabetes type; recheck at 1 h; escalate to VRIII if persistent. **Hypoglycaemia**: <4.0 mmol/L must be avoided; treat from <5.0 mmol/L with IV glucose, recheck at 15 min, then longer-acting carbohydrate. Anaesthesia abolishes the adrenergic warning signs — hourly measurement is the only safeguard.

## 1.6 Fasting

**The short version**
- Adults: **6 h** solids, **2 h** clear fluids — ⚑ then further liberalised.
- ⚑ "**Sip til send**" — clear fluids up to **200 mL/h (adult) or 3 mL/kg/h (child) until called to theatre**.
- Children: solids 6 h, **breast milk 3 h**, **formula 4 h — ⚑ only in infants under 12 months; over 12 months formula and other non-human milk count as a solid and need 6 h**, clear fluids **1 h**.
- ⚑ "Nil by mouth from midnight" is **obsolete** — ~12 h average fasts, dehydration, hypotension on induction, hypoglycaemia and distress, with no aspiration benefit.
- GLP-1RA users: 24 h clear fluid diet then standard fasting (see 1.4).

| Intake | Adults | Children |
|---|---|---|
| Solid food / limited solid food (e.g. toast) | 6 h | 6 h |
| Infant formula | — | 4 h |
| Breast milk | — | 3 h |
| **Clear fluids** | 2 h (and **sip til send**, ≤200 mL/h) | 1 h (and sip til send, 3 mL/kg/h) |

**Clear fluids** = water, clear cordial/pulp-free apple juice, black tea or coffee, clear carbohydrate drinks. **Not** clear: anything with milk or particulate matter, alcohol.

Fasting rules assume normal gastric emptying. Consider RSI, gastric ultrasound or prokinetics with: pregnancy, obesity, diabetic gastroparesis, symptomatic GORD, bowel obstruction, raised intra-abdominal pressure, trauma/pain/opioids after the last meal, and GLP-1RA use.

## 1.7 VTE risk assessment and prophylaxis

**The short version**
- Assess every surgical inpatient on admission and reassess as things change — documented risk assessment is a national safety standard.
- **Enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily** is the usual Australian agent and dose (20 mg if lower risk; UFH 5000 U SC BD–TDS if CrCl <30).
- **Mechanical prophylaxis** (GCS ± IPC) whenever pharmacological prophylaxis is contraindicated or deferred.
- Extended prophylaxis: **28 days after major abdominal/pelvic cancer surgery**; **28–35 days after hip fracture and hip arthroplasty**.
- Neuraxial: **12 h** after prophylactic LMWH, **24 h** after a therapeutic dose; **4 h** after catheter removal before the next dose.

**Risk factors — patient:** previous VTE, active cancer, age >60, obesity, immobility, thrombophilia, pregnancy/puerperium, oestrogen therapy, heart failure, sepsis, IBD, dehydration, CVC. **Procedural:** major surgery (>30–45 min), pelvic/abdominal/orthopaedic surgery, arthroplasty, hip fracture, major trauma, spinal cord injury, prolonged anaesthesia, tourniquet.

**Agents.** LMWH (enoxaparin) first line, once daily, renally cleared — reduce or switch if CrCl <30. UFH 5000 U SC BD–TDS in severe renal impairment (higher HIT risk). Fondaparinux 2.5 mg daily from 6–8 h post-op in orthopaedics. Rivaroxaban/apixaban after elective arthroplasty. Aspirin is an arthroplasty option, though ⚑ **CRISTAL** found enoxaparin superior for symptomatic VTE. Mechanical = GCS and/or IPC (IPC has the better surgical evidence). First dose usually 6–12 h post-op, balancing the bleeding window against VTE risk and neuraxial timing.

**Duration.** Until discharge or full mobility (typically 5–10 days). Extend to **28 days** after major abdominal/pelvic **cancer** surgery and **28–35 days** after hip fracture or hip arthroplasty; ~10–14 days after knee arthroplasty.

**Contraindications — pharmacological:** active bleeding, severe coagulopathy, platelets <50, recent intracranial haemorrhage or neurosurgery, untreated inherited bleeding disorder, neuraxial procedure within the window, HIT. **Mechanical:** severe PAD, peripheral neuropathy, recent skin graft, cellulitis, severe leg deformity or oedema.

## 1.8 Surgical antibiotic prophylaxis and SSI prevention

**The short version**
- **A single pre-operative dose** — the aim is a therapeutic tissue concentration at knife-to-skin, not days of "cover".
- Give **within 60 min before incision** (usually at induction; vancomycin 2 h before, as it infuses slowly).
- **Cefazolin 2 g IV** first-line in adults (⚑ the 30 mg/kg up to 2 g formula is the **paediatric** dose — do not apply it to adults, it under-doses anyone under ~67 kg), **plus metronidazole 500 mg** (given within **120 min**, as for vancomycin) for colorectal and anaerobic-risk procedures.
- **Redose** beyond ~2 half-lives (cefazolin ~4 h) or blood loss >1.5 L.
- ⚑ **Prolonged post-operative prophylaxis is not indicated** — no SSI reduction, and it drives resistance and *C. difficile*. Do not continue "until drains out".

Prophylaxis is warranted where the SSI rate is meaningful or the consequence catastrophic: clean-contaminated and contaminated surgery, and clean surgery with prosthetic material (joint, graft, valve) — ⚑ **including hernia repair with or without mesh, which Therapeutic Guidelines lists as YES**. Clean surgery without prosthesis generally needs none. Dirty cases (established infection, perforated viscus) require **treatment**, not prophylaxis.

Severe/immediate penicillin hypersensitivity: substitute per Therapeutic Guidelines (vancomycin ± gentamicin ± metronidazole). ⚑ A non-severe penicillin rash is **not** a contraindication to cefazolin — cross-reactivity with first-generation cephalosporins is very low and reflex vancomycin substitution worsens SSI outcomes. Allergy delabelling is a genuine peri-operative intervention.

### SSI prevention bundle

| Element | Position and evidence |
|---|---|
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Single dose within 60 min of incision, redose for duration/blood loss. Post-op doses only in defined settings (some cardiac/vascular, lower-limb amputation) and ⚑ **never beyond 24 h**. **Strong** |
| **Normothermia** | Core **≥36 °C** — forced-air warming, warmed fluids. Hypothermia → vasoconstriction, low tissue O₂, impaired neutrophil function, coagulopathy, blood loss. **Strong** |
| **Glycaemic control** | **<10 mmol/L** in diabetics **and** non-diabetics with stress hyperglycaemia; avoid tight control. **Strong** |
| **Skin preparation** | **Alcohol-based chlorhexidine** (2% in 70% alcohol) > aqueous povidone-iodine. Must **dry fully** — diathermy fire. Avoid near eye, ear, mucosa. **Strong** |
| **Hair removal** | ⚑ **Don't remove unless it interferes.** If needed, **clippers immediately pre-op — never a razor** (microabrasions). **Strong** |
| **Supplemental oxygen** | Reasonable intra-op and immediately post-op in intubated patients. **Contested** |
| **Technique** | Haemostasis, gentle handling, no dead space, minimise operative time. **Moderate** |
| **Peri-operative bathing** | Soap or antiseptic wash night before/day of. **Weak but harmless** |
| **Bowel prep** | Mechanical alone: no benefit. ⚑ **Mechanical + oral antibiotics** reduces SSI in elective colorectal surgery. **Moderate** |
| **Dressings / drains** | Advanced dressings no better than standard; drains do **not** prevent SSI. **Moderate** |
| **Surgical Safety Checklist** | Sign-in/time-out/sign-out, antibiotic timing confirmed at time-out. **ACSQHC standard** |

## 1.9 Consent

**The short version**
- Australia applies the **material risk** test from **Rogers v Whitaker (1992)** — **not** Bolam, which the High Court expressly rejected for the duty to warn.
- Two limbs: **objective** — a reasonable person in the patient's position would attach significance to the risk; **subjective** — the doctor knows, or ought to know, that *this* patient would.
- Valid consent = **voluntary + informed + capacity + specific to the procedure**.
- It must be taken by someone able to perform the procedure or with adequate knowledge of it — not the most junior person available.
- In an emergency, treatment to save life or prevent serious harm may proceed without consent, limited to what is immediately necessary.

**Rogers v Whitaker.** Mrs Whitaker, near-blind in the right eye, had elective surgery on that eye and developed **sympathetic ophthalmia** in the good left eye — roughly a 1 in 14,000 risk — leaving her effectively blind. She had asked repeatedly about risk to the good eye; it was not disclosed. The High Court held a risk is material if **either** limb is met, and that the courts, not the profession, set the standard. Statistical rarity does not excuse non-disclosure where the consequence matters to that patient.

**Must be disclosed:** diagnosis; the procedure and what it involves; material risks, including rare risks with grave consequences; expected benefit and likelihood of success; **alternatives, including conservative management and doing nothing**; who will perform it (including trainee involvement); recovery, pain and time off; costs where relevant; and answers to the patient's actual questions — in language they understand, with a professional interpreter where needed.

**Capacity** is decision-specific and presumed in adults: **understand, retain, weigh, communicate**. An unwise decision is not evidence of incapacity.

**Who may consent:** the competent adult; a **mature minor** (Gillick-competent) for that decision, otherwise parent/guardian; for adults lacking capacity, a substitute decision-maker under state/territory guardianship law, respecting any valid **advance care directive**.

**Emergency doctrine:** where the patient lacks capacity and treatment is urgently needed to save life, prevent serious harm or relieve significant pain, proceed without consent — limited to what is immediately necessary, and not against a known valid refusal.

**Invalidating factors:** coercion; lack of capacity; consent taken after premedication/sedation; consent for a different procedure or wrong side/site; material risks undisclosed; questions unanswered; language barrier unaddressed; form signed with no discussion. Consent is a **process**, not a signature.

## 1.10 ERAS — enhanced recovery after surgery

**The short version**
- A bundled pathway from pre-admission to discharge; benefit comes from **compliance with the bundle**, not any single element.
- Reduces length of stay and complications **without** increasing readmission.
- Best pre-op evidence: counselling, avoiding prolonged fasting, **carbohydrate loading**, smoking/alcohol cessation, anaemia optimisation.
- Best intra/post-op evidence: opioid-sparing analgesia, goal-directed fluids, **no routine NG tubes or drains**, early feeding and mobilisation, early catheter removal, PONV prophylaxis.

**Pre-op:** structured counselling (patients who know the plan mobilise and eat sooner), prehabilitation, anaemia correction, smoking/alcohol cessation ~4 weeks out, no long-acting sedative premedication, and **carbohydrate loading** — a clear carbohydrate drink the evening before and 2–3 h pre-op, which attenuates the catabolic response and post-operative insulin resistance without increasing aspiration risk where emptying is normal.

**Intra-op:** minimally invasive approach, short-acting agents, normothermia, goal-directed rather than liberal fluids (overload and hypovolaemia both harm), regional/neuraxial and TAP blocks to spare opioids, multi-agent PONV prophylaxis.

**Post-op:** ⚑ **no routine NG tube** after abdominal surgery — it delays return of bowel function and increases pulmonary complications; ⚑ **no routine abdominal drains** — they neither prevent nor reliably detect anastomotic leak; remove urinary catheters early (<24 h unless epidural/pelvic surgery); **early oral intake** on day 0–1 rather than waiting for flatus; **out of bed on day 0**; multimodal analgesia with opioids as rescue only; defined discharge criteria.

## 1.11 Pre-operative anaemia and patient blood management

**The short version**
- **Three pillars:** optimise the patient's own red cell mass, minimise blood loss, optimise tolerance of anaemia.
- Anaemia is common, an **independent** predictor of morbidity, mortality and transfusion, and **modifiable** — screen ≥4 weeks before elective surgery.
- Iron deficiency: **ferritin <30 µg/L = absolute**; 30–100 µg/L with **TSAT <20%** or inflammation = functional.
- Transfusion: Hb **<70 g/L** usually indicated; **70–100 g/L** assess clinically; **>100 g/L** usually not indicated.
- ⚑ **Single unit, then reassess** — transfusion is a dose, not a number of bags.

**Workup:** FBC, film, ferritin, transferrin saturation, CRP, B12/folate, renal function, reticulocytes. Do not treat and stop there — **iron deficiency anaemia in an adult male or postmenopausal woman mandates investigation for a GI source**; the operation may itself be the treatment.

**Treatment.** Oral iron if there is time (≥6 weeks) and it is tolerated — ⚑ **alternate-day dosing improves absorption** by avoiding hepcidin induction. **IV iron** (ferric carboxymaltose) where surgery is sooner, oral iron is not tolerated or absorbed (IBD, coeliac, bariatric surgery, ongoing loss), or in functional deficiency with inflammation; watch for hypophosphataemia. Treat B12/folate deficiency. ESAs selectively, with concurrent iron.

⚑ **PREVENTT** found no reduction in transfusion or death from a single IV iron dose close to major abdominal surgery (readmissions fell). The lesson is timing, not futility — correct anaemia **early enough for erythropoiesis to occur**, not as a token infusion the week before.

**Minimising loss and optimising tolerance:** meticulous haemostasis, **tranexamic acid** where significant blood loss is expected (established in trauma, cardiac, orthopaedic and obstetric settings), cell salvage, point-of-care coagulation testing, limited phlebotomy, normothermia, cessation of non-essential antithrombotics. Restrictive transfusion is at least as safe as liberal in most stable surgical populations; active bleeding, ACS and symptomatic patients need individual judgement.
