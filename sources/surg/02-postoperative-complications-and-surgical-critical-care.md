# Surgery Notes — Section 2: Postoperative Complications and Surgical Critical Care

*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines, AMH, ACSQHC Sepsis Clinical Care Standard (2022) and HAC list, ANZICS, ANZCA/FPM acute pain guidance and the AAGBI-derived LAST protocol, ERAS Society, RACS, NICE CG174/CG32, THANZ, Australian crystalloid PI. Scope: complications of major abdominal and general surgery in adults, plus fluids, nutrition, analgesia and sepsis.*

## 2.1 The post-operative fever ladder by day

**The short version**
- **Day 0–2**: almost always the **inflammatory (cytokine/IL-6) response to tissue injury** — proportional to operative magnitude, self-limiting. Examine; do not pan-culture reflexively.
- **Day 3–5**: **infection begins to dominate** — UTI (catheter-associated), then pneumonia, then IV cannula site.
- **Day 5–7**: **surgical site infection**, and after bowel surgery **anastomotic leak** — the fever you must not miss.
- **Beyond day 7**: **deep/organ-space collection or abscess**, **VTE**, prosthetic or line infection, *C. difficile*, drug fever.
- ⚑ **Atelectasis does not cause fever.** Repeated studies — and a 2024 review framed as debunking the teaching — find no association between radiological atelectasis and pyrexia. It causes **hypoxia**, not temperature.

### The 5 Ws (a hook, not a differential)

| W | Cause | Typical day |
|---|---|---|
| **Wind** | Pneumonia/aspiration (⚑ **not** atelectasis) | 3–5 |
| **Water** | UTI, catheter-associated | 3–5 |
| **Walking** | DVT/PE | 5+ (any time) |
| **Wound** | SSI, then leak/collection | 5–7 |
| **Wonder drugs** | Drug fever, transfusion reaction, TPN line sepsis | any |

⚑ **Fever <24 h** raises **necrotising soft-tissue infection**, **malignant hyperthermia** and **transfusion reaction** — the early fevers that kill. **Fever after day 7 in a patient who "isn't quite right"** is a collection until imaged.

### Worked stem: "Day 3 post laparotomy, temperature 38.6°C"

Day 3 sits at the pivot from inflammatory to infective. Reason in this order:

1. **Is she septic?** Vitals, perfusion, conscious state — tachycardia or hypotension out of proportion changes the tempo entirely (§2.14).
2. **Anastomotic leak** — if bowel was joined, exclude it even though day 3 is early: peritonism, failure to progress, unresolving ileus, new AF.
3. **Chest** — pneumonia/aspiration: hypoxia, focal signs, purulent sputum. **4. Urine** — catheter *in situ*? Dysuria after removal?
5. **Wound** — take the dressing down and *look*. **6. Lines** — every cannula site; phlebitis is under-diagnosed.
7. **Legs** — asymmetric calf swelling. **8. Drug chart** — new antibiotic, transfusion, heparin.

**First investigations**: FBC, CRP, U&E, LFT, **lactate**, **two sets of blood cultures** (before antibiotics if no delay results), urine MCS, CXR — and with peritonism, instability or a bowel anastomosis, **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast**. ⚑ After colorectal surgery a **falling CRP by days 3–5 has high negative predictive value for leak**; a rising or plateauing CRP with fever mandates CT.

## 2.2 Respiratory complications

**The short version**
- **Atelectasis**: near-universal after GA — hypoxia, basal creps, reduced air entry; resolves with expansion. ⚑ Not a cause of fever.
- **Pneumonia**: fever *plus* purulent sputum, new consolidation, neutrophilia/CRP rise, worsening gas exchange — usually day ≥3.
- **Aspiration**: emergency/unfasted surgery, impaired consciousness, NGT, post-extubation; dependent-segment infiltrate.
- Prevention is mechanical and behavioural: sitting up, **early mobilisation**, deep breathing/incentive spirometry, physiotherapy, **analgesia good enough to allow coughing**, smoking cessation.

| Feature | Atelectasis | Pneumonia | Aspiration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours–day 2 | Day ≥3 | Minutes–hours after the event |
| Fever | ⚑ Not attributable | Yes | Chemical pneumonitis may give early fever |
| CXR | Basal volume loss, raised hemidiaphragm | New consolidation, air bronchograms | Dependent-segment infiltrate |
| Bloods | Normal | Neutrophilia, CRP rise | Late rise only if infection supervenes |
| Treatment | Physio, mobilisation | Antibiotics | Suction and support; ⚑ antibiotics only if infection supervenes |

**Ventilator-associated pneumonia**: arises **>48 h after intubation** — new infiltrate, fever, purulent secretions, leucocytosis, worsening oxygenation. Bundle: head-up 30–45°, daily sedation interruption and extubation assessment, oral care, subglottic suction.

**Post-op hypoxia — causes**: opioid/anaesthetic **hypoventilation**, residual neuromuscular blockade, **atelectasis/shunt**, pneumonia, aspiration, **pulmonary oedema**, **PE**, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, splinting from pain, obesity/OSA — plus **anaemia and low cardiac output**, impairing oxygen delivery with a normal SpO₂.

## 2.3 Venous thromboembolism after surgery

**The short version**
- **DVT**: unilateral calf/thigh swelling, tenderness, warmth. **PE**: pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, tachycardia, unexplained hypoxia, syncope, new AF.
- ⚑ **D-dimer is useless post-operatively** — raised by surgery, inflammation and haematoma. Do not use it to exclude VTE; **image directly**.
- **CTPA** for suspected PE; **compression duplex ultrasound** for suspected DVT.
- **Surgery-provoked VTE is anticoagulated for 3 months**, then stopped.

**Wells (PE)**: DVT signs (3), PE most likely diagnosis (3), HR >100 (1.5), immobilisation ≥3 days **or surgery within 4 weeks** (1.5), previous VTE (1.5), haemoptysis (1), malignancy (1). Two-level: **>4 = likely** (image); **≤4 = unlikely** (D-dimer *in the general population*). ⚑ Post-operatively everyone scores for recent surgery and the D-dimer arm is invalid — **CTPA is the answer**. Wells (DVT) similarly weights cancer, immobilisation, **surgery within 12 weeks**, deep-vein tenderness and calf ≥3 cm larger.

| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| VTE, bleeding risk acceptable | Therapeutic **LMWH (enoxaparin)** or DOAC; LMWH preferred immediately post-op and in cancer |
| VTE, high bleeding risk / immediate post-op | **Unfractionated heparin infusion** — short half-life, titratable, reversible with protamine. This is the point of UFH |
| Anticoagulation absolutely contraindicated | **Retrievable IVC filter** |
| Massive PE with instability | ⚑ Recent major surgery contraindicates systemic thrombolysis — consider catheter-directed therapy or embolectomy |

**IVC filter** — only for **acute proximal DVT/PE in whom anticoagulation cannot be given**, or recurrence despite therapeutic anticoagulation. ⚑ It **treats nothing**: PREPIC2 showed no mortality benefit added to anticoagulation, and filters thrombose. Use a **retrievable** device and **remove it once anticoagulation can start**.

## 2.4 Wound complications

**The short version**
- **SSI** is **superficial incisional / deep incisional / organ-space**, diagnosed within **30 days** (**90 days** where an implant remains).
- Timing suggests organism: **<48 h** *Strep pyogenes* or *C. perfringens*; **days 3–7** *Staph aureus* and skin flora; **days 5–7+** enteric Gram-negatives and anaerobes after colorectal surgery.
- **Dehiscence** classically **day 7–10**; the herald sign is **pink serous ("salmon") discharge** from an intact-looking wound.
- **Evisceration**: cover with **saline-soaked sterile gauze**, resuscitate, NBM, analgesia, antibiotics, **theatre**.
- **Necrotising fasciitis is a clinical and surgical diagnosis** — **debridement, not antibiotics alone**.

| Level | Involves | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Superficial incisional** | Skin, subcutaneous tissue | Open and drain, swab, dress; antibiotics only for cellulitis/systemic features |
| **Deep incisional** | Fascia, muscle | Open, explore, debride; antibiotics; exclude fascial dehiscence |
| **Organ-space** | Any structure opened at surgery | **CT then drainage** ± antibiotics (§2.7) |

Principle: **drainage beats antibiotics** — a wound with pus is opened, not escalated antimicrobially; empirical therapy follows *Therapeutic Guidelines* by site and operated viscus.

**Dehiscence risk factors.** *Pre-op*: age, malnutrition/hypoalbuminaemia, obesity, diabetes, malignancy, jaundice, uraemia, steroids, smoking. *Intra-op*: emergency surgery, midline incision, inadequate fascial bites, contamination. *Post-op*: **raised intra-abdominal pressure** (coughing, vomiting, ileus, straining, ascites), infection, haematoma.

**Immediate management of a burst abdomen**: stay with the patient; NBM, IV access, fluids, analgesia, antibiotics; **cover exposed bowel with saline-soaked gauze** (do not push it back); NGT if distended; **urgent theatre for washout and mass closure**. Incisional hernia follows — warn the patient.

### Necrotising fasciitis vs cellulitis

| Cue | Cellulitis | Necrotising fasciitis |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Proportional | **Out of proportion**, then anaesthesia as nerves die |
| Progression | Hours–days | **Hours** — mark the margin, re-check |
| Systemic state | Usually well | **Toxic**: tachycardia, hypotension, confusion, organ dysfunction |
| Skin | Erythema, warmth | Dusky/violaceous, bullae, **crepitus**, necrosis (late) |
| Antibiotics | Improves | **Deteriorates despite them** |

**LRINEC**: CRP ≥150 (4); WCC 15–25 (1) or >25 (2); Hb 110–135 (1) or <110 (2); Na <135 (2); creatinine >141 µmol/L (2); glucose >10 mmol/L (1). ⚑ Supportive only — **≥6** raises suspicion, a low score **does not exclude** it, and neither score nor imaging may delay theatre. **Treatment**: resuscitate, **urgent radical debridement** (repeated at 24–48 h), plus **broad-spectrum antimicrobials** with an antitoxin agent (**clindamycin**, against streptococcal exotoxin). ⚑ **Antibiotics alone cannot work** — devitalised tissue is not perfused, so the drug never arrives.

## 2.5 Anastomotic leak

**The short version**
- Classic timing **post-operative day 5–7** (range ~3–10; later leaks occur).
- **Subtle before it is dramatic**: unexplained **tachycardia**, **new AF**, **failure to progress**, **unresolving ileus**, low-grade fever, oliguria, delirium.
- Highest risk: **low rectal/colorectal** and **oesophagojejunal** anastomoses. Investigation: **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV ± rectal contrast**.
- Management spans **antibiotics + percutaneous drainage** (contained) to **laparotomy, washout and defunctioning/takedown** (peritonitis or instability).

**Risk factors**: technical (tension, poor blood supply, low anastomosis); patient (malnutrition, hypoalbuminaemia, smoking, diabetes, obesity, steroids, CKD); disease (emergency surgery, contamination, obstruction, pelvic radiotherapy); peri-operative (hypotension/vasopressors, transfusion, ⚑ **NSAIDs**, linked to colorectal leak — many Australian units restrict them).

| Picture | Management |
|---|---|
| Radiological/subclinical, well | Antibiotics, bowel rest, observation, repeat imaging |
| Contained collection, stable | Antibiotics + **CT-guided percutaneous drainage** |
| Peritonitis, sepsis, instability | **Resuscitate → theatre**: washout, defunctioning stoma ± takedown (Hartmann's) |

### Worked stem: "6 days post anterior resection — abdominal pain, fever, tachycardia"

1. **Day 6 + colorectal anastomosis + fever + tachycardia = leak until proven otherwise** — day 6 is the modal day and this is the highest-risk anastomosis.
2. **Tachycardia is the earliest reliable sign.** Persistent unexplained tachycardia after bowel surgery is a leak signal — do not explain it away as pain or dehydration.
3. **Assess for peritonitis and shock**: guarding, rebound, rigidity; BP, urine output, lactate, conscious state.
4. **Resuscitate in parallel with diagnosis**: IV access, crystalloid, blood cultures, empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics, catheter, NBM, analgesia, **senior review**.
5. **Investigate**: FBC, U&E, **CRP** (⚑ failure to fall by day 3–5 is itself a red flag), coags, **lactate**, group and hold, and **CT with IV ± rectal contrast** for extraluminal gas, contrast extravasation, free fluid, perianastomotic collection.
6. **Branch**: peritonitic or unstable → **theatre**; localised collection and stable → **percutaneous drainage** plus antibiotics. ⚑ Do not wait for a better test — an unstable peritonitic patient goes to theatre without a CT.

## 2.6 Post-operative ileus vs mechanical obstruction

**The short version**
- **Ileus** = functional failure of propulsion; **prolonged POI** when it persists beyond ~3–4 days or forces NGT reinsertion.
- Ileus is **generalised and quiet**; early obstruction is **colicky and noisy**. The best bedside discriminator is **passage of flatus**.
- **CT with contrast** decides it: ileus = diffuse dilatation, **no transition point**; obstruction = **transition point** with collapsed distal bowel.
- Prevention is the **ERAS bundle**; a "stubborn ileus" is a **leak or collection** until excluded.

| Feature | Ileus | Early post-op mechanical obstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Continuous from surgery | Often after an **initial return of function**, then relapse (day 5–14) |
| Pain | Constant distension discomfort | **Colicky**, crescendo |
| Bowel sounds | Absent/hypoactive | **Hyperactive, tinkling** early; absent late |
| Flatus | Slowly returns | **Absent** |
| CT | Dilated small **and** large bowel, gas to rectum, no transition | **Transition point**, collapsed distal bowel |
| Cause | Handling, opioids, electrolytes, inflammation | Adhesions, internal or port-site hernia, anastomotic stricture, twisted stoma |

**Correctable contributors**: **opioids**, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia, uraemia, sepsis/collection, anticholinergics, immobility, **fluid overload** (bowel wall oedema).

**Prevention (ERAS)**: minimally invasive access, **no routine NGT or drains**, **opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia** and regional techniques, **goal-directed not liberal fluids**, early oral intake, **early mobilisation**, early catheter removal, **chewing gum**. ⚑ Alvimopan is not PBS-listed and is not routine Australian practice.

**Management**: NBM or clear fluids, NGT for symptom relief if vomiting, IV fluids with **K⁺ and Mg²⁺ replacement**, minimise opioids, mobilise — and **image if it fails to resolve or fever/tachycardia appear**. **Suspect early mechanical obstruction** when function returns then fails, or pain turns colicky; this favours earlier operation, because internal and port-site hernias strangulate.

## 2.7 Intra-abdominal collection and abscess

**The short version**
- Presents **day 5 onward**: swinging fever, tachycardia, anorexia, failure to progress, ileus, rising WCC/CRP.
- Site clues: **subphrenic** — shoulder-tip pain, hiccups, basal effusion; **pelvic** — diarrhoea, tenesmus, urinary frequency, boggy mass on PR; **paracolic** — local tenderness.
- **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast** is the investigation; ultrasound helps for pelvic/subphrenic collections.
- **Drainage is the treatment**, antibiotics adjunctive. Always ask **why** — a collection after bowel surgery implies a leak until excluded.

**Image-guided percutaneous drainage** is first line for an accessible unilocular collection, sparing a re-laparotomy; send fluid for **MCS** and narrow antibiotics. Operate if it is inaccessible or multiloculated, drainage fails, there is an ongoing source, or there is peritonitis or instability. Pelvic collections may be drained **transrectally**; re-image if fevers persist.

## 2.8 Post-operative acute kidney injury

**The short version**
- The dominant mechanism is **pre-renal**: hypovolaemia, blood loss, third-spacing, sepsis, intra-operative hypotension, low cardiac output.
- **Exclude post-renal first with a bladder scan** — retention and a blocked catheter are the quickest reversible causes.
- ⚑ Stop the nephrotoxins: **NSAIDs, ACEI/ARBs, aminoglycosides, diuretics**, avoidable contrast — dose-adjust metformin, morphine, enoxaparin.
- **KDIGO**: a creatinine rise, or urine output **<0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 h**. Treat the cause — **fluid treats hypovolaemia, not oliguria per se.**

| Category | Post-op examples | Bedside test |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-renal** | Hypovolaemia, haemorrhage, third-spacing, sepsis, cardiac failure | Tachycardia, low JVP, response to fluid challenge; concentrated urine |
| **Renal** | ATN from prolonged hypoperfusion, nephrotoxins, contrast, rhabdomyolysis | Muddy brown casts, CK, urinalysis |
| **Post-renal** | **Retention**, blocked IDC, ureteric injury (pelvic surgery), clot retention | **Bladder scan**; renal tract ultrasound |

**AKI vs urinary retention**: retention gives **suprapubic discomfort, an urge to void, a palpable bladder and a large scanned volume**, and catheterisation gives immediate high-volume drainage with resolution; oliguric AKI has an **empty bladder**. Hence the bladder scan precedes the fluid bolus. After relieving chronic retention watch for **post-obstructive diuresis**. Escalate for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload or uraemic complications.

## 2.9 Post-operative delirium

**The short version**
- Common, under-recognised, an **ACSQHC hospital-acquired complication**; high incidence after hip fracture, emergency and cardiac surgery, and in the frail elderly.
- **Predisposing**: age, **pre-existing cognitive impairment**, frailty, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, alcohol, prior delirium.
- **Precipitants**: pain, opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, **hypoxia**, sepsis, **retention and constipation**, electrolyte disturbance, dehydration, anaemia, sleep disruption, restraints.
- **Prevention is multicomponent and non-pharmacological**: orientation, sensory aids, sleep hygiene, early mobilisation, hydration, bowel and bladder care, deprescribing.
- **Management rule: delirium is treated by finding and fixing the cause.** Antipsychotics do not treat it — last resort only, lowest dose, shortest time, avoided first-line in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia.

⚑ In a surgical patient, new delirium is often the **presenting feature of a surgical complication** — hypoxia from pneumonia or PE, sepsis from a leak or collection, AKI, retention. Screen (4AT), examine, investigate — then consider drugs. **Cross-reference: the Geriatrics notes carry the screening tools, the delirium-vs-dementia-vs-depression table and the full pathway.**

## 2.10 Fluids and electrolytes in surgery

**The short version**
- Routine maintenance: **water 25–30 mL/kg/day**, **Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻ ~1 mmol/kg/day each**, plus **50–100 g/day glucose** to limit starvation ketosis.
- **Resuscitation is a different prescription**: **500 mL crystalloid over <15 min**, reassess, repeat.
- ⚑ Large-volume **0.9% saline causes hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis**; balanced crystalloids are preferred for most surgical resuscitation and replacement.
- ⚑ **The examinable exception**: in **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** from vomiting or gastric outlet obstruction, the correct fluid is **0.9% sodium chloride with added potassium — not Hartmann's**.
- **Over-resuscitation harms**: bowel wall oedema and ileus, anastomotic compromise, pulmonary oedema — ERAS targets **near-zero fluid balance**.

| Fluid | Na⁺ | K⁺ | Cl⁻ | Other | Glucose | Osmolarity (mOsm/L) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0.9% sodium chloride** | 154 | — | **154** | — | — | ~308 |
| **Hartmann's (compound Na lactate)** | 131 | 5 | 112 | lactate 29, Ca²⁺ 2 | — | ~278 (measured osmolality ~254) |
| **Plasma-Lyte 148** | 140 | 5 | 98 | acetate 27, gluconate 23, Mg²⁺ 1.5; ⚑ **no calcium** | — | ~295 |
| **5% dextrose** | — | — | — | — | 50 g/L | ~278 |
| **4% dextrose / 0.18% NaCl** | 30 | — | 30 | — | 40 g/L | ~283 |

**5% dextrose is free water** once metabolised — useless for resuscitation, a cause of iatrogenic hyponatraemia. **Hartmann's contains potassium**, so avoid as sole fluid in significant hyperkalaemia. ⚑ **Plasma-Lyte contains no calcium** — the balanced solution compatible with co-administered blood.

### Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis — the mechanism

Prolonged vomiting or gastric outlet obstruction loses **gastric HCl** — **H⁺ and Cl⁻**, with water, Na⁺ and K⁺. Then:
1. Loss of **H⁺** leaves the ECF **alkalotic** — bicarbonate rises.
2. Volume and **chloride** depletion drive **avid renal Na⁺ reabsorption** (hypovolaemia + aldosterone); with chloride unavailable as the accompanying anion, Na⁺ is reabsorbed **in exchange for K⁺ and H⁺**.
3. That exchange **wastes potassium** and **excretes H⁺ into the urine despite systemic alkalosis** — **paradoxical aciduria**.
4. Hypokalaemia itself promotes further H⁺ secretion, so the alkalosis is **self-perpetuating** until chloride, potassium and volume are restored.

⚑ The kidney cannot correct this until given **chloride** — hence **0.9% sodium chloride with potassium**, not Hartmann's, which is chloride-poor and whose lactate becomes **bicarbonate**, worsening the alkalosis. One of the few settings where saline is genuinely correct.

### Worked stem: "Vomiting two days post-op; Na 138, Cl 92, HCO₃ 34, K 3.0"

- **Interpretation**: metabolic alkalosis (HCO₃ 34) with **hypochloraemia** (92) and **hypokalaemia** (3.0), sodium apparently normal — classic gastric losses; the chloride deficit is the story.
- **Confirm**: VBG (raised pH, compensatory pCO₂ rise) and **urinary chloride** — **<20 mmol/L** confirms **saline-responsive (chloride-depletion) alkalosis**; the urine is **acidic**.
- **Ask why he is vomiting.** Two days post-op is not automatically gastric outlet obstruction — consider **ileus**, **mechanical obstruction** and **anastomotic leak**; examine, and image if plausible.
- **Treat**: NBM, **NGT** if obstructed or vomiting large volumes, and **IV 0.9% sodium chloride with potassium chloride** (peripherally, typically ≤10 mmol/h and ≤40 mmol/L; faster only centrally with cardiac monitoring — check local protocol and AMH). **Replace magnesium** — hypomagnesaemia makes hypokalaemia refractory. Chart losses, recheck U&E, treat the cause.

### Third-space loss, hyponatraemia, refeeding

**Third-space loss** — fluid sequestered into gut lumen, bowel wall, peritoneum and traumatised tissue; functionally lost from the circulation, greatest in the first 24–48 h after major surgery, peritonitis or pancreatitis, then **mobilised back around days 3–5** with a diuresis (and pulmonary oedema if over-filled). Replace with **balanced crystalloid** titrated to perfusion, urine output and lactate.

**Post-op hyponatraemia** is usually **non-osmotic ADH release** (pain, nausea, opioids, surgical stress) plus **hypotonic fluid**; prevent with **isotonic** maintenance fluid. ⚑ Correct chronic hyponatraemia slowly — ceiling **8–10 mmol/L in 24 h** to avoid osmotic demyelination; hypertonic saline only for severe symptoms under senior/ICU guidance.

**Refeeding syndrome** — in the malnourished (very low BMI, marked weight loss, prolonged minimal intake, alcohol misuse, low baseline K/PO₄/Mg), reintroduced carbohydrate drives insulin-mediated intracellular shift of **phosphate, potassium and magnesium** and consumes thiamine, causing arrhythmias, cardiac failure, seizures and Wernicke's. **Prevent**: **start low** (order of 10 kcal/kg/day), build over days, give **thiamine and B vitamins before and during** early feeding, replace **PO₄, K⁺, Mg²⁺** daily.

## 2.11 Nutrition in the surgical patient

**The short version**
- **If the gut works, use it** — enteral feeding preserves mucosal integrity and causes fewer infective complications than parenteral.
- ⚑ **Prolonged post-operative starvation is obsolete**; ERAS supports **early oral intake**, often within 24 h of colorectal surgery — bowel sounds are not a prerequisite.
- **TPN is for a gut that cannot be used or cannot absorb**, not one that is merely inconvenient.
- Assess **before** operating: weight loss, BMI, intake history, muscle bulk/handgrip, a validated tool (MST/MUST/SGA). ⚑ Albumin reflects inflammation more than nutrition — never use it alone.
- Malnutrition is itself an **ACSQHC hospital-acquired complication**, driving leak, SSI, dehiscence and death.

| Route | Use when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral ± supplements** | Functioning gut, safe swallow | Whether intake is actually adequate — chart it |
| **Enteral (NG/NJ, PEG/PEJ)** | Gut works, intake inadequate or unsafe | **Confirm tube position before feeding**, aspiration, diarrhoea, blockage, refeeding |
| **Parenteral (TPN, central)** | Intestinal failure: short bowel, high-output fistula, prolonged ileus, complete obstruction, severe malabsorption | **Line sepsis**, thrombosis, hyperglycaemia, electrolyte derangement, refeeding, hepatic steatosis/cholestasis |

**TPN discipline**: dedicated lumen, strict asepsis, dietitian-led prescription, daily U&E/BSL with regular LFT/PO₄/Mg — and **fever on TPN is line sepsis until proven otherwise**. Return to enteral feeding once the gut recovers.

## 2.12 Surgical drains and tubes of the abdomen

**The short version**
- Drains do not prevent complications — ⚑ ERAS discourages **routine prophylactic drainage** after colorectal surgery.
- **Open** (Penrose, corrugated) drain passively, higher infection risk; **closed suction** (Redivac, Blake) are the usual abdominal drains; **pigtail catheters** drain abscesses.
- Output **character** is diagnostic: serous → expected; **bilious/green** → bile leak; **feculent/turbid brown** → **anastomotic leak**; **frank blood at volume** → bleeding, escalate; **milky** → chyle; **urine** → ureteric/bladder injury.
- **Trend beats a single number** — a sudden rise *or* a sudden stop (possibly a blocked drain) needs assessment. Remove once output is low and the indication has passed.
- Send fluid for **MCS**, plus **amylase/lipase**, **bilirubin** or **creatinine** to confirm a pancreatic, biliary or urinary leak.
- ⚑ **A dry drain does not exclude a leak** — leaks track away from drains, and no drain substitutes for examining the patient or for CT. **Stomas are in Section 4.**

## 2.13 Post-operative analgesia

**The short version**
- **Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia** is the ANZCA/FPM standard — paracetamol, NSAIDs where safe, regional techniques and adjuncts, so opioid requirement falls.
- The WHO ladder is **inverted** for acute surgical pain: start strong, step **down**; **regular paracetamol is the foundation**, charted regularly, not PRN.
- **PCA** gives patient-triggered IV boluses with a lockout; ⚑ **no background infusion in the opioid-naive**, and **nobody else presses the button** — the patient's own sedation is the safeguard.
- Monitor **sedation score, not just respiratory rate** — sedation precedes respiratory depression.

| Step | Agents |
|---|---|
| **Foundation (always)** | **Paracetamol** regularly ± **NSAID** (caution: renal impairment, hypovolaemia, GI bleeding, elderly, ⚑ colorectal anastomosis) |
| **Mild–moderate** | Oral opioid (oxycodone; tramadol with care — serotonin toxicity, seizures) |
| **Severe / immediate post-op** | **IV opioid titration → PCA**, and/or **regional or neuraxial** |
| **Adjuncts** | Low-dose ketamine, lignocaine infusion, clonidine, TAP/rectus sheath blocks, wound infiltration |

⚑ **Discharge opioid stewardship**: **small quantities, immediate-release only, with a stop plan** — slow-release opioids are not recommended for acute post-operative pain.

| Complication | Recognise it by | Do |
|---|---|---|
| **Opioid respiratory depression** | **Rising sedation score** first, then slow shallow breathing, then falling SpO₂ (late, masked by oxygen), miosis | Stop opioid, oxygen, stimulate, support airway, **titrated IV naloxone** — short half-life, repeat or infuse |
| **Epidural haematoma / abscess** | ⚑ **Increasing or ascending motor block, block denser than expected, block not regressing after the infusion stops, new back pain, bladder/bowel dysfunction, fever** | **Stop the infusion, urgent MRI, urgent neurosurgical referral** — decompression within hours determines outcome; check coags and anticoagulant timing (Section 1) |

**LAST** — perioral tingling, metallic taste, tinnitus, agitation → seizures → bradycardia, arrhythmia, **arrest**. Management: **stop injecting**, call for help, **100% oxygen** and airway control, benzodiazepine for seizures (⚑ **propofol is not a substitute for lipid emulsion**), CPR if arrested — **prolonged resuscitation may be needed**. **20% lipid emulsion (Intralipid): 1.5 mL/kg bolus over 1 min, then 15 mL/kg/h**; repeat the bolus **up to twice at 5-minute intervals** and **double the infusion to 30 mL/kg/h** if the circulation is not restored; ⚑ **maximum 12 mL/kg**. Avoid **lignocaine** as an antiarrhythmic and vasopressin; give **adrenaline in small increments only** — large doses impair lipid rescue.

## 2.14 Sepsis in the surgical patient

**The short version**
- **Sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection.** Screen with track-and-trigger vital signs plus **lactate**; ⚑ **qSOFA is a poor screen** and is no longer recommended alone.
- ACSQHC framing: **"Could this be sepsis?"** — any clinician can escalate, and escalation must not wait for certainty.
- Australian timings (Sepsis Clinical Care Standard 2022): **blood cultures immediately** if this does not delay treatment; **antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 hour, where shock is present**; if shock is absent but concern persists, **no later than 3 hours from initial clinical review**; **antimicrobial review within 48 hours**.
- **Source control is the definitive treatment** — drainage, debridement, device removal, diversion or resection.
- ⚑ **Antibiotics without source control fail** — drug cannot reach undrained pus or dead tissue, and the patient deteriorates on "appropriate" therapy.

**Ward bundle**: oxygen if hypoxic; **two IV cannulae**; **two sets of blood cultures** plus site-specific specimens; **lactate** with FBC, U&E, LFT, coags, CRP, glucose, VBG; **empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobials per *Therapeutic Guidelines***, adjusted for source, local resistance, allergies and renal function; **balanced crystalloid in reassessed boluses**; **catheterise, measure urine output**; **escalate, consider MET/ICU** — and **control the source**.

**Source control by problem**: abscess → **drainage**; anastomotic leak → **washout ± defunctioning/resection**; necrotising soft-tissue infection → **radical debridement**; infected line → **remove it**; obstructed infected biliary tree or kidney → **ERCP or nephrostomy/stent**; ischaemic bowel → **resection**. ⚑ Achieve it **as soon as the patient is physiologically able** — sometimes the operation *is* the resuscitation.

Afterwards: **de-escalate** on cultures and chart a stop/review date (stewardship is an NSQHS requirement); plan follow-up — post-sepsis morbidity and cognitive effects are explicitly part of the standard.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Fever days 0–2 post-op in a well patient; evidence atelectasis causes fever | **Inflammatory/cytokine response to surgery**; ⚑ **none** — atelectasis causes hypoxia, not pyrexia |
| Commonest infective source of fever on days 3–5 | **UTI** (catheter-associated), then pneumonia, then cannula site |
| Fever on day 5–7 after a bowel anastomosis | **Anastomotic leak** until proven otherwise |
| Earliest sign of leak, and the investigation | **Persistent unexplained tachycardia** (± new AF, unresolving ileus); **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV ± rectal contrast** |
| Value of D-dimer in suspected post-op VTE; sole indication for an IVC filter | **None** — image directly (CTPA/duplex); filter only when **anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated** (provoked VTE treated **3 months**) |
| Classic day of abdominal wound dehiscence, and its herald sign | **Day 7–10**; **pink serous ("salmon") discharge** |
| Necrotising fasciitis vs cellulitis, and its treatment | **Pain out of proportion, toxicity, rapid progression, crepitus, deterioration on antibiotics** → **urgent radical debridement + broad-spectrum antibiotics incl. clindamycin**, never antibiotics alone |
| Ileus vs mechanical obstruction on CT | Ileus = **no transition point**; obstruction = **transition point, collapsed distal bowel** |
| Adult routine maintenance fluid prescription | **25–30 mL/kg/day water; ~1 mmol/kg/day Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻; 50–100 g/day glucose** |
| Na⁺/Cl⁻ in 0.9% saline vs Hartmann's | **154/154** vs **131/111** (Hartmann's also K⁺ 5, Ca²⁺ 2, lactate 29) |
| ABG picture of prolonged vomiting or gastric outlet obstruction | **Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria** |
| Correct fluid for that picture | ⚑ **0.9% sodium chloride with added potassium** — *not* Hartmann's |
| First test in the post-op patient not passing urine | **Bladder scan** — exclude retention/blocked catheter before the fluid bolus |
| Rule for managing post-operative delirium | **Find and treat the cause**; antipsychotics are last-resort, not treatment |
| Intralipid regimen for LAST | **1.5 mL/kg bolus over 1 min → 15 mL/kg/h**; ≤2 repeat boluses at 5 min; double to 30 mL/kg/h; **max 12 mL/kg** |
| Australian sepsis antimicrobial timing | ⚑ **Within 60 min of recognising infection-related organ dysfunction** (i.e. sepsis, shock or not), and as soon as possible if shock. The **3-hour** limit applies only where there is genuine **diagnostic uncertainty** about infection** from initial clinical review |
| Why antibiotics fail in surgical sepsis | **No source control** — undrained pus and dead tissue are not perfused |
