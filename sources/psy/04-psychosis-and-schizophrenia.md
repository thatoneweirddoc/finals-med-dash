# Psychiatry Notes — Section 4: Psychosis and Schizophrenia
*Sources: **ANZJP GRADE guidelines for the management of schizophrenia (Suetani S, Dark F, Every-Palmer S, Galletly C, … Siskind D; ANZJP 2026) — these supersede the 2016 RANZCP CPG; INTEGRATE international algorithmic guidelines (McCutcheon R, Pillinger T et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2025, RANZCP-hosted); Warren N et al., Initial physical health assessment for psychosis in Australia and New Zealand: 2026 recommendations (ANZJP 2026)**, Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, Australian clozapine monitoring requirements (TGA-approved manufacturer programs and state protocols), Australian Prescriber, RACGP, Orygen/headspace, Lancet Psychiatry clozapine consensus (2025). Scope: psychosis as a syndrome, schizophrenia and its differentials, neurobiology, first-episode workup, antipsychotics and their emergencies, clozapine, catatonia, psychosocial care and prognosis.*

## 4.1 What psychosis is

**The short version**
- Psychosis = **loss of contact with consensual reality** — a syndrome, not a diagnosis; every presentation needs a cause found (§4.4).
- Four domains: **positive** (added), **negative** (subtracted), **cognitive**, **disorganised**.
- **Negative and cognitive symptoms determine long-term function**; positive symptoms determine admission.
- Name the negative symptoms precisely — a standard written-paper mark.
- **Visual, olfactory or tactile hallucinations point organic** until proven otherwise.

**Positive.** *Delusions* — fixed false beliefs, held with conviction, not culturally shared, impervious to counter-evidence. Persecutory commonest; also referential, grandiose, nihilistic, somatic, and the schizophrenia-flavoured **passivity phenomena** (thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcast; made acts, feelings, impulses). *Hallucinations* — percept without stimulus; **auditory** dominates in primary psychotic illness (third-person commentary, voices conversing), formication is classic in stimulant use.

**Disorganised.** Formal thought disorder — derailment, tangentiality, circumstantiality, neologisms, thought block, word salad — plus disorganised behaviour and catatonia (§4.8).

**Negative — learn all five.** **Avolition** (loss of self-initiated goal-directed activity — not laziness); **alogia** (poverty of speech and of content of speech); **anhedonia** (more a deficit of *anticipatory* than consummatory pleasure); **asociality** (reduced interest in and initiation of contact, distinct from paranoid avoidance); **blunted/flat affect** (reduced facial, prosodic and gestural expression).

⚑ **Primary vs secondary negatives** is the clinically load-bearing split. Secondary negatives come from depression, drug-induced parkinsonism or sedation, untreated positive symptoms (withdrawing to escape voices), substances, or institutional understimulation — all treatable. Primary (deficit) negatives are intrinsic, persistent and respond poorly to antipsychotics. Hunt the secondary causes first.

**Cognitive.** Working memory, processing speed, attention, verbal learning, executive function and **social cognition** — present at first episode, largely stable rather than progressive.

**Why negatives drive function.** Positive symptoms fluctuate and respond to dopamine blockade; negative and cognitive symptoms are drug-refractory, persist between episodes, and predict employment and independent living. *Treat positives with drugs and negatives with everything else* (§4.9).

## 4.2 Schizophrenia

**The short version**
- Positive, negative, disorganised and cognitive features with **functional decline**; **≥6 months** of continuous disturbance including **≥1 month** of active-phase symptoms.
- Preceded by a **prodrome** of months–years: withdrawal, falling grades/work, odd beliefs, attenuated perceptual disturbance.
- Lifetime risk ~**0.3–0.7%**; incidence ~**15/100,000/year**; **men onset late teens–mid 20s, women mid 20s–early 30s with a second peri-menopausal peak**.
- Differentials separate on three questions: **duration**, **mood–psychosis relationship**, **function outside the delusion**.
- **SMR 2–3×**, life expectancy shortened ~**15–20 years** — mostly cardiovascular; ~**5%** die by suicide.

**The picture.** A young person, often male, drifts over a year or two — withdraws, drops out of study, sleeps by day, becomes preoccupied and irritable, "not themselves." Then frank psychosis: beliefs about being watched or targeted, voices commenting on actions, passivity experiences, speech hard to follow. The acute phase settles with treatment; what remains is the flattening — less speech, less drive, less contact, the degree never resumed. Course is **relapsing–remitting on accumulated functional loss**, with the first 2–5 years setting the trajectory — hence early intervention (§4.4).

⚑ Modern classifications abolished the old subtypes (paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic, simple); catatonia is now a *specifier* across diagnoses (§4.8) — don't use subtype labels as current. Male:female incidence ~1.4:1; women have later onset, more affective colouring, better short-term outcome. Australian SHIP data (2010): **1-month treated prevalence ~3.1/1000 and 12-month treated prevalence ~4.5/1000** adults aged 18–64 in contact with services; **only 21.5% currently employed**, 66.1% currently smoking, 54.8% with metabolic syndrome.

| Differential | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Schizoaffective disorder** | Mood episode present for **most of total illness duration**, *but* **≥2 weeks of psychosis with mood symptoms absent** — that mood-free window is the test |
| **Schizophreniform disorder** | Same picture, duration **1–6 months**; decline not required. ~1/3 recover, ~2/3 progress |
| **Brief psychotic disorder** | **≥1 day to <1 month**, then **full return to premorbid function**; often stressor-precipitated |
| **Delusional disorder** | **≥1 month** of delusions, **no prominent hallucinations, disorganisation or negatives**, **function outside the delusional theme preserved** |
| **Psychotic depression** | Psychosis **only within** depressive episodes, usually mood-congruent — guilt, poverty, nihilism (§2.1) |
| **Mania with psychosis** | Psychosis confined to mania; elevated/irritable mood, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech dominate |
| **Substance-induced** | Onset in intoxication/withdrawal, **resolves within ~1 month of abstinence** |

## 4.3 Aetiology and neurobiology

**The short version**
- **Dopamine hypothesis**: excess **mesolimbic** D2 transmission → positive symptoms; blockade of three other pathways generates the entire side-effect profile.
- Refined: presynaptic dopamine dysregulation in the **associative striatum** → **aberrant salience** — random stimuli feel urgently significant, and delusions are the explanation built around that.
- **Glutamate/NMDA hypofunction** explains what dopamine cannot — ketamine/PCP reproduce all domains, amphetamine only the positive.
- **Heritability ~80%**, highly polygenic; strongest signal the **complement C4/MHC** region (synaptic pruning).
- **Neurodevelopmental model**: early insult sets vulnerability; adolescent pruning and myelination unmask it.

| Pathway | Role | Effect of D2 blockade |
|---|---|---|
| **Mesolimbic** (VTA→n. accumbens) | Salience, reward | **Therapeutic** — reduces positive symptoms |
| **Mesocortical** (VTA→PFC) | Motivation, executive, affect | **Worsens negative/cognitive** symptoms — already hypoactive |
| **Nigrostriatal** (SN→dorsal striatum) | Motor control | **EPS** — dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, TD (§4.5) |
| **Tuberoinfundibular** (hypothalamus→pituitary) | Dopamine tonically **inhibits** prolactin | **Hyperprolactinaemia** |

Narrow window: ~**65–70% striatal D2 occupancy** for effect, **>80%** for EPS — escalating past response buys side effects, not efficacy.

**Glutamate.** NMDA antagonism on **cortical GABAergic interneurons** disinhibits glutamatergic output and secondarily dysregulates subcortical dopamine — placing glutamate upstream of dopamine and giving a mechanism for the drug-refractory domains.

**Genetics.** Population lifetime risk ~**0.7%** (conventionally rounded to 1% in the classic family-risk figures); one affected parent ~10%; both ~40%; **MZ twin ~50%** — the gap from 100% is the standing argument for environment. Hundreds of common variants of small effect plus rare high-impact CNVs — **22q11.2 deletion** confers up to ~25% risk; think of it with palatal, cardiac or learning problems.

**Environment.** **Cannabis** is the one most likely asked: dose-dependent. **Ever use raises risk ~40% (adjusted OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–1.65); the most frequent users roughly double it (OR 2.09, 1.54–2.84)** (Moore 2007). In EU-GEI, **daily use OR ~3.2** and **daily high-potency (THC ≥10%) use OR ~4.8** vs never-users (Di Forti 2019), and **adolescent exposure matters more than adult**. ⚑ Counsel as risk-modifying and modifiable, not deterministic. Also **obstetric complications** (maternal infection, famine, pre-eclampsia, hypoxia, low birth weight), **advanced paternal age**, **migration/minority status** (largest where own-group ethnic density is low — social defeat, not genetics), **urbanicity**, childhood adversity, methamphetamine, winter/spring birth.

**Neurodevelopmental model.** Genetic liability plus pre-/perinatal insult disturbs migration and connectivity; silent through childhood beyond subtle motor, cognitive and social markers; declares in late adolescence when **synaptic pruning and prefrontal myelination** remove the redundancy that had compensated. Structural correlates — enlarged ventricles, reduced temporal/prefrontal grey matter — are ⚑ **group-level, not diagnostic, and not a reason to image routinely**.

## 4.4 First-episode psychosis

**The short version**
- **Duration of untreated psychosis (DUP)** is one of few modifiable outcome predictors — longer DUP, poorer remission, function and relapse rate.
- Australia leads on early intervention: the **EPPIC model** (Orygen/McGorry), scaled as **headspace** and early psychosis services.
- **Every first episode gets an organic workup** — primary psychotic illness is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Atypical features force investigation**: onset <14 or >40, non-auditory hallucinations, fluctuating consciousness, seizures, focal neurology, autonomic instability, catatonia, poor response.
- Dose **low and slow** — neuroleptic-naive patients are far more EPS-sensitive.

**Why DUP matters.** Untreated psychosis costs study, work, relationships and housing, with self-harm and forensic contact accruing. Whether psychosis is itself neurotoxic is contested; that the consequences compound is not — hence rapid referral and services a frightened 19-year-old will actually attend. **headspace** — national youth centres (12–25), free/low-cost, no referral barrier, integrated GP/psychology/psychiatry/vocational care. **EPPIC-model early psychosis services** — 2–5 years of specialised care, case management, low-dose antipsychotic, CBTp, family work, **vocational/educational recovery a core outcome**; gains attenuate if discharged to standard care too early.

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Methamphetamine** | Paranoia, formication, hypertension, mydriasis, bruxism, dental disease; psychosis persisting >1 month after abstinence → reconsider diagnosis |
| **Cannabis / synthetic cannabinoids** | ⚑ Synthetics ("spice") cause severe agitation, seizures, hyperthermia and **do not show on standard urine drug screens** |
| **Delirium** | **Fluctuating attention and consciousness**, disorientation, reversed sleep–wake, visual hallucinations, acute onset |
| **Anti-NMDAR encephalitis** | Young woman, viral prodrome, rapid psychiatric onset then **seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability, reduced GCS**; **ovarian teratoma**. ⚑ Confirm on **CSF** antibodies — serum can be falsely negative |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Stereotyped brief episodes, olfactory/gustatory aura, déjà vu, automatisms, post-ictal confusion |
| **Wilson disease** | **Age <40**, tremor/dystonia/dysarthria, deranged LFTs, Kayser–Fleischer rings → **caeruloplasmin**, 24-h urinary copper |
| **Drugs** | **Corticosteroids** (dose-related), dopamine agonists, isotretinoin, interferon |
| **Infection / endocrine / metabolic** | **HIV, neurosyphilis** (risk history, cognitive decline); hyper- and hypothyroidism; **B12/folate** deficiency |
| **SLE / cerebral vasculitis** | Rash, arthralgia, serositis, cytopenias, renal disease → ANA, ENA, dsDNA |

**Investigations indicated (2026 ANZ recommendations).** Routine for all: **FBC, UEC, LFT, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipids, urine drug screen, ECG (baseline QTc), weight, waist and BP**. **Targeted, not routine: TSH** — thyroid function is no longer a universal baseline test in first-episode psychosis (§1.5); order it only where history or examination raises concern (**prominent mood or anxiety symptoms, unexplained weight change, heat or cold intolerance, goitre, tachycardia**, menstrual, sleep or hair/skin change) — **B12/folate** (guided by dietary/lifestyle assessment and the FBC), **CRP/ESR** (nonspecific and insensitive — order only if other features point that way), **βhCG**, and **HIV/syphilis serology** (offer opportunistically with consent to anyone sexually active, methamphetamine-using or injecting).

⚑ **Neuroimaging is not routine in every first episode** (many Australian services do one by local protocol). It *is* indicated with focal neurology, seizures, head injury, onset <14 or >40, delirium, rapid cognitive decline, catatonia or poor response — **MRI beats CT**. **EEG** if seizures, fluctuating consciousness or catatonia. **LP and autoimmune antibodies only when the picture points that way**, not as a screen. Microarray (22q11.2) if dysmorphism, congenital cardiac disease or intellectual disability.

## 4.5 Antipsychotics

**The short version**
- All effective agents are **D2 antagonists or partial agonists**; second-generation drugs add **5-HT2A antagonism** (aripiprazole/brexpiprazole/cariprazine are **D2 partial agonists**).
- **First vs second generation is a side-effect distinction, not an efficacy one** (clozapine excepted): FGAs → more EPS and prolactin; SGAs → more metabolic harm.
- **Start low, titrate slowly, one drug at a time, review at 4–6 weeks** before declaring failure.
- Choice is driven by **side-effect tolerability, comorbidity and preference**, not class; **offer LAIs early**, not as a punishment for non-adherence.

**Classes.** FGA high-potency (haloperidol, fluphenazine) — more EPS; low-potency (chlorpromazine) — more sedation, hypotension, anticholinergic. SGA — olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride, lurasidone, plus partial agonists.

| | **Acute dystonia** | **Akathisia** | **Parkinsonism** | **Tardive dyskinesia** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | **50% <48 h, 90% <5 days** | Days–weeks, usually **<4 weeks** of start/dose rise | Weeks to ~3 months | **Months–years** (>3 months exposure; >1 month if elderly) |
| **Risk** | Young men, naive, high-potency FGA | Any; commonly missed | Elderly, women, higher dose | Elderly, women, FGAs, diabetes, cumulative dose |
| **Features** | Sustained contraction — **torticollis, trismus, oculogyric crisis, opisthotonus**; **laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency** | **Subjective inner restlessness** + pacing, inability to sit; linked to **suicidality** | **Bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity, symmetrical tremor, masked facies, shuffling gait** (symmetry vs idiopathic PD) | **Choreoathetoid orofacial movements** — lip smacking, tongue protrusion; often persists after withdrawal |
| **Treatment** | **Benztropine 1–2 mg IM/IV** (repeat prn), or promethazine — relief in minutes; airway support; then reduce/switch | **Reduce dose or switch**; **propranolol** first-line adjunct; short-term benzodiazepine; **mirtazapine 7.5–15 mg nocte**. ⚑ **Anticholinergics do not help akathisia** | Reduce dose/switch to a lower-EPS agent; **amantadine** or anticholinergic if switching impossible | ⚑ **Stop the anticholinergic** (worsens TD); reduce slowly/switch — **clozapine** best evidence; **VMAT2 inhibitors** are specific: **deutetrabenazine (Austedo) is TGA-registered in Australia for tardive dyskinesia in adults and for Huntington chorea, but is not PBS-subsidised for TD; valbenazine is not registered in Australia.** Tetrabenazine is available. Abrupt withdrawal can unmask TD |

⚑ **No routine prophylactic anticholinergics** — reserve for high-risk cases and plan withdrawal.

**Other adverse effects.** **Hyperprolactinaemia** — worst with **risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride** and FGAs; **aripiprazole and quetiapine** spare it. Galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, gynaecomastia, sexual dysfunction, **reduced BMD**; manage by dose reduction, switch, or low-dose aripiprazole. **Metabolic syndrome/weight gain** — worst **clozapine and olanzapine**, least aripiprazole/ziprasidone/lurasidone/amisulpride; **fastest in the first 6–12 weeks** and in the naive, so intervene early (**metformin** attenuates it). Monitor to the Australian **Positive Cardiometabolic Health Resource** (Mindgardens/Equally Well 2023) — **weight and waist weekly for the first 6–8 weeks** after starting or changing an antipsychotic, and **all parameters (weight/waist, BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids) at baseline, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months, then 6-monthly**; see §1.7 for the full monitoring table. **QT prolongation** — haloperidol (esp. IV), droperidol, ziprasidone, pimozide, amisulpride; **QTc >500 ms** (or rise >60 ms) → stop/switch, correct K⁺/Mg²⁺. **Sedation** (clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, chlorpromazine) and **sexual dysfunction** are the under-asked drivers of covert non-adherence. **Anticholinergic burden** — dry mouth, blurred vision, retention, constipation, **delirium in the elderly**. **Postural hypotension** (α1 blockade) — clozapine, quetiapine, chlorpromazine; falls. Also lowered seizure threshold and ⚑ **increased mortality and stroke in elderly people with dementia — not first-line for BPSD**.

**Which agent for whom.** First episode, young, weight-conscious → the 2026 ANZ GRADE first-line agents, chosen for cardiometabolic profile: **aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine or lurasidone**; low-dose risperidone remains reasonable but carries more prolactin elevation and EPS. Agitation/insomnia → olanzapine or quetiapine. QT concern → avoid haloperidol, ziprasidone, amisulpride. Elderly → lowest dose, no anticholinergics. Affective comorbidity → quetiapine or lurasidone. Repeated relapse → **LAI**. **Two failed trials → clozapine** (§4.6).

**LAIs.** Risperidone (**2-weekly IM, or 4-weekly subcutaneous**), paliperidone palmitate (**loading doses day 1 and day 8, then** monthly, 3- and 6-monthly), aripiprazole (**monthly or 2-monthly**), olanzapine pamoate (**2- or 4-weekly**; ⚑ **post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome** can occur up to 3 hours after injection — post-dose observation is mandatory), flupentixol (2–4 weekly), zuclopenthixol decanoate (2–4 weekly), haloperidol decanoate (4-weekly). ⚑ RANZCP: discuss LAIs **early, including at first episode** — they cut relapse and rehospitalisation; establish oral tolerability first. ⚑ **Zuclopenthixol acetate (Acuphase)** is short-acting (2–3 days) for acute disturbance — *not* a maintenance depot; classic trap.

## 4.6 Clozapine

**The short version**
- The **only agent with proven superiority in treatment-resistant schizophrenia**; also reduces suicidality, TD and aggression.
- **TRS = failure of ≥2 antipsychotics**, adequate dose, **≥6 weeks each**, adherence confirmed.
- **Agranulocytosis** drives the mandatory FBC program; ⚑ **myocarditis in the first 4 weeks and constipation/ileus are what actually kill people**.
- Monitoring is a **condition of supply** — no blood result, no dispensing.
- **>48 h of missed doses → re-titrate from 12.5 mg; >28 days → treat as a new patient.**

**Indications.** TRS — inadequate response to **≥2 different antipsychotics**, each at **adequate dose** (≥600 mg chlorpromazine equivalent daily) for **≥6 weeks with adherence confirmed (>80%)**, plus at least moderate functional impairment. **Australian guidance does not require one of the two to be second-generation.** Also severe EPS/TD intolerance, **persistent suicidality**, and psychosis in Parkinson disease. ⚑ Chronically under- and late-prescribed; RANZCP warns against delay.

**Dosing.** Inpatient **12.5 mg day 1**, rising in 25–50 mg increments; community initiation slower (from 6.25 mg). Maintenance **200–600 mg/day**, maximum **900 mg/day**. Level ~350–600 ng/mL for response; **>1000 ng/mL** raises seizure risk. ⚑ **Smoking induces CYP1A2** — cessation (including forced abstinence on admission) can **raise levels sharply into toxicity**; restarting drops them. Fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin and systemic inflammation also raise levels.

**Haematological monitoring (Australia): FBC before starting, weekly for 18 weeks, then at least 4-weekly for the duration of treatment.**

| Light | WCC (×10⁹/L) | Neutrophils (×10⁹/L) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green** | ≥3.5 | **≥2.0** | Continue, routine monitoring |
| **Amber** | 3.0–<3.5 | **1.5–<2.0** | Continue but **≥twice-weekly FBC** until green |
| **Red** | <3.0 | **<1.5** | **Cease immediately**, daily FBC, haematology, watch for infection |

- **Agranulocytosis = ANC <0.5** — emergency: treat as febrile neutropenia (cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, consider G-CSF). Incidence ~0.8%, **highest in the first 18 weeks**.
- **Any fever or sore throat on clozapine → urgent FBC**, whenever the last one was.
- ⚑ A red result normally means **permanent cessation and "non-rechallengeable" registration**; rechallenge is exceptional and needs haematologist plus senior psychiatrist agreement. **Benign ethnic neutropenia** (African/Middle Eastern ancestry) is the main legitimate reason for individualised thresholds, agreed with haematology in advance.
- ⚑ **Practice is shifting**: the 2025 international Delphi consensus (*Lancet Psychiatry*) recommends weekly ×18 weeks, monthly to 2 years, then **discussing stopping routine ANC monitoring** if no abnormality; the EU has relaxed its schedule. **Australian requirements remain as tabulated** — answer with the current Australian protocol.

**Myocarditis.** ⚑ Peaks in the **first 3–4 weeks** (rare beyond 8); Australia reports higher rates than most countries and drove the standard protocol: **baseline then weekly troponin and CRP for 4 weeks** with ECG and clinical review, plus baseline echocardiogram. Suspect with **tachycardia out of proportion, fever, dyspnoea, chest pain, unexplained CRP rise**. Rising **troponin (>2× ULN) and/or CRP >100 mg/L** → **stop clozapine, echocardiogram, cardiology**. Later **cardiomyopathy** also occurs; rechallenge only with cardiologist input.

**Constipation — the genuine killer.** Affects up to ~60%, and clozapine-related **GI hypomotility carries higher mortality than agranulocytosis**; patients under-report because visceral sensation is blunted. **Ask every review**, prescribe aperients prophylactically (osmotic ± stimulant, not bulking agents alone), avoid stacking anticholinergics, and treat abdominal pain, distension, vomiting or absent bowel sounds as **ileus/toxic megacolon until excluded** — imaging and surgical review.

**Other.** **Seizures** — dose/level-dependent, ~3–5% cumulative at one year; consider valproate cover at higher doses (⚑ not in childbearing potential — §2.6). **Sialorrhoea** — **reported by ~30–80%** (one study 91%), nocturnal, aspiration risk; dose reduction, sublingual atropine 1% eye drops 1–2 drops nocte, or hyoscine. **Weight gain, dyslipidaemia, diabetes** — worst of any antipsychotic, including DKA without preceding weight gain. Also sedation, postural hypotension, tachycardia (benign only once myocarditis excluded), enuresis, and early fever (usually benign, but triggers FBC, troponin/CRP and NMS consideration).

**Missed doses.** ≤48 h — resume usual dose. **>48 h — no grace period: obtain psychiatric review and recommence at 12.5 mg once or twice daily, re-titrating** (⚑ tolerance to hypotension and sedation is lost), or risk severe hypotension, seizures and collapse. **A break of >72 h to 28 days additionally requires weekly FBC for 6 weeks. A break of >28 days means the patient is treated as new: full pre-treatment workup, re-registration, restart at 12.5 mg with the full 18 weeks of weekly monitoring.**

## 4.7 Psychiatric emergencies of antipsychotics

**The short version**
- **NMS**: rigidity + hyperthermia + autonomic instability + altered consciousness over **days**, **grossly raised CK**.
- **Serotonin syndrome**: **clonus and hyperreflexia** over **hours**, after a serotonergic drug added.
- **Malignant catatonia**: near-identical to NMS but preceded by **catatonic signs**; responds to **benzodiazepines and ECT**.
- **Acute dystonia is not NMS**: no fever, no altered consciousness, resolves in minutes with IM benztropine.

**NMS.** Idiosyncratic, not dose-related; risk rises with high-potency agents, rapid titration, parenteral dosing, dehydration, agitation, catatonia and prior NMS. Incidence **~0.01–0.03% with second-generation antipsychotics (~0.2% in the first-generation era)**, mortality now **~5–8%**. **Develops over 1–3 days**, usually within 2 weeks of starting or increasing a dopamine blocker (or after abrupt withdrawal of a dopamine *agonist*). **Lead-pipe rigidity** (uniform, not cogwheel), **hyperthermia — the diagnostic threshold is >38.0 °C on at least two occasions measured orally, and it is often far higher**, **autonomic instability**, **fluctuating consciousness**. Labs: **CK markedly raised** (often >1000, sometimes tens of thousands), leukocytosis, raised transaminases/LDH, metabolic acidosis, myoglobinuria.

**Management.** **Stop all antipsychotics** and other dopamine blockers (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine); **medical/ICU transfer**. Aggressive **IV fluids** against rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI, **active cooling**, electrolyte correction, **VTE prophylaxis**, serial renal function and CK. **Benzodiazepines** (lorazepam) first pharmacologically — treat agitation, rigidity and any coexisting catatonia. Severe/refractory: **dantrolene — IV bolus 1–2.5 mg/kg, then 1 mg/kg 6-hourly, maximum 10 mg/kg/day** — and/or **bromocriptine 2.5 mg 2–3 times daily, increased by 2.5 mg every 24 h to a maximum of 45 mg/day**, or amantadine — case-series evidence only, and dantrolene monotherapy is linked to worse outcomes. **ECT** if refractory or malignant catatonia cannot be excluded. Resolves over **1–2 weeks** (up to a month after a depot). **Rechallenge** ≥2 weeks after full resolution, **low-potency or second-generation agent**, low dose, monitoring CK and temperature.

| | **NMS** | **Serotonin syndrome** | **Malignant catatonia** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Dopamine **blocker** started/increased, or agonist withdrawn | Serotonergic drug added/increased (SSRI+MAOI, tramadol, linezolid) | Underlying psychiatric illness, often mood; follows simple catatonia |
| **Onset** | **Days (1–3)** | **Hours (<24 h)** | Days, on catatonic background |
| **Tone** | **Generalised lead-pipe rigidity** | Lower-limb predominant rigidity + **inducible/ocular clonus, tremor** | Rigidity, **waxy flexibility, posturing, negativism** |
| **Reflexes** | Normal or reduced | **Hyperreflexia** — best single discriminator | Variable |
| **Pupils / gut** | Normal; bowel sounds normal or reduced | **Mydriasis**; **hyperactive bowel sounds, diarrhoea** | Normal |
| **CK** | **Very high** | Normal or mildly raised | Raised |
| **Treatment** | Stop drug, cool, fluids, benzodiazepine ± dantrolene/bromocriptine; ECT if refractory | Stop drug, supportive, benzodiazepine; **cyproheptadine** if severe | **Lorazepam** then **ECT** — ⚑ antipsychotics may worsen it |

**Acute dystonia.** **Benztropine 1–2 mg IM/IV** (or promethazine), repeat once if needed — response within minutes is diagnostic. Secure the airway if laryngeal; then reduce dose or switch.

## 4.8 Catatonia

**The short version**
- A **psychomotor syndrome**, not a diagnosis — a specifier applied across mood, psychotic, medical and neurological conditions.
- ⚑ **More often affective than psychotic in origin** — bipolar disorder is the commonest single cause.
- Diagnosed on **≥3 characteristic signs**; **Bush–Francis scale** is the standard screen.
- **Lorazepam challenge** is diagnostic and therapeutic; **ECT** is definitive and first-line if malignant.
- Untreated it kills — dehydration, VTE, pressure injury, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis.

**Features.** *Reduced* — **stupor**, **mutism**, **negativism** (motiveless resistance to instruction or movement), **withdrawal** (food/fluid refusal), **staring**. *Abnormal* — **catalepsy** (posture held against gravity), **waxy flexibility**, posturing, mannerisms, stereotypies, **echolalia/echopraxia**, automatic obedience, ambitendency. *Excited catatonia* — purposeless hyperactivity. **Malignant (lethal) catatonia** adds **fever, autonomic instability and rigidity** — the NMS look-alike of §4.7.

**Assessment.** Bush–Francis screen; examine for waxy flexibility and negativism. FBC, UEC, CMP, **CK**, LFT, TSH, glucose, CRP, **ECG**, plus **CT/MRI and EEG** if the cause is unclear (EEG separates catatonia from non-convulsive status); consider **autoimmune encephalitis** (§4.4). **VTE prophylaxis, hydration, pressure-area and nutritional care from day one.**

**Lorazepam challenge.** **1–2 mg IV/IM/oral** (0.5–1 mg if elderly/frail), reassess at ~10 min (IV) or 30 min. **Improvement within minutes supports the diagnosis** and predicts response; then scheduled lorazepam to **6–16 mg/day**, tolerated without sedation in true catatonia. ⚑ Non-response does not exclude catatonia; ⚑ a challenge in a delirious patient may worsen confusion.

**ECT.** When benzodiazepines fail or only partly work, and **first-line without delay in malignant catatonia**, life-threatening food/fluid refusal or rapid deterioration; response ~80–90%. ⚑ **Antipsychotics can precipitate or worsen catatonia and raise NMS risk** — withhold or minimise while it is active.

## 4.9 Psychosocial management and prognosis

**The short version**
- Medication controls positive symptoms; **psychosocial intervention determines function** — offer both.
- Best evidence: **CBT for psychosis**, **family intervention**, and **Individual Placement and Support (IPS)** supported employment.
- **High expressed emotion** predicts relapse, and family intervention modifies it.
- Continue antipsychotic **≥1–2 years after a first episode**; ~**80% of first-episode patients who remit relapse within 5 years regardless of treatment, and discontinuing antipsychotics multiplies that risk about 5-fold.**
- **Cannabis cessation** is one of the few prognostic levers the patient directly controls.

**CBTp.** Normalises psychotic experience, examines the evidence for beliefs collaboratively rather than confronting them, builds coping strategies for voices, and targets the distress as much as the symptom. Recommended for **persistent positive symptoms despite adequate medication**. **Cognitive remediation** targets the cognitive domain, best paired with vocational rehabilitation.

**Family intervention and expressed emotion.** **EE** = household emotional climate on three dimensions — **criticism, hostility, emotional over-involvement**. High EE predicts relapse; the relationship is bidirectional and ⚑ **not a matter of blame**, usually reflecting exhausted carers reading negative symptoms as wilfulness. **Family intervention** (psychoeducation about negative symptoms, communication training, problem-solving, crisis planning; ≥10 sessions over ≥3 months) **reduces relapse and admission**. Address carer burden and respite.

**Supported employment.** **IPS** — rapid placement into open employment with on-the-job support — outperforms prevocational training and sheltered work; vocational recovery belongs in the plan from first episode.

**Other components.** Assertive community treatment, NDIS psychosocial supports, **housing first**, exercise and diet programs; smoking cessation (~60–70% smoke — varenicline/NRT effective, ⚑ cessation raises clozapine and olanzapine levels); **cardiometabolic care** via GP management plan with annual CV risk assessment; a relapse plan naming **early warning signs** (sleep loss, suspiciousness, withdrawal).

**Relapse prevention.** Continue **≥1–2 years after a first episode** (indefinitely after multiple episodes) at the **lowest effective dose**; taper slowly if stopping. Non-adherence is the commonest cause of relapse — ask non-judgementally about **sexual dysfunction, weight gain, akathisia and sedation**, use LAIs early, involve family.

**Prognosis.** ~20% good outcome, ~50% fluctuating with residual symptoms, ~20–30% severe persistent illness. **Suicide ~5%**, highest early in illness in young men with preserved insight; **cardiovascular disease drives the 15–20-year mortality gap**.

| Better prognosis | Worse prognosis |
|---|---|
| Good premorbid function, later onset | Insidious onset in early adolescence |
| **Acute onset** with clear precipitant | Long prodrome and **long DUP** |
| Prominent **affective** symptoms | Prominent **negative and cognitive** symptoms |
| Female sex, in a relationship | Male sex, socially isolated, high EE |
| **No substance use** | **Ongoing cannabis or methamphetamine use** |

**Cannabis cessation.** Continued use after a first episode predicts relapse, admission and worse function; **cessation improves outcome in proportion to the reduction**. Motivational interviewing framed around the person's own goals (staying out of hospital, keeping study or work), addressing the sleep and anxiety being self-medicated — a conversation across years, not one warning.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| The five negative symptoms of schizophrenia | **Avolition, alogia, anhedonia, asociality, blunted affect** |
| Duration: schizophrenia vs schizophreniform vs brief psychotic disorder | **≥6 months** (≥1 month active) vs **1–6 months** vs **≥1 day to <1 month, full recovery** |
| The feature making it schizoaffective rather than psychotic depression/mania | **≥2 weeks of delusions/hallucinations with mood symptoms absent** |
| Pathway giving therapeutic effect vs EPS vs galactorrhoea | **Mesolimbic** vs **nigrostriatal** vs **tuberoinfundibular**; D2 occupancy ~65–70% for effect, >80% for EPS |
| Young woman, rapid psychosis, seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability | **Anti-NMDAR encephalitis** — CSF antibodies, look for ovarian teratoma |
| Restlessness that anticholinergics won't fix | **Akathisia** — propranolol, dose reduction/switch, mirtazapine |
| Effect of anticholinergics on tardive dyskinesia | **Worsens it** — stop them; switch to clozapine; VMAT2 inhibitors are specific |
| Definition of treatment-resistant schizophrenia | Failure of **≥2 antipsychotics**, adequate dose, **≥6 weeks each**, adherence confirmed |
| Clozapine FBC schedule in Australia | **Weekly × 18 weeks, then ≥4-weekly for the duration of treatment** |
| Neutrophil count stopping clozapine; agranulocytosis threshold | Cease if **ANC <1.5 ×10⁹/L** (or WCC <3.0); **agranulocytosis = ANC <0.5** |
| Clozapine effect peaking in weeks 1–4, and its monitoring | **Myocarditis** — **weekly troponin + CRP × 4 weeks**, ECG, echo |
| Clozapine complication with higher mortality than agranulocytosis | **Constipation → ileus/toxic megacolon** — prophylactic aperients, ask every review |
| Clozapine doses missed for more than 48 hours | **Re-titrate from 12.5 mg** — tolerance to hypotension and sedation is lost; **>28 days → treat as a new patient** (re-registration, 18 weeks weekly FBC) |
| Lead-pipe rigidity, fever, CK 8000, over 3 days | **NMS** — stop antipsychotic, ICU, fluids, cooling, benzodiazepine ± dantrolene/bromocriptine |
| Clonus, hyperreflexia, mydriasis, diarrhoea, onset within hours | **Serotonin syndrome** — cyproheptadine if severe; resolves in 24–72 h |
| NMS look-alike that responds to lorazepam and ECT | **Malignant catatonia** — antipsychotics may worsen it |
| Lorazepam challenge dose, and meaning of a response | **1–2 mg IV/IM** (0.5–1 mg elderly); rapid improvement is **diagnostic and therapeutic** |
| Household factor predicting relapse, and its fix | **High expressed emotion** (criticism, hostility, over-involvement) → **family intervention** |
