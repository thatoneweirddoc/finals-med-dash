# Psychiatry Notes — Section 3: Anxiety, Trauma-Related and Somatic Disorders
*Sources: RANZCP CPG for panic, social anxiety and GAD (Andrews et al., ANZJP 2018) — ⚑ **withdrawn from the RANZCP Clinical Guidelines Library in 2023 as beyond the NHMRC five-year lifespan and retained for reference only; no replacement RANZCP anxiety CPG has been issued**, so verify drug and dose claims against AMH/TG Psychotropic, Phoenix Australia/NHMRC PTSD guidelines (2020–21), Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, RACGP (drugs of dependence; medically unexplained illness), TGA scheduling advisories, ANZCA Faculty of Pain Medicine PS01(PM) (2025), Australian Prescriber. Scope: GAD, panic/agoraphobia, social anxiety and phobias, OCD-spectrum, ASD/PTSD/complex PTSD, their management, and somatic symptom, functional and chronic pain presentations.*

## 3.1 Generalised anxiety disorder

**The short version**
- **Free-floating, multi-domain worry** — health, money, work, family, the future — that shifts topic but never switches off.
- **≥6 months**, most days, with the worry experienced as **uncontrollable**.
- Obligatory somatic tail: **restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, disturbed sleep**.
- Discriminator from normal worry = **uncontrollability + disproportion + impairment**, not the presence of worry.
- **GAD-7: 0–21.** Bands **5 / 10 / 15** = mild / moderate / severe; **≥10** warrants further assessment.

**The picture.** Usually presents somatically — tension headache, neck/shoulder ache, IBS-type symptoms, palpitations, insomnia — not as "I'm anxious." Onset is gradual, often traceable to adolescence; the patient says they have "always been a worrier." Worry is **anticipatory**, generating reassurance-seeking, checking on family, over-preparation and avoidance of uncertainty. Sleep disturbance is characteristically **initial insomnia**, which discriminates from the early-morning wakening of melancholic depression.

**Normal vs pathological worry.** Normal worry is proportionate, circumscribed, problem-solving, and stops when the problem resolves. Pathological worry is **disproportionate to actual likelihood**, **mobile** (resolve one worry and it lands on the next), felt as **beyond voluntary control**, persistent for months, and **costs function**.

| Differential | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Panic disorder** | Discrete crescendo attacks; fear is of the attack itself, not of life events |
| **Social anxiety** | Worry confined to **scrutiny/evaluation**; absent when alone |
| **OCD** | Ego-dystonic, often senseless content with **compulsions**; GAD worries are plausible real-life problems |
| **Depression** | Anhedonia, guilt, retardation; comorbidity is the rule — establish which came first |
| **Organic/substance** | Hyperthyroidism (tremor, heat intolerance, AF → **TSH**); caffeine, stimulants, salbutamol; **withdrawal states** |

**Baseline workup** (not a shotgun): FBC, EUC, LFT, **TSH**, glucose/HbA1c, ECG if palpitations or a QT-liable drug is planned. Anything further should follow a positive finding, not the anxiety (§3.8).

## 3.2 Panic disorder and agoraphobia

**The short version**
- **Panic attack** = discrete surge of fear **peaking within minutes**. A **descriptor, not a diagnosis** — occurs across anxiety, mood, psychotic and substance disorders and in healthy people.
- **Panic disorder** = **recurrent unexpected** attacks **plus ≥1 month** of anticipatory fear or maladaptive avoidance.
- Cognitive model: bodily sensation → **catastrophic misinterpretation** → adrenergic surge → more sensation, a closed loop maintained by **safety behaviours**.
- **Agoraphobia** = fear of situations where **escape is difficult or help unavailable** — transport, open/enclosed spaces, queues, crowds, being outside home alone.
- Exclude the mimics with a **small targeted workup**, not repeated cardiology referral.

**The mechanism.** A benign internal cue (postural tachycardia, caffeine, exertion, heat) is read by a hypervigilant patient as **imminent catastrophe — heart attack, suffocation, going mad**. That interpretation triggers sympathetic discharge, amplifying the sensation and confirming the belief. Hyperventilation drops pCO₂ → dizziness, paraesthesiae, chest tightness, derealisation, all reading as "something is seriously wrong." **Safety behaviours** (sitting near the exit, gripping a trolley, carrying a benzodiazepine) prevent disconfirmation — the patient concludes the catastrophe was *averted*. Treatment is therefore **interoceptive exposure**: deliberately provoking the sensations (hyperventilation, spinning, breath-holding) **while dropping safety behaviours**, so the prediction is falsified.

| Mimic | Doubt panic if… | Targeted test |
|---|---|---|
| **Arrhythmia** (SVT, AF, WPW, long QT) | Abrupt start *and* stop, rate >150 or irregular, **syncope**, symptoms from sleep, family history of sudden death | **ECG**; Holter/event monitor |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | **Pleuritic** pain, hypoxia, unilateral leg swelling, immobility/surgery/OCP/pregnancy | Wells → D-dimer/CTPA |
| **Thyrotoxicosis** | Weight loss with preserved appetite, heat intolerance, tremor, goitre, tachycardia **between** attacks | **TSH** ± free T4 |
| **Phaeochromocytoma** | Triad of **episodic headache, sweating, palpitations** with **paroxysmal hypertension**; pallor not flushing; ⚑ rare — don't screen everyone | Plasma free **metanephrines** |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Tied to fasting/exertion, relieved by eating, on insulin/sulfonylurea | BSL **during** the episode (Whipple's triad) |
| **Substance / caffeine** | New stimulant, energy drinks, salbutamol, pseudoephedrine, cannabis, amphetamines; or **withdrawal** | Quantified history; UDS if indicated |
| **Asthma** | Wheeze, cough, nocturnal symptoms; dyspnoea **precedes** the fear | Spirometry with bronchodilator response |

**What is actually indicated.** For a young patient with recurrent unexpected attacks, normal examination and no red flags: **ECG, FBC, EUC, TSH, glucose** plus a caffeine/substance/medication history — that is the whole of it. ⚑ **Repeated normal investigation is itself a treatment failure**: it feeds the catastrophic belief and delays effective treatment. Escalate only for syncope, exertional onset, hypoxia, focal signs, abnormal ECG, first attack over 45, or attacks arising from sleep.

## 3.3 Social anxiety disorder and specific phobias

**The short version**
- **Social anxiety** — fear of **scrutiny and negative evaluation**; the feared outcome is **humiliation or visible signs of anxiety** (blushing, tremor, voice shake). **≥6 months**, avoidance or endurance under distress, functional cost.
- **Performance-only subtype**: fear restricted to **performing/speaking in public**, ordinary social interaction unimpaired.
- **Specific phobia** — fear cued by **one object/situation** (animal, blood-injection-injury, natural environment, situational) with near-immediate anxiety on exposure.
- **Blood-injection-injury phobia is the exception** — a **biphasic vasovagal response with syncope**, treated with **applied tension**, not relaxation.
- Exposure is the treatment; drugs have little role in specific phobia.

| Presentation | Fear is of… | Discriminator |
|---|---|---|
| **Social anxiety** | Being judged or seen to be anxious | Symptoms only where **evaluation is possible**; fine alone |
| **Panic + agoraphobia** | Having an attack and being unable to escape | Fear is of the **body**, not the audience |
| **Avoidant PD** | Same content, but **lifelong, pervasive, trait-level** | Dimensional overlap — AvPD is the severe end |
| **Autism spectrum** | Not fear — **difficulty reading/using social cues**; early onset, restricted interests | Social *skill* deficit vs social *fear* |
| **Paranoid ideation** | Being harmed or targeted, with **conviction** | Social anxiety retains insight |

⚑ Performance-only anxiety is where the beta blocker question lives: Australian Prescriber notes **little evidence for beta blockers in anxiety disorders including social anxiety**, and Australian guidelines do not endorse them for generalised social anxiety — **propranolol before a discrete performance** is at best adjunctive and off-label. Generalised social anxiety: **CBT with exposure and video feedback first-line; SSRI/SNRI if moderate–severe or CBT unavailable**. Specific phobia: **graded in-vivo exposure**, often effective in single extended sessions.

## 3.4 Obsessive-compulsive disorder

**The short version**
- **Obsessions** = recurrent, intrusive, **unwanted** thoughts, images or urges causing marked anxiety/disgust, which the person tries to suppress or neutralise.
- **Compulsions** = repetitive behaviours **or mental acts** felt driven to perform in response, aimed at reducing distress or preventing a feared event, but **not realistically connected** or clearly excessive.
- Time-consuming (working threshold **>1 h/day**) or markedly impairing.
- **Insight is a specifier, not a requirement** — good/fair, poor, or absent (delusional). Absent insight is still OCD if the obsession–compulsion structure holds.
- Discriminator from **OCPD** = **ego-dystonic vs ego-syntonic**.

**Symptom dimensions.** (1) **Contamination** → washing, avoidance; (2) **Harm/responsibility** → checking locks and stove, driving back to see if someone was hit, reassurance-seeking; (3) **Symmetry and "just right"** → ordering, counting, repeating; (4) **Taboo thoughts** (aggressive, sexual, blasphemous) → mental rituals, praying, trigger avoidance. ⚑ Taboo obsessions are routinely misread as risk — they are **ego-dystonic and repugnant**, provoke avoidance of the feared target, and carry **no intent**: the opposite of a genuine violent or paraphilic drive.

| | **OCD** | **OCPD** |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | **Ego-dystonic** — alien, resisted, distressing | **Ego-syntonic** — seen as correct and virtuous |
| Structure | Discrete obsessions with linked compulsions | Pervasive **trait**: perfectionism, orderliness, rigidity |
| Insight | Recognises excessiveness | Sees no problem; distress is in **those around them** |
| Response | ERP + high-dose SSRI | Psychotherapy; poor SSRI response |

**In brief.** **Body dysmorphic disorder** — preoccupation with a defect unobservable or slight to others, with mirror-checking, camouflaging and **cosmetic-procedure seeking**; high suicide risk; ⚑ **procedures do not help and typically worsen it** — CBT + SSRI. **Hoarding disorder** — difficulty discarding regardless of value, with congestion rendering living areas unusable; ⚑ **a diagnosis in its own right, not an OCD subtype**, with poorer treatment response and major fire/falls implications in the elderly.

## 3.5 Acute stress disorder and PTSD

**The short version**
- Trauma exposure = direct experience, witnessing, learning of violent/accidental trauma to a close other, or repeated **occupational** exposure to aversive detail. ⚑ **Media exposure does not count** unless work-related.
- Four clusters: **intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, altered arousal and reactivity**.
- **ASD = 3 days to 1 month post-trauma; PTSD = beyond 1 month** (delayed expression if full criteria first met ≥6 months out). ⚑ **Adjustment disorder** is the third member of this trio and the commonest exam distractor: emotional/behavioural symptoms **beginning within 3 months of an identifiable stressor** (which need not meet criterion A), out of proportion to the stressor, and **resolving within 6 months of the stressor or its consequences ending** — no criterion A trauma, no intrusion/avoidance cluster structure required.
- **Complex PTSD (ICD-11)** = full PTSD **plus disturbances in self-organisation**.
- ⚑ **Single-session psychological debriefing is not recommended and may increase PTSD risk.**

**The four clusters, in plain terms.** **Intrusion** — the trauma re-enters awareness uninvited: involuntary memories, nightmares, and **flashbacks** in which the person acts or feels as if it is happening now (dissociative re-experiencing, not vivid recall), with autonomic reactivity to reminders. **Avoidance** — steering away from internal reminders (memories, feelings) and external ones (people, places, activities); this cluster entrenches the disorder and shrinks the person's world. **Negative alterations in cognition and mood** — inability to recall key parts of the event (dissociative, not head injury or substance), distorted beliefs about self and world ("nowhere is safe"), distorted **self-blame**, persistent fear/anger/shame, anhedonia, detachment, inability to feel positive emotion. **Arousal and reactivity** — irritability and unprovoked anger, reckless behaviour, **hypervigilance**, exaggerated startle, poor concentration, disturbed sleep. Specify **dissociative subtype** if depersonalisation/derealisation is prominent.

**Complex PTSD** (ICD-11; Ch. 7 of the Australian guidelines): full PTSD **plus** the three **disturbances in self-organisation (DSO)** — **affect dysregulation**, **persistent negative self-concept** and **interpersonal disturbance** — with **at least one symptom from each DSO cluster**. Typically follows **prolonged, repeated, inescapable interpersonal trauma**: childhood abuse, family violence, trafficking, torture. ⚑ Commonly mislabelled **borderline personality disorder**; in CPTSD the negative self-concept is stable rather than unstable, frantic abandonment-avoidance is less prominent, and re-experiencing is required.

**What does and does not predict PTSD.** Meta-analyses (Brewin 2000; Ozer 2003) show **peritraumatic and post-trauma variables outperform pre-trauma demographics**. Strongest: **perceived life threat and peritraumatic distress/dissociation**; **interpersonal/intentional trauma** (assault, sexual violence, torture > accident/disaster); and above all **lack of social support afterwards** and **subsequent life stress**. Moderate: prior psychiatric history, previous trauma, childhood adversity, physical injury/TBI, acute stress symptoms in the first month. Weak/inconsistent: age, sex, education. ⚑ **Most trauma-exposed people do not develop PTSD** — spontaneous recovery is the norm, which is why universal early intervention fails.

⚑ **Psychological debriefing.** The Cochrane review of single-session individual debriefing (Rose 2002) found **no reduction in PTSD** and some trials showed **worse outcomes at follow-up**; the Australian guidelines make a **conditional recommendation against** it, advising **information, emotional support and practical assistance** instead. The plausible harm is re-exposure consolidating the trauma memory during a physiologically aroused window, plus medicalisation of normal recovery. Correct early response = **psychological first aid** — monitor wellbeing and stabilise if needed, self-care advice, encourage social support, encourage limiting substance use — and, per the Australian guideline's **stepped care model, regular screening for PTSD symptoms across the three months following exposure**, with early referral for trauma-focused psychological treatment if symptoms emerge or persist. ⚑ The "review at 4 weeks" figure is a NICE convention, not the Australian recommendation.

## 3.6 Management of anxiety and trauma disorders

**The short version**
- **CBT with exposure is first-line for essentially every anxiety disorder**, at least as effective as medication with more durable gains.
- PTSD: **trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy, prolonged exposure and EMDR all carry strong recommendations** in the Australian guideline.
- Drugs: **SSRIs first-line for all anxiety disorders; SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine) also first-line for many** — RANZCP ranks them first-line "to a lesser extent" and switches to an SNRI where an SSRI is ineffective or not tolerated; ⚑ **start at half the lowest tablet strength** in panic; onset **4–6 weeks**; continue **≥12 months**.
- **OCD needs higher doses and longer trials** — maximum tolerated dose for **≥12 weeks** before declaring failure.
- ⚑ **Benzodiazepines are not first-line**, are **ineffective in OCD and PTSD**, and are for short-term use only.

**Stepped care, Australian version.** (1) **Treat comorbidity** — depression, substance use, sleep disorder. (2) **Psychoeducation, sleep, exercise, alcohol and caffeine reduction**, plus low-intensity digital CBT (**THIS WAY UP, MindSpot**) — the right answer for mild disease and rural access. (3) **Structured CBT with exposure** under a Mental Health Treatment Plan (Better Access). (4) **Pharmacotherapy** if moderate–severe, comorbid depression, or therapy declined/unavailable/failed. (5) **Psychiatry referral** for treatment resistance, diagnostic doubt, high risk, complex trauma.

**Exposure is the active ingredient**: **in-vivo** for phobias and agoraphobia, **interoceptive** for panic, **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** for OCD, **imaginal/in-vivo trauma-focused** for PTSD, with cognitive restructuring throughout. ⚑ **Relaxation-only and supportive counselling are inferior** — a common distractor. Beta blockers: ⚑ little evidence in any anxiety disorder.

| Drug | Australian place | Practical points |
|---|---|---|
| **SSRI** (sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, fluvoxamine) | **First-line, all anxiety disorders**; sertraline/paroxetine/fluoxetine also support PTSD | Start **half the lowest tablet strength available** — the rule applies across the anxiety disorders (OCD excepted, where ~75% do *not* respond to minimum doses), and matters most in panic, where initial jitteriness is the commonest cause of abandonment |
| **SNRI** (venlafaxine, duloxetine) | Also **first-line for many anxiety disorders** (RANZCP: first-line "to a lesser extent" than SSRIs); switch to one where an SSRI is ineffective or not tolerated; **venlafaxine has PTSD evidence**; duloxetine if comorbid pain | Check BP at higher doses; discontinuation symptoms |
| **Pregabalin** | Effective in GAD; ⚑ **not PBS-subsidised for anxiety**; recognised drug of misuse | Sedation, weight gain |
| **Quetiapine** | Some GAD efficacy (off-label); ⚑ **insufficient evidence in the Australian PTSD guideline** — not a recommended PTSD adjunct | ⚑ Long-term use inadvisable — metabolic, tardive dyskinesia |
| **Prazosin** | ⚑ **Not recommended by the Australian PTSD guideline** — insufficient evidence; the definitive multisite RCT (Raskind, NEJM 2018, n=304, 26 weeks) found **no difference from placebo** for nightmares, sleep or PTSD severity. Off-label; sometimes still trialled for refractory nightmares | Off-label; first-dose postural hypotension |
| **Clomipramine** | OCD after ≥2 failed SSRIs (tolerability, not efficacy, is why it is second-line). Australian PI dose for OCD: build up stepwise by 25 mg every few days to **100–150 mg/day**, maintenance **50–100 mg/day**; elderly **50–75 mg/day**. ⚑ Doses above this (the 75–300 mg range quoted in Australian Prescriber) are **off-label**, and the PI warns the recommended dose should not be exceeded because seizure risk is dose-dependent | Anticholinergic; **dose-dependent seizure risk**; cardiotoxic in overdose — baseline ECG |
| **Benzodiazepines** | **Not first-line**; short-term/crisis or refractory only | See below |

**Timing rules.** Anxiety responds **more slowly than depression — 4–6 weeks**; treat **≥12 months** after response before a slow taper. **OCD is the outlier**: increase every ~2 weeks toward maximum tolerated dose; full effect may take **12 weeks**, and an adequate trial is **maximum dose for ≥12 weeks**; continue **≥12 months** — **>50% relapse within 12 weeks** of stopping. Doses used in OCD run high: fluoxetine **40–80 mg**, sertraline **50–200 mg**, paroxetine **20–60 mg**, fluvoxamine **50–300 mg** — all within the TGA-approved maxima for OCD. ⚑ **Two SSRIs are capped and cannot be pushed:** citalopram at **40 mg** (**20 mg** if >65 y, hepatic impairment or CYP2C19 poor metaboliser) and **escitalopram at 20 mg** (**10 mg** if >65 y) — both for **dose-dependent QT prolongation**. The 10–40 mg escitalopram range quoted in Australian Prescriber is **above the TGA-approved maximum and off-label**; if used it needs specialist involvement, baseline and on-treatment ECG, and electrolyte checks. When these two are inadequate the move is to **switch to a different, uncapped SSRI** (fluoxetine, sertraline, fluvoxamine, paroxetine) — not to exceed the cap.

⚑ **Why benzodiazepines are not first-line, and the harm they do.** They relieve arousal in minutes but **do not treat the disorder**: by aborting anxiety they act as a **safety behaviour**, blocking the extinction learning exposure depends on, and they **worsen CBT outcomes**. **No benefit in OCD or PTSD.** Harms: **tolerance within days to weeks**; **withdrawal symptoms after 4–6 weeks of use in ~15–30%** — the RACGP figure derives specifically from benzodiazepines **prescribed for insomnia**; with regular therapeutic-dose use for more than a few months the estimate rises to **30–45%**; withdrawal brings rebound anxiety, insomnia, tremor and seizures on abrupt cessation; **falls and hip fracture** in the elderly; **anterograde amnesia and cognitive slowing** with a debated dementia association; **driving impairment — 60–80% higher crash risk, around sevenfold with alcohol**; **paradoxical disinhibition**; and **respiratory depression and death with opioids or alcohol**.

**The Australian position.** RACGP: **lowest effective dose, shortest clinical timeframe**; continuation **beyond 4 weeks is uncommon** and requires explicit justification; if long-term use is unavoidable, use **intermittent dosing with regular attempts at reduction**. ⚑ **Alprazolam was rescheduled to Schedule 8 in 2014** after misuse, overdose deaths and paradoxical excitation. All S8s and many benzodiazepines are captured by **real-time prescription monitoring (SafeScript / national RTPM)** — check before prescribing. Deprescribe by **stepwise taper**. ⚑ **Routine conversion to diazepam is not recommended in Australia** — switching short- to long-acting has not been shown to improve cessation rates or reduce withdrawal severity, and it is specifically discouraged in older people; RACGP reserves it for patients with **problematic withdrawal symptoms on reduction**. Rate: no evidence-based optimum, individualise — a commonly cited approach is **~10–25% of the daily dose every 1–2 weeks** (RACGP: minimum 6 weeks, up to 6 months overall), with **slower final steps (~10–12.5%)** as the dose gets low.

## 3.7 Somatic symptom and related disorders

**The short version**
- The organising question is no longer "are the symptoms explained?" but **"are the thoughts, feelings and behaviours about the symptoms excessive and disabling?"**
- **Somatic symptom disorder** — distressing somatic symptoms (which **may be medically explained**) **plus** disproportionate health-related thoughts, anxiety and time/energy, usually **>6 months**.
- **Illness anxiety disorder** — preoccupation with *having or acquiring* illness with **minimal or no somatic symptoms**.
- **Functional neurological disorder** — neurological symptoms with **positive rule-in signs**.
- **Factitious disorder vs malingering** turns on the **presence of an obvious external incentive**, not on proving motive — both involve conscious falsification.

| Disorder | Production | External incentive | Core feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Somatic symptom disorder** | **Unconscious** | No | Excessive thought/anxiety/behaviour about real symptoms; distress genuine |
| **Illness anxiety disorder** | **Unconscious** (few/no symptoms) | No | Fear of *having* disease; reassurance does not hold |
| **Functional neurological disorder** | ⚑ **Not assessed** — DSM-5 deliberately **dropped** the DSM-IV requirement that symptoms be judged "not intentionally produced or feigned", because assessment of conscious intention is unreliable. FND is diagnosed on **positive rule-in signs**, not on inferring the patient's intent | No | Internal inconsistency, incompatible with recognised disease |
| **Factitious disorder** (imposed on self or on another) | **Conscious** falsification | **No obvious external reward** — ⚑ DSM-5 defines it by the *absence of an obvious external incentive*, and **removed** the DSM-IV requirement to establish that the motive is assuming the sick role; you do not have to prove motive to diagnose it | Deception, falsification of history/signs/investigations, self-induced illness or injury; often healthcare-connected |
| **Malingering** | **Conscious** | **Yes** — money, drugs, avoiding work/court | Not a psychiatric disorder; a description of behaviour |

⚑ Two traps: **SSD does not require the symptom to be unexplained** — a patient with genuine IHD can have SSD if the psychological response is disproportionate; and **la belle indifférence and secondary gain are unreliable** and must not be used to diagnose FND or imply malingering.

**FND is a rule-in diagnosis.** ⚑ Not diagnosis by exclusion — it rests on **positive signs of internal inconsistency** (symptom varies with attention/distraction) or **incompatibility** with neuroanatomy.

| Sign | Elicited by | Shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Hoover's sign** | Weak hip **extension** normalises when the patient flexes the *contralateral* hip against resistance | Voluntary weakness with intact automatic movement. **Highly specific, moderately sensitive** — pooled estimates across studies ~94% sensitivity / ~99% specificity; the only prospective study (McWhirter, Stone et al., 2011) gave **63% sensitivity (95% CI 24–91) and 100% specificity (95% CI 97–100)** but rested on only 8 patients with functional weakness, hence the very wide CI. Quote it as "highly specific, sensitivity uncertain", not as a hard number. |
| **Tremor entrainment/distraction** | Ask patient to copy a rhythmic tap in the unaffected limb | Functional tremor **entrains to** or drops out at the external rhythm |
| **Tubular (non-expanding) field** | Confrontation/perimetry at two distances | Field fails to widen with distance — geometrically impossible |
| **Give-way (collapsing) weakness** | Sustained resistance testing | Normal initial power then abrupt ratchety yielding |
| **Dissociative seizures** | Event semiology | >2 min, waxing–waning, **eyes closed with resistance**, side-to-side head movement, pelvic thrusting, no post-ictal confusion, **normal prolactin/lactate**, event recall |

Caveat: no sign is infallible, and **FND frequently coexists with neurological disease** — a positive Hoover's does not exclude MS or stroke.

## 3.8 Managing medically unexplained symptoms in practice

**The short version**
- **Take the symptoms seriously and say so** — validation precedes any psychological framing.
- **Investigate once, well, and early**; then stop. Each extra test buys false positives, cascades and reinforced illness belief.
- **Explain what it is, not what it isn't** — a positive, mechanism-based, non-blaming explanation.
- **One GP coordinates**, with scheduled time-based reviews replacing symptom-triggered attendances.
- The goal is **function and quality of life**, not symptom eradication.

**The consultation.** Take an unhurried history including the patient's **own explanatory model** ("what do you think is causing this? what worries you most?") — you cannot correct a belief you have not elicited. Examine properly: the examination is therapeutic as well as diagnostic, and it is where the **positive signs** are found. Screen for depression, anxiety, trauma and substance use, and ask about **function** — work, sleep, exercise, relationships — making that the outcome measure.

**Avoiding iatrogenic harm.** Repeated testing generates incidentalomas, radiation, procedural complications, cost, and above all **entrenchment** — each new test implies you still suspect serious disease. RACGP guidance: **negotiate a bounded investigation plan up front**, explain what will and will not be done and why, then **monitor for genuine change** rather than re-testing on distress. Avoid opioids, benzodiazepines and invasive procedures — while remembering these patients remain at risk of **under-treatment of real disease**.

**Explaining it without implying it is imaginary.** Name it, because an unnamed problem is an unmanaged one. Then: **the symptoms are real and genuinely disabling**; the problem is one of **function, not structure** — "the hardware is intact, the software is misfiring"; **show them the positive sign** ("your leg moved normally when you weren't focusing on it — the pathway works"), which convinces far more than a normal MRI; it is **common, understood and treatable**; and it is explicitly **not "all in your head", not malingering, and not a diagnosis of exclusion**. Acknowledge uncertainty and offer to revisit if the picture changes.

**The GP as single coordinating clinician.** Fewer clinicians means fewer contradictory explanations, duplicated tests and less doctor-shopping. Structure: one named GP; **scheduled appointments at fixed intervals independent of symptoms**; a written management plan; referral only with a clear question. Treatment is **rehabilitative and multidisciplinary** — CBT (best evidence across MUS), graded activity and pacing, **physiotherapy with a retraining rather than strengthening focus in FND**, and treatment of comorbid depression/anxiety.

## 3.9 Chronic pain and the psychological dimension

**The short version**
- Chronic pain = pain persisting or recurring **>3 months**; beyond that it behaves as **a disease in itself**, not a symptom of tissue damage.
- **Biopsychosocial model**: nociception is modulated by central sensitisation, mood, cognition, sleep, behaviour, work and compensation context — imaging correlates poorly with disability.
- **Catastrophising** (rumination, magnification, helplessness) and **fear-avoidance** are the strongest modifiable predictors of disability.
- First-line care is **active and multimodal**: education, graded exercise and pacing, CBT/ACT, sleep, treat depression.
- ⚑ **Opioids have no demonstrated long-term benefit in chronic non-cancer pain.**

**The fear-avoidance cycle.** Pain → catastrophic interpretation ("something is being damaged") → **kinesiophobia** → avoidance and guarding → deconditioning, sleep loss, withdrawal, loss of role → depression and further disability → lower pain threshold → more pain. The opposite path, **confrontation**, leads to graded resumption of activity and recovery. Hence the intervention is **graded exposure to movement with pacing**, not rest — and catastrophising predicts outcome better than imaging does.

⚑ **The Australian position on opioids in chronic non-cancer pain** (ANZCA Faculty of Pain Medicine PS01(PM), 2025 update): opioids are **not first-line**; **self-management and non-opioid options must be considered first**, with multimodal therapy the foundation. Any opioid use is a **time-limited trial of 6–8 weeks** against a defined **functional** goal, **limited to about 100 mg oral morphine equivalent daily**, reviewed with the **"5 As" — analgesia, activity, adverse effects, affect, aberrant behaviour** — documented at intervals of no more than a month before repeats, with a colleague involved in any decision to continue and **at least annual peer or specialist review**. Cease if functional goals are not met. Cautions: age >65 or <18, household members with substance use disorder or suicidality, respiratory/hepatic/renal disease, and **concurrent sedatives or alcohol** — FPM lists this as a **risk factor demanding heightened caution and documented risk assessment**, not a formal contraindication: "the combination of these, potentially with other sedatives and alcohol, is associated with further increased risk of apnoea and death." The 2020 TGA opioid reforms (smaller pack sizes, tightened indications, boxed warnings) and national RTPM have moved practice toward deprescribing and referral to **multidisciplinary pain programmes** rather than dose escalation.

| The maintaining mechanism in panic and the therapy that targets it | **Catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations**, held in place by safety behaviours → **interoceptive exposure** |
| Episodic headache, sweating and palpitations with paroxysmal hypertension | **Phaeochromocytoma** — plasma free **metanephrines**; do not screen all panic patients |
| Core panic workup with no red flags, and the harm of exceeding it | **ECG, FBC, EUC, TSH, glucose** + caffeine/substance history; ⚑ repeated normal testing **entrenches the catastrophic belief** |
| OCD vs OCPD | **Ego-dystonic** (resisted, distressing) vs **ego-syntonic** (seen as correct); **discrete obsessions with linked compulsions** vs **pervasive lifelong trait** of perfectionism/orderliness/rigidity |
| Timing separating ASD from PTSD (and adjustment disorder) | **ASD 3 days–1 month; PTSD beyond 1 month** (delayed expression if **full** criteria first met ≥6 months); **adjustment disorder** = symptoms within **3 months** of an identifiable stressor (no criterion A needed), resolving within **6 months** of the stressor ending |
| The four PTSD clusters | **Intrusion; avoidance; negative alterations in cognition/mood; altered arousal and reactivity** |
| Complex PTSD — what is added to PTSD | **Disturbances in self-organisation**: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, interpersonal disturbance (≥1 each) |
| Strongest predictors of PTSD after trauma | **Peritraumatic distress/dissociation, perceived life threat, interpersonal trauma**, and above all **poor social support and later life stress** — not demographics |
| Single-session debriefing after a disaster — and what to do instead | ⚑ **Not recommended, may increase PTSD risk** → **psychological first aid**, practical support, and **screening for PTSD symptoms across the 3 months after exposure** (stepped care) — the "~4 weeks" review is NICE, not the Australian guideline |
| First-line psychological treatments for PTSD (Australian guideline) | **Trauma-focused CBT, CPT, cognitive therapy, prolonged exposure, EMDR** — all strong recommendations |
| Starting an SSRI in panic — dosing rule, latency, duration | **Half the lowest tablet strength**; onset **4–6 weeks**; continue **≥12 months** |
| What makes OCD pharmacotherapy different | **Higher doses, longer latency** — max dose for **≥12 weeks**; ⚑ two SSRIs cannot be pushed — **citalopram capped at 40 mg** (20 mg if >65 y) and **escitalopram at 20 mg** (10 mg if >65 y) for QT; switch SSRI rather than exceed the cap; clomipramine after 2 failed SSRIs |
| Why benzodiazepines are not first-line, and the Australian rule | **Safety behaviour blocking extinction**; **useless in OCD and PTSD**; **withdrawal after 4–6 weeks in ~15–30%** (insomnia prescribing; **30–45%** after months of regular therapeutic use), falls, cognitive impairment, **60–80% higher crash risk (×7 with alcohol)**, fatal with opioids. Lowest dose, shortest time — **beyond 4 weeks uncommon**; ⚑ **alprazolam → Schedule 8 (2014)** |
| Is FND a diagnosis of exclusion, and what rules it in? | ⚑ **No — rule-in** on positive signs: **Hoover's (highly specific, sensitivity uncertain — the one prospective study, n=8, gave 63%/100%)**, tremor entrainment, tubular fields, give-way weakness |
| Single most useful structural intervention in medically unexplained symptoms | **One coordinating GP with scheduled time-based (not symptom-triggered) reviews** and a bounded investigation plan |
| Opioid trial in chronic non-cancer pain — ceiling, length, review framework | ⚑ **~100 mg oral morphine equivalent/day**, trial **6–8 weeks**, **5 As** at ≤monthly intervals, annual peer/specialist review |
| The two psychological factors best predicting pain-related disability | **Catastrophising** and **fear-avoidance/kinesiophobia** — better predictors than imaging |
