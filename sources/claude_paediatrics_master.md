# Paediatrics Notes — Section 1: Growth, Development and Nutrition
*Sources: QLD Red Flags guide, RCH, Safer Care Victoria, NHMRC, ASCIA 2026. Scope: development and its failure, growth, nutrition, puberty, short stature.*

## 1.1 Milestones by domain — the two-way table

**The short version**
- Four domains: gross motor, fine motor/vision, speech/language/hearing, social/self-care.
- Stems give milestones and want an **age**, or give an age and want the **expected/absent** skill — learn this by row *and* column.
- Correct for prematurity until **2 y**.

| Age | Gross motor | Fine motor / vision | Speech / language | Social / self-care |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 wk | Head lifts briefly prone; head lag | Fixes and follows to 90°; **fixes on face** | Coos; startles to sound | **Social smile (absent by 8 wk = red flag)** |
| 3 mo | Head control on forearms; minimal head lag | Follows 180°; hands open at midline | Cooing, turn-taking | Laughs |
| 6 mo | Rolls both ways; **sits with support/tripod** | **Palmar grasp**; transfers hand-to-hand; mouths | **Babbles**; turns to voice | Stranger awareness begins |
| 9 mo | Crawls; **pulls to stand**; cruises from ~10 mo (⚑ **sits unsupported is a 6–8 mo skill** — WHO median 5.9 mo, 99th centile 9.2 mo, which is why *not* sitting at 9 mo is a red flag) | **Immature pincer**; bangs 2 cubes | Polysyllabic babble; responds to name | **Stranger anxiety**; waves, peek-a-boo; finger feeds |
| 12 mo | Cruises; **stands alone**; walks (range 10–18 mo) | **Mature pincer**; casting; object into cup | **1–3 words with meaning**; follows "no" | Points to show; cup; separation anxiety |
| 18 mo | **Walks steadily**, runs stiffly; stairs with help | **Scribbles**; tower of 3–4; spoon to mouth | **6–20 words**; 1 body part; 1-step command | Imitates housework; removes shoes |
| 2 y | Runs; **kicks ball**; stairs 2 feet per step | Tower of 6; **copies vertical line** | **2-word phrases**; ~50 words; **~50% intelligible** | Parallel play; toileting starting |
| 3 y | **Tricycle**; stairs one foot per step up | Tower of 9; **copies circle**; threads beads | **3-word sentences**; **~75% intelligible**; name/age | **Pretend/interactive play**; dry by day; dresses with help |
| 4 y | **Hops**; stairs alternating both ways | **Copies cross**; person with 3 parts; scissors | **~100% intelligible**; tells a story | Cooperative play; dresses self |
| 5 y | **Skips**; catches ball; 1 leg 5–10 s | **Copies triangle/square**; writes name | Fluent complex grammar; counts 10 | Chooses friends; independent toileting |

**Reverse lookup — milestone → age**

| Milestone in stem | Age |
|---|---|
| Social smile / transfers + sits with support / sits unsupported + stranger anxiety | 6 wk / 6 mo / 9 mo |
| Mature pincer, first words, stands alone | 12 mo |
| Scribbles, walks well, ~10 words | 18 mo |
| 2-word phrases, tower of 6, runs | 2 y |
| Tricycle, copies circle, 3-word sentences | 3 y |
| Hops, copies cross, draws person | 4 y |
| Skips, copies triangle, writes name | 5 y |
| Tower of 3 / 6 / 9 | 18 mo / 2 y / 3 y |

Hook: **line 2, circle 3, cross 4, square 4½, triangle 5** — one shape per year.

## 1.2 Red flags for referral

The **QLD "Red Flags" Early Identification Guide** sets each threshold at the age by which nearly all children have the skill — failure = referral, not "watch and wait."

| Age | Refer if… |
|---|---|
| 6 mo | Not lifting head/shoulders prone; **hands persistently fisted**; not reaching for toys; not babbling; not smiling |
| 9 mo | Not rolling; no head control in supported sitting; hands not to midline; no gestures; no shared eye contact |
| 12 mo | **Not sitting independently**; not weight-bearing; no pincer; no babbled phrases; no response to familiar words |
| 15–18 mo | **Not attempting to walk without support at 15 mo**; **no babbled phrases / no response to familiar words at 18 mo**; not scribbling; no interest in others |
| 2 y | **Not walking independently**; **no clear words**; **not putting 2 words together**; not learning new words; no spoon-feeding; non-functional toy use |
| 3 y | **Speech unintelligible to strangers**; not managing stairs holding rail; no symbolic/pretend play emerging. ⚑ CHQ puts **no interest in pretend play at 4 y** and **not toilet trained by day at 5 y** — do not refer a 3-year-old for daytime wetting |
| 4 y | Not running/jumping; can't follow 2-step directions; can't draw lines/circles; can't play cooperatively |
| 5 y | Can't hop 5× or stand on 1 leg 5 s; can't hold a simple conversation |
| **Any age** | **Regression/loss of skills** (neurodegenerative, Rett, epileptic encephalopathy, ASD regression); **strong parental concern**; no response to sound or vision; **asymmetry of tone or movement** (hemiplegic CP); persistent tight toe-walking |

**Hand preference before 12–18 months is abnormal** — it means relative weakness of the other side, i.e. hemiplegia, until proven otherwise.

## 1.3 Primitive reflexes

**The short version**
- **Persistence beyond the expected age** is abnormal (UMN lesion/CP); so is **absence when expected** (cord lesion, severe hypotonia, brachial plexus injury if asymmetric Moro).
- Postural reflexes (parachute, lateral propping) appear as primitive ones fade.

| Reflex | From | Gone by | Abnormal if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moro | Birth | **3–6 mo** | **Asymmetric** → clavicle #/Erb's; absent → CNS depression; persists → CP |
| Rooting/sucking | Birth | 4 mo | Absent → prematurity/CNS depression |
| Palmar grasp | Birth | **4–6 mo** | Persists → blocks voluntary grasp/pincer |
| Plantar grasp | Birth | ~9–12 mo | Persists → delays walking |
| ATNR ("fencing") | ~1 mo | **4–6 mo** | Obligatory or persistent → CP |
| Stepping | Birth | 6–8 wk | — |
| **Parachute** (postural) | **6–9 mo** | Lifelong | **Absent at 12 mo = red flag**; asymmetric → hemiplegia |

## 1.4 Growth charts and centiles

**The short version**
- Australia: **WHO standards 0–2 y** (breastfed reference), **CDC 2–18 y**. ⚑ UK uses UK-WHO 0–4 y then UK90.
- Plot **corrected age** to 2 y if born <37 wk. One centile line ≈ 0.67 SD.
- **Crossing centile lines is the signal, not the absolute centile.**

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Tracking along the 3rd centile, small parents | **Normal — familial short stature**; position alone never diagnoses |
| **Downward crossing of ≥2 major centile lines** (weight) | Faltering growth until proven otherwise |
| Upward crossing 0–2 y in a small well baby | Normal **catch-up** (SGA, ex-prem) |
| Weight ↓ then length ↓ then head circumference ↓ | **Order of sacrifice in undernutrition** — HC last, so ↓HC = chronic/severe |
| **HC crossing upward**, bulging fontanelle, sun-setting | Hydrocephalus/raised ICP — image |
| Short **and** overweight with poor height velocity | **Endocrine** (GH deficiency, hypothyroidism, Cushing) — undernutrition makes children thin *and* short |
| Normal centiles, **poor height velocity** | Pathology in evolution — velocity beats a single point |

Normal: birthweight loss ≤**10%**, regained by **day 10–14**; doubles ~4–5 mo, triples by 12 mo; length +50% by 12 mo; anterior fontanelle closes **9–18 mo**.

## 1.5 Faltering growth

**Definition (Safer Care Victoria):** weight gain slower than expected for age/sex, typically **weight crossing ≥2 major centile lines downward**. ⚑ Preferred term over "failure to thrive"; Australian charts differ from the NICE birthweight-centile thresholds in UK notes, concept identical.

| Category | Clue → cause |
|---|---|
| **Inadequate intake** (the commonest mechanism overall, esp. <2 y) | Formula over-dilution/measuring error; poor latch, short feeds; food insecurity; grazing toddler |
| **Psychosocial / neglect** (a major contributor to inadequate intake rather than a separate majority category) | Gains weight when admitted or in respite; parental mental illness, IPV, substance use; poor attachment |
| **Inadequate absorption** | **Coeliac** (onset after gluten introduction, distended abdomen, irritability); **CF** (bulky greasy stools, chest sx); CMPA (blood/mucus in stool, eczema) |
| **Excess losses** | GORD; **pyloric stenosis (projectile, 2–8 wk, hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis)**; raised ICP |
| **Increased requirements** | **CHD** (sweaty/breathless feeds, murmur), chronic lung disease, **CKD/RTA (poor growth + normal anion gap acidosis + polyuria)**, hyperthyroidism, chronic infection, malignancy |

**Assessment:** detailed feeding history (volumes, scoops per water, frequency, duration), 3-day diary, developmental and social history, plot all three parameters against previous charts, full exam including dysmorphology, and **observe a feed**. Most need no bloods.

**If indicated:** FBC + film, iron studies, UEC/LFT/CMP, **coeliac serology (tTG-IgA + total IgA, valid only on gluten)**, TFT, CRP, urine MCS (occult UTI), stool MCS ± faecal elastase. Then by clue: sweat test, microarray, duodenal biopsy, echo, CXR.

## 1.6 Infant feeding, weaning, iron and vitamin D

**The short version**
- Exclusive breast/formula to **~6 mo**, solids when developmentally ready — **around 6 mo, not before 4 mo**; continue milk feeds to ≥12 mo.
- **First foods must be iron-rich** — term infant iron stores deplete at ~6 mo and breast milk is iron-poor.
- ⚑ **ASCIA 2026**: introduce **well-cooked egg and peanut in the first year**, then **keep them in the diet ≥1×/week**. Don't delay allergens, don't use hydrolysed formula for prevention, don't exclude allergens from the mother's diet.
- ⚑ Australia has **no universal infant vitamin D supplement** (UK gives 400 IU to all) — **at-risk infants only**.

| Topic | Australian practice |
|---|---|
| Breastfeeding | Exclusive ~6 mo, continue ≥12 mo with solids. Few contraindications: **untreated TB, some drugs (chemo, lithium, amiodarone, radioiodine), infant galactosaemia**. ⚑ **Maternal HIV is no longer an absolute contraindication in Australia** — formula is the safest option and remains the recommendation, but ASHM supports breastfeeding as a viable choice under shared decision-making with suppressed viral load and close monitoring |
| Formula | Cow's-milk-based standard; soy only for galactosaemia/vegan; eHF/AAF for CMPA |
| Weaning order | Iron-rich first (fortified cereal, pureed meat, legumes) → lumpy textures by 8–9 mo (texture-acceptance window) → finger foods → family diet by 12 mo |
| Cow's milk | **Not the main drink before 12 mo** (low iron, occult GI loss, renal solute load); in food from 6 mo, full-fat to 2 y. **>500–600 mL/day in toddlers → iron deficiency** |
| Avoid <12 mo | **Honey (infant botulism)**, added salt/sugar, unpasteurised, juice; whole nuts/hard round foods until 3–5 y (choking) |
| Iron | Term stores last ~6 mo; **preterm/LBW need supplementation** (accrual is third-trimester). Deficiency → pallor, irritability, developmental impact; microcytic, ↓ferritin (acute-phase reactant — check CRP) |
| Vitamin D | Risk-based: **dark skin, covered/veiled, breastfed by a deficient mother, minimal sun, malabsorption, anticonvulsants**; **400 IU (10 µg)/day**. Deficiency → **rickets**: bowing, widened wrists, rachitic rosary, ↓Ca/↓PO₄, **↑ALP, ↑PTH, ↓25-OH-D** |
| Vitamin K / fluoride | Universal IM at birth (2.3) / fluoridated tap water, not added to formula |

## 1.7 Puberty and Tanner staging

**The short version**
- **Girls: thelarche first** (B2, ~8–13 y) → pubarche → **peak height velocity Tanner 2–3** → **menarche ~2–2.5 y after thelarche, Tanner 4** (mean ~12.5 y); only ~5–7 cm growth left.
- **Boys: testicular enlargement first (≥4 mL, ~9–14 y)** → penile growth → pubarche → **peak height velocity late, Tanner 3–4 (~14 y)**; start later, grow longer, end taller.
- Girls' growth spurt **early**, boys' **late** — a favourite discriminator.

| Stage | Girls — breast | Boys — genitals | Pubic hair (both) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prepubertal | Testes <4 mL | None |
| 2 | **Breast bud (thelarche)**, areolar widening | **Testes 4–8 mL**, scrotal thinning/reddening | Sparse, long, slightly pigmented at labia/base of penis |
| 3 | Breast + areola enlarge, single contour | Testes 8–12 mL, penis lengthens | Darker, coarser, curling, over pubis |
| 4 | **Areola/papilla form secondary mound** | Testes 15–20 mL, penis widens, glans develops | Adult-type, not yet to thighs |
| 5 | Adult, areola recedes to breast contour | Testes **>20 mL** (to ~25), adult | Adult, spread to medial thighs |

| Clue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Breast development **<8 y** (girl); testes ≥4 mL or virilisation **<9 y** (boy) | **Precocious puberty** — refer. Pre-referral: LH/FSH, oestradiol/testosterone, TFT, **bone age** |
| Precocious puberty in a **boy** | More likely **pathological — image the brain** (hamartoma, tumour); in girls usually idiopathic central |
| Pubic/axillary hair + body odour, **no breast/testicular enlargement** | **Premature adrenarche** — benign; exclude CAH/androgen tumour if rapid or virilising |
| Isolated breast buds, girl <2 y, normal growth and bone age | **Premature thelarche** — observe |
| Boy 14 y, testes 8 mL, gynaecomastia | **Physiological pubertal gynaecomastia** (Tanner 3–4), resolves 1–2 y |
| **No breast development by 13 y, no testicular enlargement by 14 y** | **Delayed puberty** — LH/FSH, karyotype, coeliac, TFT, bone age |
| Delayed puberty, **high LH/FSH** | Hypergonadotropic — **Turner (45,X), Klinefelter (47,XXY)**, chemo/radiotherapy |
| Delayed puberty, **low LH/FSH** | Hypogonadotropic — constitutional delay, coeliac/chronic disease, anorexia, **Kallmann (+ anosmia)**, pituitary lesion |

## 1.8 Short stature

Definition: height **<3rd centile (< −2 SD)**, or height markedly below the mid-parental target, or **abnormal height velocity crossing centiles** at any absolute height.

**Mid-parental height:** boys = (mother + father + 13 cm)/2; girls = (mother + father − 13 cm)/2; target range ±8–10 cm.

| Clue → | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| **Normal velocity, bone age = chronological age**, short parents, puberty on time | **Familial short stature** — no investigation needed |
| Normal velocity, **delayed bone age**, "late-developing" parent, delayed puberty, normal final height | **Constitutional delay** — commonest cause of delayed puberty in boys |
| **Short + relatively fat**, ↓velocity, delayed bone age | **Endocrine**: GH deficiency, **hypothyroidism** (lethargy, constipation, goitre), Cushing (striae, ↑BP) |
| Short **girl**, webbed neck, wide-spaced nipples, cubitus valgus, ↑LH/FSH, **coarctation/bicuspid valve** | **Turner syndrome** — karyotype every unexplained short girl |
| Short, **thin**, distended abdomen, anaemia, irritability | **Coeliac disease** |
| **Disproportionate** (↓upper:lower segment, short limbs), FHx | **Skeletal dysplasia** (achondroplasia) |
| Poor growth + polyuria/polydipsia/acidosis | **CKD / renal tubular acidosis** |
| SGA with no catch-up by 2 y | **SGA without catch-up** — ⚑ **not a PBS growth-hormone indication in Australia** (unlike the US/EU); such a child qualifies only via the generic "short stature and slow growth" criteria. PBS categories include Turner, SHOX, Prader–Willi, chronic renal insufficiency and biochemical GHD |

**Workup:** serial heights + velocity, MPH, **bone age (left wrist)**, FBC, UEC/CMP/LFT, **coeliac serology**, TFT, IGF-1, urinalysis, **karyotype in all short girls**. GH stimulation testing is specialist-only.

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 2: Neonatology
*Sources: ANZCOR 13.1–13.9, Queensland Clinical Guidelines, Safer Care Victoria, NSW Health, RANZCO. Scope: delivery room through the common neonatal presentations.*

## 2.1 Newborn resuscitation — ANZCOR

**The short version**
- The problem is **ventilation, not circulation** — lung inflation fixes most newborns.
- **HR <100 → PPV; HR <60 after 30 s of effective ventilation → compressions, 3:1.**
- **Term starts in AIR**; **100% only once compressions start**, then wean. Cord clamping **≥60 s** if vigorous.

| Step | ANZCOR detail |
|---|---|
| At birth | Assess **tone, breathing, heart rate**. Dry, stimulate, warm (**<32 wk: plastic wrap without drying + hat**, room ≥25 °C generally, ⚑ **at least 26 °C for the very preterm**) |
| Cord | **Defer clamping ≥60 s** if vigorous and **≥34 wk**; **≥30 s if <34 wk** and not needing resuscitation; clamp immediately if resuscitation needed |
| Assess HR | **Auscultate** (cord palpation unreliable); **ECG fastest in the first 3 min**; oximeter on **right hand (preductal)** |
| Apnoeic/gasping/**HR <100** | **PPV 40–60 inflations/min**, Ti 0.3–0.5 s; **30 cmH₂O term, 20–25 preterm**; PEEP 5 |
| Not improving | Fix technique first — reposition, suction only if obstructed, ↑pressure, airway adjunct/intubation. **Rising HR is the primary sign of effective ventilation** |
| **HR <60 after 30 s of effective ventilation** | **Compressions: two thumbs, lower third of sternum, one-third AP depth, 3:1 = 90 + 30/min**; FiO₂ to **100%** |
| Still HR <60 | **Adrenaline IV/UVC 10–30 µg/kg (0.1–0.3 mL/kg 1:10,000)** q3–5 min; ETT only if no access, **50–100 µg/kg** |
| Suspected hypovolaemia | **0.9% NaCl or Hartmann's 10 mL/kg IV**; O-negative blood for acute loss |
| Oxygen | **Air term/near-term**; **21–30% <32 wk**; titrate to preductal targets |
| Preductal SpO₂ | **1 min 60–70%, 2 min 65–85%, 3 min 70–90%, 5 min 80–90%, 10 min 85–90%** |
| Meconium, **non-vigorous** | ⚑ **No routine intubation to suction** — PPV as for any apnoeic newborn |
| Post-resus | Glucose and temperature; **therapeutic hypothermia 33–34 °C × 72 h, started within 6 h** for moderate–severe HIE ≥35 wk |

## 2.2 Apgar

Scored at **1 and 5 min** (then q5 min while <7). It **documents condition and response — it does not direct resuscitation** (ANZCOR uses tone/breathing/HR for that).

| 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **A**ppearance: blue/pale | peripheral cyanosis | fully pink |
| **P**ulse: absent | <100 | >100 |
| **G**rimace: none | grimace | cry/cough/sneeze |
| **A**ctivity (tone): flaccid | some flexion | active |
| **R**espiration: absent | slow/irregular/weak cry | strong cry |

Low at 1 min with recovery by 5 min = transitional; **persistently low 5- and 10-min scores + acidosis + encephalopathy** = HIE.

## 2.3 Routine newborn care and screening

**The short version**
- Universal: **vitamin K IM**, **hep B vaccine**, newborn examination, **bloodspot 48–72 h**, **hearing screen before discharge**.
- ⚑ The national bloodspot panel is expanding (SMA and SCID already in; sickle cell disease and X-ALD being added) — the old "4–5 conditions" answer is out of date.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Vitamin K (phytomenadione)** | IM at birth: **1 mg if BW ≥1500 g, 0.5 mg if <1500 g**. If IM declined: **oral 2 mg at birth, day 3–5, day 22–28**. Prevents VKDB — **late VKDB at 2–12 wk with intracranial haemorrhage**, higher risk if exclusively breastfed or **cholestatic** |
| **Hepatitis B** | Birth dose within 24 h (+ HBIG if mother HBsAg+) |
| **Bloodspot** | **48–72 h**, heel prick, **signed consent**. **>25 conditions**: PKU, congenital hypothyroidism, CF, MCAD/other FAO defects, CAH, galactosaemia, **SMA, SCID** ⚑ (national panel still expanding) |
| **Hearing** | Universal screen (e.g. SWISH, NSW) by **automated ABR/OAE before discharge**; diagnosis by 3 mo, intervention by 6 mo. Risk: NICU, congenital CMV, FHx, craniofacial anomaly |
| **Newborn examination** (Australian equivalent of UK NIPE; repeated at 6–8 wk in general practice) | Fontanelles, **red reflex**, palate (look + palpate), **femoral pulses**, precordium, abdomen, **hips**, spine/sacral dimple, testes, anus, tone/Moro, measurements |
| **Red reflex** | **Absent/white (leukocoria) = same-day ophthalmology** — cataract, **retinoblastoma**, retinal detachment, glaucoma |
| **Femoral pulses** | Weak/absent or radiofemoral delay → **coarctation**; check pre/post-ductal SpO₂ |
| **Pulse oximetry screen** | For **critical CHD**: right hand + foot at ~24 h |

**DDH (Safer Care Victoria):** risk factors = **breech (esp. after 34 wk), family history, female, oligohydramnios, neuromuscular disease, BW >5 kg, hips-extended swaddling.**
- **Ortolani** relocates a **dislocated** hip (abduct + lift, clunk *in*); **Barlow** dislocates a **dislocatable** one (adduct + push back, clunk *out*). A **click is not a clunk**.
- Abnormal exam → orthopaedic referral. Risk factors + normal exam → **ultrasound at 6–12 wk** (6–8 wk corrected if preterm). **X-ray useless <6 mo** (cartilaginous head) — ultrasound under 6 mo, X-ray after.
- Late signs: **asymmetric skin creases, limited abduction, Galeazzi sign, late walking, waddling gait**. Treatment: **Pavlik harness <6 mo**; reduction + spica later.

## 2.4 Neonatal jaundice

**The short version**
- **Jaundice <24 h is ALWAYS pathological** — haemolysis (ABO/Rh, G6PD, spherocytosis) or congenital infection; measure SBR urgently.
- Treatment thresholds come from **plotting total SBR against age in hours on a gestation-specific nomogram**, not a remembered number.
- **Prolonged = >14 d (term), >21 d (preterm)** → **measure the conjugated fraction**, exclude **biliary atresia**.

| Timing | Think |
|---|---|
| **<24 h — always pathological** | **Haemolysis**: Rh/ABO incompatibility (mother O or Rh-negative), **G6PD deficiency**, spherocytosis; congenital infection/sepsis |
| **24 h – 14 days** | **Physiological** (peaks day 3–5, unconjugated, well baby); **breastfeeding jaundice**; polycythaemia, cephalhaematoma/bruising, sepsis, Gilbert/Crigler-Najjar |
| **>14 days (prolonged)** | **Breast milk jaundice** (commonest); hypothyroidism; UTI; galactosaemia; **conjugated → biliary atresia, choledochal cyst, neonatal hepatitis, TPN, α1-antitrypsin deficiency** |

| Confusable pair | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Breastfeeding** vs **breast milk** jaundice | Breastfeeding: **first week**, dehydration, **weight loss >10%**, few wet nappies — *not enough milk*; fix the feeding. Breast milk: **day 4–7 onset, peaks 2–3 wk, to 12 wk**, baby **feeding and gaining well** — exclusion diagnosis, continue breastfeeding |
| **Unconjugated** vs **conjugated** | **Conjugated = >34 µmol/L or >20% of total (QCG)** — **always pathological**; **pale stools + dark urine + hepatomegaly = biliary atresia** until excluded (**Kasai best before 6–8 wk**) |
| Physiological vs pathological | Physiological: **>24 h onset**, peaks day 3–5 (later/higher preterm), gone by 14 d, unconjugated, well baby, below treatment line. Anything else = investigate |

**Investigations:** total **and conjugated** SBR, blood group + **DAT (Coombs)**, FBC/film/reticulocytes, **G6PD assay** (Mediterranean, African, SE Asian ancestry), maternal group; if unwell or prolonged add sepsis screen, urine MCS, TFT, galactosaemia screen.

**Treatment:**
- **TcB** screens; confirm with **serum bilirubin if TcB >250 µmol/L or within 50 µmol/L of the phototherapy line**, and always if <24 h, unwell or preterm (TcB unreliable during/after phototherapy).
- **Phototherapy** (450 nm blue-green → water-soluble lumirubin), eye protection, hydration; thresholds **lower with lower gestation and neurotoxicity risk factors (<38 wk, positive DAT, G6PD deficiency, sepsis, instability, albumin <30)**.
- **Exchange transfusion** if above the exchange line, rising despite intensive phototherapy, or acute bilirubin encephalopathy; **IVIG** in isoimmune haemolysis.
- **Kernicterus** — unconjugated bilirubin crosses the BBB into the **basal ganglia**: acute **lethargy, poor feeding, high-pitched cry, retrocollis/opisthotonus, seizures** → **choreoathetoid CP, sensorineural hearing loss, upgaze palsy, enamel dysplasia**.

## 2.5 Respiratory distress in the newborn

**The short version**
- Signs: **tachypnoea >60, grunting, flaring, recession, cyanosis**.
- **Gestation discriminates first**: preterm → RDS; term/late-preterm elective LSCS → TTN; post-term + meconium → MAS.
- Grunting = **auto-PEEP** — the infant is splinting alveoli open.

| Feature | **RDS** | **TTN** | **Meconium aspiration** | **Congenital pneumonia** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gestation** | **Preterm, risk ↑ as GA ↓** | **Term/late preterm, elective LSCS without labour** | **Term/post-term >41 wk** | Any, esp. **preterm + prolonged ROM** |
| Onset | At birth, **worsens over 24–48 h** | Hours, **peaks 24 h, resolves 24–72 h** | From birth, often severe | Birth to 48 h |
| Stem clue | Maternal diabetes, no antenatal steroids, male, second twin | **"Planned caesarean at 38 wk", otherwise well** | **Meconium-stained liquor, stained nails/cord, barrel chest** | **PROM ≥18 h, maternal fever, GBS, chorioamnionitis** |
| CXR | **Ground-glass + air bronchograms, low volumes** | **Fluid in the horizontal fissure, perihilar streaking, hyperinflation** | **Patchy coarse asymmetric infiltrates, hyperinflation ± pneumothorax** | May mimic RDS; **focal consolidation** |
| Management | Early CPAP, **surfactant**; **antenatal steroids prevent it** | **Supportive** — O₂ ± CPAP | Ventilation, **iNO for PPHN**, surfactant, antibiotics | **Benzylpenicillin + gentamicin** + support |
| Trap | — | Exclusion diagnosis; **if >72 h, rethink** | ⚑ **No perineal suctioning or routine intubation** | **Indistinguishable from RDS in a preterm — treat both** |

Also consider: **pneumothorax** (sudden deterioration, asymmetric movement, transilluminates); **CDH** (scaphoid abdomen, bowel sounds in chest — **intubate + NGT, do not bag-mask**); **choanal atresia** (cyanosis relieved by crying, NG won't pass); **duct-dependent CHD** (cyanosis **not** corrected by oxygen → **prostaglandin E1**); sepsis; inborn error.

## 2.6 Neonatal sepsis

| | **Early onset (<72 h, often <24 h)** | **Late onset (>72 h)** |
|---|---|---|
| Source | **Vertical — maternal genital tract** | **Nosocomial / environmental — lines, gut** |
| Organisms | **Group B streptococcus**, *E. coli*, *Listeria monocytogenes* | **Coagulase-negative staphylococci** (long-lines), *S. aureus*, Gram-negatives, *Candida* |
| Risk factors | **Maternal GBS colonisation/bacteriuria, previous EOGBS infant, ROM ≥18 h, <37 wk, intrapartum fever ≥38 °C, chorioamnionitis, inadequate IAP** | **Prematurity, central lines, ventilation, TPN, surgery, prolonged antibiotics** |
| Presentation | Non-specific: **respiratory distress/grunting, temperature instability (hypothermia as often as fever), poor feeding, lethargy, apnoea, hypoglycaemia, jaundice, shock** | Same + **abdominal distension, apnoeas, line-associated instability** |
| Empiric therapy | **Benzylpenicillin (or ampicillin) + gentamicin** | **Flucloxacillin (or vancomycin if line sepsis/MRSA) + gentamicin**; cefotaxime for meningitis |

**GBS prevention — Australia:** Queensland uses a **risk-factor–based** approach to IAP; other jurisdictions use **universal antenatal low vaginal–rectal swab at 35–37 wk** ⚑ — name the state variation in a SAQ. **IAP = benzylpenicillin 3 g IV load then 1.8 g q4h until birth**; **adequate = ≥4 h before birth** (≥2 h accepted, QCG). Well term infant, risk factors, adequate IAP → **observe 48 h**.

**Workup:** blood culture **≥1 mL**, FBC + differential, **LP if clinical signs, positive culture or suspected meningitis**, CXR if respiratory signs, ± serial CRP to guide duration. **Antibiotics within 1 hour** — don't wait for results.

## 2.7 Neonatal hypoglycaemia (QCG)

**The short version**
- Target **BGL ≥2.6 mmol/L in the first 48 h** (≥3.0 from 48–96 h, ≥3.5 after 96 h).
- **Screen the at-risk only**: preterm/post-term, SGA/LGA/LBW/macrosomia, **infant of a diabetic mother**, hypothermia, sepsis/asphyxia, maternal beta-blocker/beta-agonist/betamethasone, FHx metabolic disease.
- **First BGL before the second feed (<3 h)**, second before the third feed (<6 h), then q6h for 24 h.

| Level / situation | Action |
|---|---|
| BGL <2.6, asymptomatic, ≥35 wk, <48 h, feeding | **Feed + 40% dextrose gel 0.5 mL/kg (200 mg/kg) buccal**, recheck in 30 min |
| **<1.5, symptomatic, or persistent despite gel** | **10% glucose 1–2 mL/kg IV bolus** then **60 mL/kg/day** (80 if symptomatic); >12% needs central access |
| Symptoms | Jitteriness, poor feeding, hypotonia, apnoea, **abnormal cry, seizures, coma** |
| Refractory, high glucose infusion rate | **Critical sample during hypoglycaemia** (insulin, C-peptide, cortisol, GH, ketones, lactate, FFA, ammonia, acylcarnitines) → **hyperinsulinism** (no ketones, ± Beckwith-Wiedemann), **CAH, hypopituitarism (micropenis, midline defects), FAO defects** |

## 2.8 Necrotising enterocolitis

**Clue → NEC:** **preterm, formula-fed, feeds recently advanced, days 7–14** → **abdominal distension, bilious aspirates, bloody stools, discoloured tender abdominal wall**, with apnoeas, thrombocytopenia, metabolic acidosis.
- **AXR: pneumatosis intestinalis (intramural gas) is pathognomonic**; also dilated loops, portal venous gas (poor prognosis), **free air/Rigler's sign = perforation → surgery**.
- Management: **NBM, NG decompression, IV fluids/TPN, broad-spectrum antibiotics ± metronidazole**, serial AXRs. Protective: **breast milk** (± probiotics). Complications: strictures, short gut.
- Discriminator: **spontaneous intestinal perforation** is earlier (first week), smallest infants, **no pneumatosis, bluish abdomen**.

## 2.9 Birth injuries

| Clue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Boggy scalp swelling **crossing sutures**, present at birth | **Caput succedaneum** — resolves in days |
| Fluctuant swelling **limited by sutures**, appears hours later, ± jaundice | **Cephalhaematoma** (subperiosteal) — weeks; jaundice risk |
| **Crossing sutures, expanding, shock, anaemia** (esp. ventouse) | **Subgaleal haemorrhage — can hold the entire blood volume; resuscitate** |
| Arm adducted, internally rotated, **"waiter's tip"**, absent Moro, grasp intact | **Erb's palsy (C5–6)** — shoulder dystocia/macrosomia; usually recovers |
| **Claw hand**, weak grip ± **Horner's** | **Klumpke's palsy (C8–T1)** — worse prognosis |
| Clavicular crepitus/lump, asymmetric Moro, pseudoparalysis | **Clavicle fracture** — commonest birth fracture |
| Facial asymmetry, eye won't close, worse crying | **Facial nerve palsy** — forceps; usually resolves |

## 2.10 Prematurity and its complications

**The short version**
- **Preterm <37 wk; extremely preterm <28 wk. LBW <2500 g, VLBW <1500 g, ELBW <1000 g.**
- **Betamethasone 24–34 wk** reduces RDS, IVH, NEC and mortality; **MgSO₄ <30 wk for neuroprotection** ⚑ — both standard Australian practice.
- Answer complications by system — that is how SAQs are marked.

| System | Complication and key detail |
|---|---|
| Respiratory | **RDS** (surfactant, CPAP); **apnoea of prematurity** (caffeine); **BPD = O₂ requirement at 36 wk corrected**; pneumothorax |
| CNS | **IVH** (germinal matrix, first 72 h, grades I–IV) and **periventricular leukomalacia → spastic diplegic CP** — cranial US screening |
| Cardiac | **PDA** — bounding pulses, wide pulse pressure, **continuous machinery murmur**, hard to wean support; ibuprofen/paracetamol or ligation |
| GI | **NEC**, feed intolerance, TPN cholestasis; breast milk protective |
| Eyes | **ROP** — screen **≤30–32 wk or ≤1250–1500 g (unit-dependent)**, first exam **31–32 wk PMA or 4–6 wk postnatal, whichever later**; treat **type 1 within 72 h** (laser or anti-VEGF) ⚑ |
| Metabolic | **Hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, hypocalcaemia**, metabolic bone disease, jaundice at lower thresholds — poor stores, high surface area:mass |
| Haem/ID | Anaemia of prematurity; **early and late sepsis**; no maternal IgG before ~32 wk |
| Long term | Neurodevelopmental impairment, CP, hearing/vision loss, chronic lung disease; **immunise by chronological, not corrected, age** |

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 3: The Acutely Unwell Child

*Sources: RCH Melbourne CPGs, Children's Health Queensland, ANZCOR, ASCIA, Therapeutic Guidelines. Australian practice; UK/NICE divergences flagged. Written finals scope — recognition, thresholds, first drug, first dose.*

## 3.1 Recognising the seriously ill child

Children deteriorate predictably: **respiratory or circulatory failure → arrest**; primary cardiac arrest is rare. Assess from the end of the bed first (paediatric assessment triangle): **appearance, work of breathing, circulation to skin**.

**Work of breathing** — earliest and most sensitive: tachypnoea (age-adjusted), flaring, tracheal tug, recession, head bobbing, grunting (auto-PEEP — ominous in an infant). **A distressed child whose work of breathing suddenly falls is exhausting, not improving.** Silent chest, bradypnoea and apnoea are pre-terminal.

**Circulation** — BP is held up by tachycardia and vasoconstriction until very late, so **hypotension is a pre-arrest sign**. Use central capillary refill (>2 s), peripheral–central temperature gradient, pulse volume, urine output. Tachycardia disproportionate to fever and persisting after antipyretics and fluid is the discriminator.

**Conscious state** — AVPU; **below "V" ≈ GCS ≤8**, airway at risk. Inconsolable irritability or a floppy infant weighs as much as drowsiness. **"This is not my child" from a parent is data.**

The **traffic-light system** (green/amber/red across colour, activity, respiratory, circulation/hydration, plus "other" — fever ≥38 °C under 3 months, non-blanching rash, bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, status epilepticus, focal neurology) is a **NICE (UK) tool**, widely taught here but ⚑ **not formally adopted by RCH or Queensland**.

**The short version**
- Kids compensate then crash — **hypotension is pre-arrest**; tachycardia + poor perfusion is the alarm.
- Falling work of breathing in a tired child = deterioration, not recovery.
- Traffic-light = NICE; Australian tools = RCH/Qld sepsis and febrile-child pathways.

| Domain | Concerning | Critical |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Irritable, less active | Floppy, **<"V" on AVPU** (i.e. P or U) |
| Breathing | Tachypnoea, recession, flaring | Grunting, silent chest, apnoea |
| Circulation | Tachycardia, mottled, CRT 2–3 s | Cold, weak pulses, **hypotension** |
| Skin | Pallor | Mottled/cyanosed, **non-blanching rash** |

## 3.2 Paediatric sepsis and meningococcaemia

Sepsis = suspected/proven infection **plus** organ dysfunction. Triggers: unwell appearance, altered conscious state, tachypnoea, tachycardia or poor perfusion, unexplained severe pain, fever *or* hypothermia. **Absence of fever does not exclude sepsis** in neonates or the immunocompromised.

**Fever + purpura + shock = meningococcaemia until disproven.** Give **ceftriaxone immediately — do not wait for cultures, LP or transfer**; RCH specifies **within 15 minutes**. Rash: petechial/purpuric, non-blanching, often flexural/pressure areas, spreading fast; ⚑ **an early blanching maculopapular rash does not exclude it** — re-examine the fully undressed child.

**Ceftriaxone dosing (Australian):**
- **Suspected meningococcal disease / meningitis: ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV (max 4 g) daily** — RCH CPGs and the CHQ Antibiocard agree. ⚑ The commonly memorised "50 mg/kg" is the *non-CNS* figure; **use the meningitis dose once meningococcaemia or meningitis is on the table.**
- **Neonates <4 weeks:** cefotaxime 50 mg/kg IV **plus benzylpenicillin 60 mg/kg IV** — ceftriaxone avoided in the jaundiced neonate and with calcium-containing fluids.
- **Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg) 6-hourly for 4 days**, **with or just before the first antibiotic**, for bacterial meningitis beyond the neonatal period. **Not in neonates; never a reason to delay antibiotics.**

**LP is contraindicated while shocked.** Also defer for reduced/fluctuating GCS, focal neurology, ongoing seizures, coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia (DIC), respiratory compromise, or local infection. **Defer the LP, give the antibiotic** — blood culture and meningococcal PCR retain yield for hours afterwards.

**Notifiable.** Household/intimate/childcare contacts need clearance chemoprophylaxis (**single-dose ciprofloxacin, or rifampicin**) via the public health unit; so does the index case unless given ceftriaxone.

## 3.3 Shock and fluid resuscitation

Usually **hypovolaemic** (gastroenteritis, burns) or **distributive** (sepsis, anaphylaxis). Cardiogenic shock in an infant → **duct-dependent congenital heart disease** (collapse at 1–2 weeks as the duct closes, absent femorals, worse with oxygen — **prostaglandin E1, not fluid**) or myocarditis. **In cardiogenic shock, boluses harm.**

**Bolus: 10–20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid (0.9% saline or balanced solution) fast, then reassess.** ⚑ Sources diverge on the aliquot — **ANZCOR PALS 10 mL/kg**, **RCH 10–20 mL/kg**. Safe answer: **20 mL/kg in hypovolaemic shock; 10 mL/kg aliquots where the heart may be the problem** (neonates, cardiac disease, DKA, malnutrition). **Escalate to inotropes — not a fourth bolus — after ~40 mL/kg**; adrenaline/noradrenaline can run peripherally or IO.

**Maintenance fluid (RCH, "4/2/1"):**

| Weight | Per hour | Per 24 h |
|---|---|---|
| First 10 kg | 4 mL/kg | 100 mL/kg |
| Next 10 kg (10–20 kg) | +2 mL/kg | +50 mL/kg |
| Each kg >20 kg | +1 mL/kg | +20 mL/kg |
| Ceiling | ~100 mL/h | ~2400 mL/day |

**Fluid type: isotonic — 0.9% sodium chloride with 5% glucose ± potassium** (RCH). ⚑ **Hypotonic fluids (0.45% saline, "N/4", 4%/0.18%) are no longer used for maintenance** — the biggest change from older texts. Mechanism: the unwell child (pain, vomiting, respiratory illness, meningitis, post-op, ventilated) has **non-osmotic ADH release** and retains free water, so hypotonic fluid causes **acute hyponatraemia → cerebral oedema, seizures, death**. Also **run two-thirds maintenance in unwell non-dehydrated children** and **check electrolytes before starting and daily**.

Access: **if IV fails twice or takes >90 s in a shocked child, go intraosseous** — anything IV can go IO.

## 3.4 Paediatric BLS and ALS — ANZCOR

**DRSABCD.** Infant <1 year, child 1 year to puberty; beyond puberty use adult algorithms.

| Element | ANZCOR paediatric | Contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Ratio, health professional | **15:2** (single or two rescuers) | Adult 30:2 |
| Ratio, lay rescuer | **30:2** for all ages | Simplicity over physiology |
| Initial ventilations | **2 initial breaths if a bag-mask is immediately available**, otherwise start compressions | ⚑ ERC/UK teaches **5** initial rescue breaths |
| Rate / depth | 100–120/min; **≥⅓ AP diameter** (~4 cm infant, ~5 cm child) | Adult ~5 cm |
| Technique | Infant **two-thumb encircling**; child one or two hands | — |
| Defibrillation | **4 J/kg first and all subsequent shocks** | Adult biphasic 200 J |
| Adrenaline | **10 microgram/kg IV/IO, max single dose 1 mg**, repeat every 3–5 min (every second loop) | Adult 1 mg |
| Adrenaline timing | Shockable: **after the 2nd shock**; non-shockable: as early as possible | ⚑ **UK/ERC: after the 3rd shock** |
| Amiodarone | **5 mg/kg (max 300 mg)** for shock-refractory VF/pVT | Adult 300 mg |
| Fluid | 10 mL/kg crystalloid bolus for hypovolaemia | — |

**Differences from adult ALS, for a short answer:** arrest is usually **hypoxic/asphyxial** so airway and ventilation come first and compression-only CPR is inadequate; **15:2** for trained rescuers; **all doses weight-based**; **4 J/kg throughout, no escalation**; shockable rhythms uncommon (suspect with known cardiac disease, sudden collapse, channelopathy). Reversible causes skew to **hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypothermia**.

## 3.5 Fever assessment

**Fever = >38.0 °C.** Height of fever and antipyretic response are **poor discriminators** of serious bacterial infection — **appearance is the most useful single indicator**. Antipyretics are for comfort, not diagnosis.

**Fever without focus, by age (RCH febrile child pathway):**

| Age | Approach |
|---|---|
| **≤28 days** | **Full septic screen regardless of appearance**: FBE, CRP, blood culture, urine (SPA), **LP**, ± CXR. **Admit + empiric IV antibiotics.** Add HSV cover if encephalopathic/vesicles/maternal history |
| **29 days–3 months** | Guided by appearance and whether a focus is obvious; **urine in essentially all**; LP + antibiotics if unwell or investigations abnormal/delayed. Discharge only if well, normal results, senior-reviewed, **follow-up 12–24 h** |
| **>3 months** | Appearance-driven. Well with a clear viral focus → no investigation. **Urine if no focus.** Bloods/imaging if unwell or red flags |

**Fever without focus at any age = check the urine** — UTI is the commonest occult bacterial infection and the commonest miss.

**Fever >7 days** (the paediatric FUO threshold — shorter than the adult 3 weeks). Broaden to:
- **Kawasaki disease** — **fever ≥5 days plus ≥4 of: bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, mucositis/cracked lips/strawberry tongue, polymorphous rash, extremity changes (erythema/oedema then periungual peeling), cervical node >1.5 cm.** Incomplete forms common in infants <1 year, who carry the **highest coronary aneurysm risk** — echo and IVIG.
- Malignancy (ALL, neuroblastoma), systemic JIA (quotidian fever, evanescent salmon rash, arthritis), IBD, osteomyelitis/deep abscess, endocarditis, TB, **travel-related infection — ask about travel, take a malaria film.**

## 3.6 Anaphylaxis (ASCIA)

**Definition:** acute **airway/breathing/circulation compromise**, or acute skin/mucosal change **plus** persistent severe GI symptoms or hypotension after likely allergen exposure. ⚑ **In infants the first sign may be persistent cough, hoarse cry, floppiness or pallor — not urticaria.** Wheeze may be the only respiratory feature: a known asthmatic wheezing after allergen exposure has anaphylaxis until proven otherwise.

**Adrenaline IM into the anterolateral thigh is first-line and time-critical — no antihistamine, steroid or nebuliser step comes before it. Dose: 10 microgram/kg (0.01 mg/kg) of 1:1000, max 0.5 mg; repeat every 5 minutes as needed.**

| Age (approx weight) | Volume of 1:1000 IM | Autoinjector |
|---|---|---|
| <1 y (~5–7.5 kg) | 0.1 mL | 150 microgram — **standard band is 7.5–20 kg**; ⚑ devices are **not usually recommended below 7.5 kg** (individual assessment) |
| 1–2 y (~10 kg) | 0.1 mL | 150 microgram |
| 2–4 y (~15 kg) | 0.15 mL | 150 microgram |
| 4–6 y (~20 kg) | 0.2 mL | 150 microgram (7.5–20 kg) |
| 7–10 y (~30 kg) | 0.3 mL | **300 microgram (>20 kg)** |
| 10–12 y (~40 kg) | 0.4 mL | 300 microgram |
| >12 y (>50 kg) | 0.5 mL | 300 microgram (Anapen 500 >50 kg) |

⚑ Volumes <0.1 mL are unreliable to draw and are a documented **10-fold overdose** trap.

**Posture kills. Lay the patient flat (left lateral if pregnant/vomiting); do not sit or stand them, never walk them** — sudden death on standing (empty-ventricle syndrome). Sit only if breathing is impossible flat.

**Refractory:** adrenaline infusion — **1 mL of 1:1000 in 1000 mL 0.9% saline (≈1 microgram/mL) at 5 mL/kg/h (≈0.1 microgram/kg/min)**, titrated, plus fluid and oxygen; salbutamol only as an adjunct for persistent bronchospasm.

**Observe ≥4 hours after the last adrenaline dose** (longer/overnight if severe, protracted, or remote). Discharge with **autoinjector prescribed and demonstrated**, ASCIA **Action Plan**, avoidance advice, **allergy referral**. Antihistamines treat itch only; **steroids do not prevent biphasic reactions** and are no longer routine.

## 3.7 Choking and drowning (ANZCOR, in brief)

**Choking (FBAO).** **Effective cough** → encourage and observe. **Ineffective cough** → call for help, **up to 5 sharp back blows** (infant head-down across lap/forearm), then **up to 5 chest thrusts** (compression landmark, sharper, slower), checking between each. Alternate until relieved or unresponsive → **start CPR**, removing only what is visible (**no blind finger sweeps**).
⚑ **ANZCOR does not recommend abdominal thrusts at any age** — a genuine Australia/NZ vs UK/US divergence and a favourite MCQ.

**Drowning.** Hypoxic mechanism → **airway and ventilation first**; **compression-only CPR is strongly discouraged**. **Do not attempt to drain water or expel frothy fluid** — it re-accumulates; ventilate through it. Spinal precautions only if the mechanism suggests it (diving, surf) — **rescue and airway take priority**. Prolonged resuscitation is justified in the hypothermic child; observe survivors for **delayed pulmonary oedema**.

| Clue / question | Answer |

| Hypotension in a child | **Pre-arrest sign** |

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 4: Paediatric Respiratory

*Sources: RCH Melbourne CPGs, Children's Health Queensland, Australian Asthma Handbook, Therapeutic Guidelines, Australian Immunisation Handbook, TSANZ. Australian dosing; UK divergences flagged.*

## 4.1 Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)

Parainfluenza; **6 months–3 years**, autumn/winter, **worse at night**. **Barking cough + inspiratory stridor + hoarse voice after 1–2 days of coryza, child otherwise interactive and drinking.** Graded on **stridor at rest and work of breathing**, not the loudness of the cough.

| Grade | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Barking cough, **no stridor at rest**, no/minimal recession | **Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg oral**; discharge |
| Moderate | Stridor at rest, moderate recession, still consolable | Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg oral (Qld: 0.15–0.3 mg/kg, max 12 mg); observe |
| Severe | **Persistent stridor at rest ± expiratory**, marked recession, agitation, **SpO₂ ≤93%** | **Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg (max 12 mg) PO/IM/IV + nebulised adrenaline** |
| Life-threatening | Quiet stridor, exhaustion, poor effort, drowsy, cyanosis | Adrenaline + steroid + **100% O₂ 15 L/min**, call for senior airway/ICU |

**Nebulised adrenaline: 5 mL of undiluted 1:1000**, repeatable at ~10-min intervals. ⚑ **Australian practice gives a flat 5 mL regardless of weight.** Works within 10–30 min but **wears off by ~2 h** → **observe 3 hours after it**. **Prednisolone 1 mg/kg** is the fallback if dexamethasone is unavailable, but needs a **second dose next day**. ⚑ **Nebulised budesonide (2 mg) is second-line**, reserved for the child who is vomiting or cannot tolerate oral steroid — it has not been withdrawn from Australian practice.

**Do not** examine the throat, cannulate or upset a child with stridor at rest — agitation worsens turbulent flow and can precipitate complete obstruction. Discharge once **stridor-free at rest**.

## 4.2 Bronchiolitis ⚑

**RSV in the great majority; <12 months**, peak 3–6 months, winter. Coryza 1–3 days → **cough, tachypnoea, widespread fine crackles ± wheeze, feeding difficulty, apnoea in young infants.** **Worst on days 3–5** — tell parents, as this drives representations.

**Supportive care only. The examinable point is what you must not do:**

| Intervention | Verdict (RCH) |
|---|---|
| **Salbutamol / beta-2 agonists** | **Not indicated — including in atopic infants** ⚑ |
| **Corticosteroids**, any route | **Not indicated** |
| **Antibiotics**, incl. azithromycin | **Not indicated** |
| **Nebulised adrenaline** | Not indicated except peri-arrest |
| **Hypertonic saline, physiotherapy, antivirals** | Not indicated |
| **Chest X-ray, routine bloods/swabs** | **Not indicated** — CXR drives needless antibiotics; diagnosis is clinical |

**Indicated:** **oxygen when SpO₂ persistently <90%** (⚑ **NICE agrees**: <90% for infants over 6 weeks, <92% only for babies **under 6 weeks** or any child with underlying disease — there is no Australia/UK split here), **NG or IV hydration** when feeding poorly, **minimal handling**, **superficial** nasal suction if it helps feeding or apnoea (**deep suction is not recommended**). Escalate to **HFNP** if SpO₂ stays <90% or no improvement after ~2 h of low-flow oxygen. Discharge when **SpO₂ ≥90% and feeding adequately**.

**Apnoea** may be the presenting feature in infants <2 months (especially ex-premature) and mandates admission. Severe-disease risk: **age <10 weeks, prematurity, chronic lung disease, congenital heart disease, neuromuscular disease, immunodeficiency, Indigenous background.** ⚑ **Prevention has changed:** long-acting RSV monoclonal antibody (**nirsevimab**) and maternal RSV vaccination are now in Australian infant programs — confirm eligibility in the Immunisation Handbook.

## 4.3 Asthma in children

Diagnosis under 6 years is clinical — **spirometry is unobtainable before ~6 years**, so recurrent wheeze + bronchodilator response + atopy/family history carries it. Distinguish **preschool viral-induced wheeze** (wheeze only with URTIs, no interval symptoms, no atopy).

| Severity | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Normal mental state, mild ↑WOB, talks in sentences | Salbutamol via spacer; review 20 min |
| Moderate | Some ↑WOB, tachypnoea, short sentences | Salbutamol q20min ×3, **oral steroid**, reassess |
| Severe | **Marked ↑WOB, agitated, pale**, unable to complete sentences | Salbutamol q20min + **ipratropium ×3 doses**, oxygen, oral/IV steroid, senior review |
| Life-threatening | **Maximal WOB, confused/drowsy, cyanosed, silent chest** | Continuous nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium, O₂, IV steroid, **IV magnesium**, ICU |

**Doses (Qld paediatric emergency guideline, cross-checked against the Australian Asthma Handbook):**
- **Salbutamol MDI + spacer: 6 puffs if ≤5 years, 12 puffs if ≥6 years** (100 microgram/puff), repeated **every 20 min for the first hour**, then spaced by response.
- **Nebulised salbutamol: 2.5 mg (≤5 y), 5 mg (≥6 y)** — reserve nebulisers for severe/life-threatening disease; ⚑ **a spacer is at least as effective in mild–moderate asthma**, with fewer side effects.
- **Ipratropium (severe only): ≤5 y 4 puffs / 250 microgram neb; ≥6 y 8 puffs / 500 microgram neb — every 20 min for the **first hour (3 doses)**, then **4–6 hourly for up to 24 h if still required**; it is the first-hour burst that stops, not the drug.
- **Prednisolone 1 mg/kg (max 50 mg) daily for 3 days** (Australian Asthma Handbook). ⚑ **Sources differ** — Queensland: **2 mg/kg day 1 then 1 mg/kg days 2–3**, or **dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg (max 16 mg) once**.
- **IV magnesium sulfate: 50 mg/kg (0.2 mmol/kg) over 20 min** (RCH max 8 mmol; Qld max 10 mmol).
- **IV salbutamol (Qld): 15 microgram/kg over 10 min (max 300 microgram), then 0.5–1 microgram/kg/min.**

**Preventer (ICS) indications:** symptoms or reliever use **>2 days/week**, night waking, ≥1 flare needing oral steroids in the past year, or any life-threatening flare. ⚑ **The adult ICS-formoterol reliever/MART approach does not transfer to young children — under 12s use a SABA reliever plus a separate ICS preventer.** Escalate by raising ICS dose or adding montelukast (counsel on **neuropsychiatric effects**) before LABA.

**Spacer technique:** **one puff at a time**, shake between puffs, **4 tidal breaths** each; **mask <4 years**, mouthpiece after; **never fire several puffs in at once**; wash monthly and **drip-dry, do not towel-dry** (static); rinse mouth after ICS. Check **technique and adherence before escalating dose** — **uncontrolled asthma stunts growth more than ICS does** — and give a written **Asthma Action Plan**.

## 4.4 Community-acquired pneumonia

**Viral predominates under 2 years; *Streptococcus pneumoniae* leads bacterial causes at all ages; *Mycoplasma* matters in school-age/adolescence** (gradual onset, headache, dry cough, CXR worse than the child looks). **Fever + tachypnoea is the most useful pair**; focal signs may be absent in infants. **Think pneumonia in the febrile child with abdominal pain and no abdominal signs.**

**CXR is not required for uncomplicated CAP** — reserve it for suspected complicated disease (effusion, empyema), failure to improve at 48–72 h, or suspected foreign body; repeat at ~6 weeks only for complicated or recurrent same-lobe disease.

**Antibiotics (CHQ Antibiocard / Therapeutic Guidelines):**
- **Amoxicillin 30 mg/kg oral 8-hourly (max 1 g/dose)** — first line, oral, even for many children admitted.
- **Benzylpenicillin 60 mg/kg IV 6-hourly (max 2.4 g/dose)** if oral not tolerated.
- **Azithromycin 10 mg/kg oral daily (max 500 mg)** if *Mycoplasma* likely or penicillin hypersensitivity; **doxycycline** if ≥8 years (50/75/100 mg BD by weight).
Admit for **SpO₂ <90%**, moderate–severe work of breathing, poor hydration, or young age. Still febrile at 48 h → **think empyema** (ultrasound first, not CT).

## 4.5 Pertussis

**Clue → diagnosis: paroxysmal cough with post-tussive vomiting, inspiratory whoop, child well between paroxysms.** ⚑ **In infants <6 months the whoop is often absent — apnoea or cyanosis is the presentation**, and this group carries the mortality. Stages: catarrhal (1–2 weeks, most infectious) → paroxysmal (2–6 weeks) → convalescent ("100-day cough"). **Marked lymphocytosis** supports it in infants.

**Diagnosis: nasopharyngeal swab PCR** (best yield in the first 2–3 weeks); serology for late presentations. **Notifiable.**

**Treatment (macrolide) — shortens infectivity; alters symptoms only if started within ~2–3 weeks of cough onset:**
- **Azithromycin: <6 months — 10 mg/kg daily for 5 days; ≥6 months — 10 mg/kg (max 500 mg) day 1, then 5 mg/kg (max 250 mg) days 2–5.**
- **Clarithromycin 7.5 mg/kg/dose (max 500 mg) BD for 7 days** — **avoid <1 month; azithromycin preferred** (erythromycin–pyloric stenosis association).
- **Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole 4+20 mg/kg BD for 7 days** if macrolide contraindicated — ⚑ **only over 6 weeks of age** (sulfonamide/kernicterus risk).

**Exclusion: until the earliest of — 5 days of appropriate antibiotics, 14 days from onset of paroxysmal cough, or 21 days from onset of symptoms.**
**Household/close-contact prophylaxis** (same macrolide regimen, ideally within 14 days of exposure) targets households with **an infant <6 months** or **a woman in the last month of pregnancy** — not blanket prophylaxis. **Prevention: dTpa in every pregnancy (from 20 weeks) plus the primary schedule at 6 weeks, 4 and 6 months** — maternal vaccination plus cocooning protects the newborn.

## 4.6 Epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis and inhaled foreign body

**The short version**
- **Rapid, toxic, drooling, no cough → epiglottitis; toxic with barking cough unresponsive to adrenaline → bacterial tracheitis.**
- **Sudden choking in a well toddler → inhaled foreign body; a normal CXR does not exclude it.**
- **Never examine the throat or distress a child with acute upper airway obstruction.**

| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis | Bacterial tracheitis | Foreign body |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | 1–2 days | **Hours** | Days (post-URTI deterioration) | **Instant** |
| Cough | **Barking** | **Absent/minimal** | Barking, then productive/thick | Choking episode, then may settle |
| Voice | Hoarse | **Muffled ("hot potato")** | Hoarse | Normal |
| Fever/toxicity | Low grade, well | **High, toxic** | **High, toxic** | Afebrile |
| Drooling/dysphagia | No | **Yes, tripod posture, neck extended** | No | No |
| Organism | Parainfluenza | ***H. influenzae* type b** — ⚑ rare post-Hib; think unimmunised/immunocompromised, and strep/staph now relatively commoner | ***S. aureus***, strep | — |
| Response to nebulised adrenaline | Good | Poor | **Poor — the key clue** | None |
| Management | Steroid ± adrenaline | **Do not examine.** Senior airway team to theatre; intubate; **ceftriaxone** | Intubation likely, **antistaphylococcal cover (flucloxacillin) + ceftriaxone**, ICU | Rigid bronchoscopy |

**Inhaled foreign body:** peanut/food in a toddler. Acute — choking, cough, stridor (laryngeal) or unilateral wheeze/reduced air entry (bronchial, **usually right main**). Chronic — **persistent monophonic wheeze, recurrent pneumonia or collapse in the same lobe**. Inspiratory CXR often normal; **expiratory/decubitus films may show air trapping (ball-valve)**. **Suspicion alone justifies bronchoscopy.**

## 4.7 Cystic fibrosis

**Autosomal recessive**, defective CFTR chloride channel; **ΔF508 commonest** in Australia. **Screening: newborn bloodspot — raised immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) → CFTR variant panel → sweat test.** **Sweat chloride ≥60 mmol/L diagnostic; 30–59 intermediate; <30 normal.**

**Presentations beyond screening:** **meconium ileus at birth** (near-pathognomonic), failure to thrive with **bulky, greasy, offensive stools** (pancreatic insufficiency, ~85%), recurrent chest infection/persistent wet cough, **rectal prolapse**, **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic alkalosis with hyponatraemic dehydration in hot weather** (pseudo-Bartter — an Australian-climate classic), nasal polyps, and later **CF-related diabetes, distal intestinal obstruction syndrome, infertility (CBAVD)**.

**Management** — multidisciplinary CF centre: **airway clearance physiotherapy and exercise; inhaled mucolytics (dornase alfa, hypertonic saline); early *Pseudomonas* eradication; segregation against cross-infection; pancreatic enzymes with vitamins A, D, E, K; high-energy high-salt diet; surveillance for CF-related diabetes and liver disease.** ⚑ **CFTR modulators (elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor) are now standard for eligible genotypes and have transformed prognosis** — genotype-dependent, not universal.

## 4.8 OSA, adenotonsillar hypertrophy and chronic cough

**Peaks at 2–6 years**, when adenotonsillar tissue is largest relative to the airway. ⚑ **Unlike adults, the child is often neither obese nor sleepy — they are hyperactive, inattentive and irritable**, so OSA belongs in the differential of a "possible ADHD" referral. **Ask about habitual snoring, witnessed apnoeas, mouth breathing, restless sleep with neck extension, enuresis, morning headache, poor growth.** High risk: **Down syndrome, obesity, craniofacial syndromes, neuromuscular disease**.

**Polysomnography is the gold standard** but access is limited here; **overnight oximetry is a useful positive screen** (desaturation clusters confirm; normal does not exclude). **Adenotonsillectomy is first-line** with adenotonsillar hypertrophy; high-risk children (age <2, severe OSA, comorbidity) need inpatient post-operative monitoring. Untreated: **pulmonary hypertension/cor pulmonale, growth failure, neurocognitive and behavioural impairment.**

**Chronic cough = >4 weeks in children** (⚑ shorter than the adult 8 weeks). Characterise it:
- **Wet cough >4 weeks, no other cause → protracted bacterial bronchitis (PBB)**, the commonest cause in preschoolers. **Amoxicillin–clavulanate for 2 weeks** ⚑ (DACS trial: 2 weeks non-inferior to 4). **Failure after two courses, or recurrent episodes → investigate for bronchiectasis** — prevalent in Indigenous Australian children, and the reason wet cough is never dismissed.
- **Dry cough, wheeze, nocturnal symptoms** → asthma. **Barking cough absent during sleep** → habit (tic) cough.
- **Cough since birth or with feeding** → aspiration, TOF, congenital lesion.
- **Red flags → CXR and referral:** haemoptysis, clubbing, failure to thrive, chest deformity, focal signs, choking history, wet cough since infancy (**CF, primary ciliary dyskinesia, immunodeficiency, bronchiectasis**).

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 5: Paediatric Gastroenterology and Surgery
*RCH Melbourne CPGs, Queensland Clinical Guidelines, Therapeutic Guidelines, RACGP; Australian written finals register. Divergence from NICE/UK flagged. ⚑ = changed from older textbooks.*

## 5.1 Gastroenteritis and assessment of dehydration
Gastroenteritis = **diarrhoea ± vomiting**; vomiting *without* diarrhoea should make you doubt it, especially <6 months. ⚑ Rotavirus has fallen sharply post-NIP; **norovirus** now dominates. Bloody stool + high fever + severe pain → bacterial (*Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*). Dehydration is assessed **clinically**; best measure = **acute weight loss vs premorbid weight** (1 kg = 1 L).

| | Mild <5% | Moderate 5–9% | Severe ≥10% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conscious state | Alert | Lethargic *or* irritable | **Reduced** |
| Circulation | Normal | Mild tachycardia, CRT prolonged | Tachycardia, **cold/mottled, weak pulses, hypotension (terminal)** |
| Breathing | Normal | Mildly raised RR | **Deep acidotic (Kussmaul)** |
| Eyes, mucosa, turgor | Normal, moist | Sunken, dry, reduced | Deeply sunken, parched, doughy |

**Clue → diagnosis:** no signs → assume <5%; **reduced conscious state + cold peripheries** → ≥10% and shock. Thirst, sunken eyes, dry mucosa come **early**; **hypotension is pre-arrest** in children.

**The short version**
- Weight loss quantifies the deficit; clinical signs triage it.
- Enteral (oral → NG) beats IV for mild–moderate dehydration.
- IV is for shock, failed NG, ileus or altered conscious state.
- Ondansetron in >6 months reduces admission.

| Severity | Route | Regimen |
|---|---|---|
| Mild–moderate | **Oral ORS first-line** | **10 mL/kg/hr** ORS (Gastrolyte/Hydralyte), small frequent sips |
| Oral failing/refused | **Nasogastric** ⚑ | Same volume via NG — Australian units use NG far more readily than the UK, which escalates to IV sooner |
| Shock / failed NG / ileus / ↓LOC | **IV** | **20 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl bolus**, repeat; escalate if ≥40 mL/kg needed |

⚑ Maintenance and deficit use **isotonic fluid (0.9% NaCl + 5% glucose ± KCl)** — hypotonic (0.18/0.45%) is obsolete after deaths from hyponatraemic encephalopathy. Holliday-Segar: 100/50/20 mL/kg/day per successive 10 kg. **Ondansetron** (>6 months): 8–15 kg → 2 mg, 15–30 kg → 4 mg, >30 kg → 8 mg. **No loperamide.**

**Electrolytes** only pre-IV, severe dehydration, <6 months, or abnormal conscious state. **Doughy turgor + jittery + disproportionate thirst → hypernatraemic dehydration**: correct **over 48 h** (Na fall ≤0.5 mmol/L/hr).

**Red flags against gastroenteritis:** <6 months, **bilious vomit or haematemesis**, diarrhoea >10 days, severe pain, refusal to weight-bear, re-presentation → UTI, intussusception, DKA, surgical abdomen, sepsis.

## 5.2 Vomiting in the neonate and infant — the bilious-vomiting emergency
**Bilious (green) vomiting in a neonate is malrotation with midgut volvulus until excluded** — bile means obstruction below the ampulla. Same-hour problem.

| Diagnosis | Age / clue | Discriminator | Investigation | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Malrotation + midgut volvulus** | Any age, **75% in month 1**; well, then suddenly not | **Bilious vomit; abdomen deceptively soft early**; PR blood = ischaemia (late) | **Urgent upper GI contrast** — corkscrew duodenum, DJ flexure right of midline; AXR may be normal | NBM/NG/fluids → **Ladd's procedure**; peritonitic/shocked → theatre |
| **Duodenal atresia** | Day 1; **T21 ~30%**, polyhydramnios | **Bilious** (post-ampullary); **"double bubble" with NO distal gas** (volvulus has gas) | AXR | NG, fluids, repair once stable |
| **Pyloric stenosis** | **3–6 weeks**, firstborn male, FHx | **Non-bilious projectile vomit, hungry afterwards**; visible peristalsis; RUQ **"olive"**; **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic alkalosis** + paradoxical aciduria | **US: muscle >3 mm, channel >15–17 mm** | Correct alkalosis/Cl/K *first*, then **Ramstedt pyloromyotomy** — medical emergency, surgical semi-emergency |
| **Hirschsprung** | **No meconium in first 48 h**; constipation from birth; T21 | **Explosive stool/gas on PR withdrawal**, distension | Contrast enema (transition zone); **suction rectal biopsy definitive — no ganglion cells** | Washouts, pull-through. **Enterocolitis** (fever, foul diarrhoea, toxic) is lethal |

Also: **oesophageal atresia/TOF** (polyhydramnios, chokes on first feed, **NG coils in the upper pouch on CXR**, VACTERL); **physiological GOR** (**thriving, happy**); **sepsis, UTI, raised ICP**; **salt-wasting CAH at 1–3 weeks (↓Na, ↑K → saline + hydrocortisone)**.

**The short version**
- Green vomit + neonate = contrast study now.
- Non-bilious projectile at 3–6 weeks in a hungry baby = pyloric stenosis; fix biochemistry before theatre.
- Double bubble **without** distal gas = duodenal atresia; **with** gas = suspect volvulus.
- No meconium by 48 h = Hirschsprung until disproved.

**Hook:** losing gastric HCl → alkalosis; the kidney then trades K⁺ and H⁺ for Na⁺ → **hypokalaemia + paradoxical aciduria**. Anaesthesia before correction risks apnoea.

## 5.3 Intussusception
Usually **ileocolic**, peak **3 months–2 years**, boys > girls.

**Clue → diagnosis:** **paroxysms of screaming with legs drawn up every 15–20 min, pallor during, striking lethargy between** — the well/unwell cycling with disproportionate listlessness is the giveaway. **Redcurrant jelly stool is late and in a minority — never wait for it.** ± **sausage-shaped RUQ mass**.

- Lead point ~5–10% (if >2 y or recurrent): **Meckel, polyp, HSP haematoma, lymphoma**; HSP is often **ileoileal**, missed by contrast enema.
- **US: "target"/doughnut sign, longitudinal pseudokidney.**
- Resuscitate, NBM, NG, antibiotics → **air enema reduction** (~75–90%), surgeons available. **Contraindicated if peritonitis, perforation or shock → theatre.** Recurrence ~10%.
- ⚑ Small excess risk 1–7 days after rotavirus **dose 1** — hence the strict age caps.

## 5.4 Appendicitis in children
Commonest surgical emergency after age 5; **under 5 it is uncommon but far more often perforated** (poor localisation, immature omentum, delayed diagnosis).

- Sequence: **anorexia → periumbilical colic → migration to RIF → vomiting**. **Vomiting *before* pain argues against it.**
- Signs: McBurney tenderness, guarding, **pain on hopping/coughing/percussion** (better than rebound in children), **psoas sign (retrocaecal)**.
- **Alvarado / Paediatric Appendicitis Score** stratify, they don't diagnose. **Ultrasound first-line**; a non-visualised appendix does not exclude. ⚑ Australian practice favours **serial review + US over early CT**.
- Traps: **mesenteric adenitis, UTI, DKA, right lower lobe pneumonia, testicular or ovarian torsion, HSP** — always examine the scrotum.
- Laparoscopic appendicectomy; perforated with abscess → drainage + antibiotics ± interval procedure.

## 5.5 Constipation and encopresis
>95% functional; peaks at weaning, toilet training, school entry.

**Red flags for organic cause:** **delayed meconium >48 h (Hirschsprung)**, onset <1 month, ribbon stools, **faltering growth**, bilious vomiting, abnormal anal position/tone, **sacral dimple, absent anal wink (spinal dysraphism)**, leg neurology. Otherwise no investigations — ⚑ **AXR is not indicated to diagnose constipation**. Also coeliac, hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, CMPA, fissure pain-avoidance, opioids/iron.

**Encopresis = overflow incontinence around an impaction** — parents report "diarrhoea" or soiling. It is **involuntary**; calling it behavioural is the classic trap.

1. **Disimpaction** — escalating oral **macrogol over 3–7 days**; warn of transient increased soiling.
2. **Maintenance** — macrogol **for months** (≥6, or as long as symptoms lasted), weaned slowly; stopping early is the commonest error.
3. **Behavioural** — post-meal toileting (gastrocolic reflex), footstool, demystification; diet/fluid are adjuncts only.

## 5.6 Coeliac disease
**HLA-DQ2/DQ8**-restricted gluten enteropathy; Australian prevalence ~1:70.

- **Clue → diagnosis:** **diarrhoea, distension, irritability, buttock wasting after weaning onto cereals**; or **short stature/delayed puberty/iron deficiency refractory to oral iron** with no GI symptoms.
- Associations: **T1DM, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down/Turner, IgA deficiency, dermatitis herpetiformis**.
- **tTG-IgA + total IgA**: **selective IgA deficiency (2–3%) gives a false-negative tTG-IgA** → use DGP/tTG-IgG. **Must still be eating gluten** — a self-started gluten-free diet is the commonest false negative.
- ⚑ **Biopsy-free paediatric diagnosis** (ESPGHAN): **tTG-IgA ≥10× ULN + EMA positive on a second sample**; otherwise duodenal biopsy. **HLA typing excludes but never confirms.**
- Lifelong gluten-free diet, dietitian, micronutrient and bone screening, screen first-degree relatives.

## 5.7 GORD and cow's milk protein allergy
Regurgitation is physiological in up to half of infants <3 months, peaks ~4 months, resolves in >90% by 12 months.

| | **GOR(D)** | **Non-IgE CMPA** | **IgE cow's milk allergy** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Weeks–4 months | Days–weeks after exposure (incl. breast milk) | Minutes–2 h |
| Features | Effortless posseting | **Eczema, blood/mucus in stool, colic, poor weight gain** | **Urticaria, angioedema, vomiting, wheeze, anaphylaxis** |
| Discriminator | **Happy spitter, growing normally** | **Blood-streaked stool in an unsettled but thriving infant** | Immediate, reproducible |
| Diagnosis | Clinical | **2–4 week elimination + planned re-challenge** (essential, often skipped) | **Skin prick / specific IgE** (ASCIA) |
| Management | Positioning, smaller feeds, thickening; ⚑ **acid suppression only for proven complicated GORD** — PPIs don't help infant crying and raise gastroenteritis/LRTI risk | Maternal dairy exclusion if breastfed; **extensively hydrolysed formula** (amino-acid if severe) | Avoidance, ASCIA action plan, autoinjector if indicated |

GORD *disease* = faltering growth, feed refusal, haematemesis, pain, aspiration. **Sandifer syndrome** (dystonic arching with reflux) is mistaken for seizures. **Do not** use soy formula <6 months (10–15% cross-reactivity) or lactose-free formula for CMPA — **lactose intolerance ≠ cow's milk protein allergy**.

## 5.8 Inguinal hernia, hydrocele and undescended testis
All arise from a patent **processus vaginalis**.

| | Inguinal hernia | Communicating hydrocele |
|---|---|---|
| Discriminator | **Cannot get above it**; may reduce | **Varies through the day, bigger by evening**; **transilluminates**; can get above it |
| Risk/course | **Incarceration risk highest in early infancy** | Benign |
| Management | **Repair — urgent in infants**; same admission if incarcerated | Repair if persisting **beyond 1–2 years** |

A **non-communicating hydrocele** transilluminates, stays the same size and **resolves by 12–24 months** — observe.

**Incarcerated hernia:** irritable infant, tender irreducible groin lump, vomiting → analgesia and gentle taxis with surgical input, repair same admission; irreducible or unwell → theatre.

**Undescended testis:** refer at **6 months corrected**; ⚑ **orchidopexy 6–12 months** (earlier than the old "before 2 years"); malignancy risk persists afterwards. **Bilateral impalpable testes in a newborn = urgent DSD/CAH assessment, check electrolytes.** A **retractile testis** (brisk cremaster, stays down once milked) is normal.

## 5.9 Testicular torsion and the acute scrotum
**Torsion is a clinical diagnosis and goes to theatre — do not delay for ultrasound.** Salvage ~90–100% <6 h, ~50% at 12 h, <10% beyond 24 h. **A normal Doppler does not exclude torsion** (intermittent/partial; high false-negative rate in children).

| | **Torsion** | **Torsion of appendix testis** | **Epididymo-orchitis** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age | Neonatal peak and **12–18 y** | **7–12 y** | **Bimodal: <2 y** (post-viral or urinary tract anomaly) **and post-pubertal** |
| Onset | **Sudden, severe, may wake from sleep**, vomiting | Gradual over days | Gradual, dysuria, fever |
| Exam | **High-riding, transverse lie, absent cremasteric reflex**, no relief on elevation | **Focal upper-pole tenderness, "blue dot"**, rest of testis fine | **Relief on elevation (Prehn)**, pyuria |
| Action | **Theatre — bilateral fixation** (bell-clapper is bilateral) | Analgesia, settles ~1 week | Antibiotics; **image the urinary tract in a prepubertal boy** |

Also: **HSP scrotal involvement**, **idiopathic scrotal oedema** (florid erythema, remarkably non-tender), **incarcerated hernia**, and **neonatal extravaginal torsion** (firm, discoloured, non-tender hemiscrotum — usually infarcted; review the other side urgently).

## 5.10 Abdominal pain — mesenteric adenitis, abdominal migraine, functional
~10% of school-age children, mostly functional — diagnose functional pain **positively**, after excluding red flags: waking from sleep, **pain far from the umbilicus** (Apley), weight loss, **PR bleeding**, persistent/bilious vomiting, fever, **joint/eye involvement, perianal disease**, FHx of IBD/coeliac, delayed puberty.

| Condition | Clue → diagnosis |
|---|---|
| **Mesenteric adenitis** | **Preceding URTI + cervical nodes + higher fever than appendicitis + tenderness that shifts on re-examination** |
| **Abdominal migraine** | **Stereotyped midline episodes ≥1 h with pallor, anorexia, nausea, photophobia; completely well between**; FHx migraine; responds to migraine prophylaxis |
| **Functional pain / IBS** | Periumbilical, no red flags, normal growth, school avoidance |
| **Constipation** | Commonest organic cause — always ask about stool |
| **Coeliac / IBD / UTI** | FBE, CRP, coeliac serology + total IgA, urine MCS, **faecal calprotectin** if IBD suspected |

Sudden severe pain still demands examination of the genitalia (ovarian/testicular torsion) and a pregnancy test in adolescents. Management of functional pain: explain the **gut-brain axis** ("real pain, no dangerous cause"), stop repeat investigation, treat constipation, return to school; **CBT/gut-directed hypnotherapy** have the best evidence.

| Clue / question | Answer |

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 6: Paediatric Infectious Disease and Immunisation
*Anchored to the Australian Immunisation Handbook and NIP schedule (verified August 2026 against health.gov.au and NSW Health — two independent sources, in agreement), RCH CPGs, NCIRS, Therapeutic Guidelines, ASHM. UK/NICE differences flagged.*

## 6.1 The childhood exanthems
Work **prodrome → morphology → distribution → progression**, not rash alone.

| Disease | Prodrome | Rash — the discriminator | Complications / infectivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Measles** | **3 Cs — cough, coryza, conjunctivitis**, high fever, miserable child | **Koplik spots** (white buccal grains 1–2 d *before* the rash, pathognomonic); rash **starts behind ears/hairline → descends, confluent**, then brownish desquamation | **Otitis media (commonest), pneumonia (commonest cause of death), encephalitis ~1:1000, SSPE later**; infectious 4 d before to 4 d after rash; **notify same day** |
| **Rubella** | Mild/absent; **child looks well** | Fine pink macules, face → trunk, gone in ~3 days | **Tender suboccipital/postauricular nodes**; **congenital rubella <16 weeks — cataracts, deafness, PDA** |
| **Parvovirus B19** | Mild coryza | **"Slapped cheek" with circumoral pallor**, then **lacy reticular limb rash recurring with heat** | **Not infectious once the rash appears**; **aplastic crisis in sickle cell/spherocytosis; hydrops fetalis; adult arthropathy** |
| **Roseola** (HHV-6/7) | **3–5 days of high fever, 6–24 months, otherwise well** | **Rash appears as the fever breaks** — pink trunk macules, spares face | Commonest cause of **febrile convulsion** |
| **Scarlet fever** (GAS toxin) | Fever, **sore throat**, vomiting | **Sandpaper texture**, flexural accentuation (**Pastia's lines**), **circumoral pallor**, **strawberry tongue**, late desquamation | **Phenoxymethylpenicillin 10 days to prevent acute rheumatic fever** — critical in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where ARF/RHD rates lead the world ⚑ |
| **Hand, foot and mouth** (Coxsackie A16, **EV71**) | Low fever, sore mouth | **Oval grey-white vesicles on palms, soles, buttocks + oral ulcers** | Main risk **dehydration from refusing to drink**; **EV71 → brainstem encephalitis, pulmonary oedema** |
| **Chickenpox** (VZV) | Fever, malaise | **Crops in different stages at once**, **centripetal (trunk > limbs)**, intensely itchy | Infectious **until all crusted**. **Secondary GAS/staph infection, cerebellar ataxia, adult pneumonia**; **avoid NSAIDs**; aciclovir if immunocompromised, neonate or adult |

**The short version**
- Koplik → measles; slapped cheek → parvovirus; fever-then-rash-and-well → roseola; sandpaper + strawberry tongue → scarlet fever.
- Crops at different stages → varicella; same-stage descending confluent rash → measles.
- Parvovirus is contagious *before* the rash; varicella until crusted; measles from 4 days before.
- Fever ≥4–5 days plus rash — consider **Kawasaki**.

## 6.2 Kawasaki disease
Medium-vessel vasculitis, peak **6 months–5 years**; **leading cause of acquired heart disease in children**. Untreated, **20–25% develop coronary aneurysms**; timely IVIG cuts this to **~3–5%**.

**Diagnostic criteria (RCH): fever ≥4 days ⚑ plus ≥4 of 5 principal features** (need not be simultaneous — take a retrospective history):
1. **Bilateral non-exudative conjunctival injection** (limbal sparing).
2. **Mucosal change** — cracked red lips, **strawberry tongue**, oropharyngeal erythema (no ulcers, no exudate).
3. **Polymorphous rash** — anything but vesicular/bullous; often groin-accentuated.
4. **Extremity change** — palmar/plantar erythema and oedema acutely; **periungual desquamation in week 2–3** (a late, retrospective clue).
5. **Cervical lymphadenopathy ≥1.5 cm**, usually unilateral — the **least common** feature.

⚑ **Sources disagree:** AHA and most UK teaching require **fever ≥5 days**; **RCH uses ≥4 days**. For an Australian paper quote ≥4 days and note the international ≥5-day convention.

**Incomplete/atypical KD** — fever with <4 features plus supportive labs; **commonest in infants <6 months, who carry the highest aneurysm risk**. Supportive: **raised CRP/ESR, anaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, raised ALT, sterile pyuria, thrombocytosis after week 1**, echo **coronary Z ≥2.5** (or ≥3 of reduced LV function, mitral regurgitation, pericardial effusion, Z 2–2.5).

**Discriminators: exudative tonsillitis, vesicles or generalised lymphadenopathy argue against KD.** Scarlet fever responds to penicillin; measles has cough/coryza and **Koplik spots**; toxic shock has hypotension; systemic JIA has quotidian fever and an evanescent salmon rash. **Irritability out of proportion** is characteristic.

**Management**
- **IVIG 2 g/kg single infusion over 8–12 h, within 10 days of fever onset** (ideally days 5–7); still give after day 10 if fever/inflammation persists.
- ⚑ **Aspirin: RCH specifies low-dose 5 mg/kg/day from the outset** (the 3–5 mg/kg band is the general antiplatelet range) to the 6-week review; Australian practice has largely dropped the **high-dose anti-inflammatory phase (30–50, or US 80–100 mg/kg/day)**, which does not reduce aneurysms. Know both; say which you quote.
- **Steroids upfront if high-risk**: **age ≤6 months**, shock, coronary change already present, marked inflammation, hypoalbuminaemia.
- **IVIG resistance** (fever ≥36 h post-infusion, 10–20%) → second IVIG, steroids, or **infliximab**.
- **Echo at diagnosis and 4–6 weeks** (earlier repeat if high-risk or baseline coronary abnormality); giant aneurysms (Z ≥10) need anticoagulation.
- **Defer live vaccines ~11 months after IVIG**; stop aspirin during influenza/varicella (Reye) and vaccinate against influenza.

## 6.3 Bacterial meningitis and encephalitis
**Organisms: <2 months — GBS, *E. coli*/gram-negatives, *Listeria*; >2 months — *S. pneumoniae*, *N. meningitidis*** (⚑ Hib now rare post-vaccination).

**Clue → diagnosis:** neck stiffness is unreliable under 18 months — instead **fever with lethargy, irritability, high-pitched cry, poor feeding, bulging fontanelle or seizure**. **Non-blanching purpuric rash + shock = meningococcaemia → antibiotics now, before LP and before transfer.**

- **Empirical (RCH):** neonate — **benzylpenicillin (or amoxicillin) + cefotaxime**; **≥2 months — ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg (max 4 g) IV**, plus **vancomycin** if gram-positive cocci on Gram stain, plus **aciclovir** if encephalitis is possible.
- **Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg (max 10 mg) IV 6-hourly × 4 days** in children **≥2 months**, with or just before the first antibiotic dose; if missed, still give **ideally within 4 h and up to 12 h** after the first dose — reduces hearing loss. **Not in neonates.**
- **Defer LP** for focal neurology, ongoing seizures, markedly reduced GCS, cardiovascular compromise or coagulopathy — but **never delay antibiotics**; if LP will take >30 min, treat first.
- **CSF:** bacterial — polymorphs, **low glucose (CSF:serum <0.4)**, high protein; viral — lymphocytes, normal glucose; TB — lymphocytes, **very low glucose, very high protein**.
- **Encephalitis** is distinguished by **altered conscious state, behaviour change or focal signs rather than pure meningism**. Suspect **HSV** in any encephalopathic child → **aciclovir 20 mg/kg IV 8-hourly** (10 mg/kg if >12 y); MRI shows temporal change; **do not stop aciclovir on one early negative PCR**. Also enterovirus, **anti-NMDAR**, **ADEM**.
- **Contacts:** meningococcal — **ciprofloxacin (or rifampicin)** for household/intimate contacts, plus notification. **Audiology within 4–8 weeks** for every survivor.

## 6.4 UTI in children — including when to image (Australian practice)
Fever without a source in an infant is UTI until proven otherwise (~5–7% of febrile under-2s). *E. coli* dominates; **Proteus** suggests stones, **Klebsiella/Pseudomonas** structural abnormality.

| Age | Collection |
|---|---|
| **<6 months / unwell infant** | **Suprapubic aspirate = gold standard**; catheter acceptable |
| 6 months–not toilet trained | In-out catheter or **clean catch** |
| Toilet trained | **Midstream urine** |
| Any | **Bag urine — useful only if negative**; a positive bag never diagnoses UTI |

**Dipstick:** nitrites specific but insensitive; leucocyte esterase sensitive, non-specific; **unreliable <3 months — always send microscopy and culture**. Sterile pyuria → Kawasaki, partially treated UTI, TB, adjacent appendicitis.

**Treatment (RCH/TG):** cystitis — **oral cefalexin 3–5 days**; pyelonephritis — **oral cefalexin 7–10 days**; **IV (gentamicin ± benzylpenicillin) for infants <3 months, septic children, or oral intolerance**, then oral switch.

⚑ **Imaging — Australian practice is deliberately more conservative than NICE.** RCH: **ultrasound** for
- **atypical UTI** — septic/seriously unwell, **no response by 48 h**, **non-*E. coli*** organism, raised creatinine, poor urine flow, abdominal/bladder mass;
- **infants <6 months** with recurrent UTI or no normal antenatal renal ultrasound;
- **recurrent UTI** at any age.

**MCUG** only for suspected **posterior urethral valves** (boy, poor stream, palpable bladder), abnormal US, or recurrent atypical infection — **not routine after a first febrile UTI**. **DMSA** for scarring, usually delayed **4–6 months**. NICE by contrast prescribes an age-banded US/DMSA/MCUG algorithm — **quote RCH in an Australian question**. Prophylaxis is not routine; always treat **constipation and dysfunctional voiding**, the commonest reversible cause of recurrence.

## 6.5 Osteomyelitis and septic arthritis — Kocher criteria
**Clue → diagnosis:** a febrile child who **will not weight-bear or move a limb** ("pseudoparalysis" in infants). Septic arthritis holds the hip **flexed, abducted, externally rotated** and resists passive movement, especially internal rotation.

**Organisms:** ***S. aureus*** at all ages (⚑ cover MRSA where community prevalence is high, e.g. remote Aboriginal communities); ***Kingella kingae*** at **6 months–4 years** (afebrile, indolent, culture-negative — needs PCR); GBS/*E. coli* in neonates; **GAS post-varicella**.

**Kocher criteria** (septic arthritis vs transient synovitis of the hip), one point each: **non-weight-bearing; temperature >38.5 °C; ESR >40 mm/hr; WBC >12 ×10⁹/L**.

| Predictors | Kocher 1999 | Prospective validation 2004 |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.2% | 2.0% |
| 1 | 3% | 9.5% |
| 2 | 40% | 35.0% |
| 3 | 93% | 72.8% |
| 4 | 99% | 93.0% |

⚑ **Sources disagree** on the 2- and 3-predictor figures (Luhmann's series lower again). Quote the **original 0.2 / 3 / 40 / 93 / 99%**, noting that validation gave lower PPVs — the score **supplements clinical judgement**. **CRP >20 mg/L** is the strongest single lab predictor in later work.

**Transient synovitis** — the main mimic: **afebrile/low fever, recent viral URTI, still weight-bears (limp not refusal), near-normal inflammatory markers**, settles in days on NSAIDs. If in doubt, **aspirate**.

**Investigations:** blood culture (~40% positive), FBE, CRP/ESR, **hip US for effusion** (US cannot distinguish septic from sterile — **aspiration does**); ⚑ **X-ray is normal for the first 7–10 days of osteomyelitis**, so **MRI is the investigation of choice**.

**Management:** **washout** for septic arthritis, empirical **IV flucloxacillin** (vancomycin/clindamycin if MRSA risk), early oral switch by response and CRP, ~3–4 weeks total for osteomyelitis. Also consider **SUFE, Perthes, leukaemia (night pain, cytopenias), non-accidental injury**.

## 6.6 Periorbital vs orbital cellulitis
| | **Periorbital (preseptal)** | **Orbital (postseptal)** |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Skin breach, bite | **Ethmoid sinusitis** |
| Eye movements | **Normal, painless** | **Painful, restricted — ophthalmoplegia** |
| Proptosis, acuity/colour vision | Absent, normal | **Proptosis; reduced acuity and colour vision, RAPD** |
| Imaging | Not required | **CT orbits + sinuses with contrast** |
| Management | Oral antibiotics if mild, review 24 h | **Admit, IV cefotaxime 50 mg/kg (max 2 g) 6-hourly or ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg (max 4 g) daily** — ⚑ **add vancomycin only if MRSA is suspected**, flucloxacillin is not part of the Australian regimen — **urgent ENT/ophthalmology**, drain subperiosteal abscess |

**Discriminator: pain on eye movement, proptosis or reduced colour vision converts periorbital into orbital cellulitis.** Complications: **subperiosteal abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, intracranial extension, blindness.**

## 6.8 True vs false contraindications to vaccination
**True — all vaccines: anaphylaxis to a previous dose or to a vaccine component.**

**Additional for live vaccines** (MMR, MMRV, varicella, rotavirus, BCG, yellow fever):
- **Significant immunocompromise** — disease (SCID, active leukaemia) or treatment (**prednisolone ≥2 mg/kg/day or ≥20 mg/day for ≥14 days**, chemotherapy, biologics). Mildly immunocompromised children, including many with HIV, **can** have MMR and varicella with specialist advice.
- **Pregnancy** (avoid conception for 28 days after).
- **Rotavirus:** previous **intussusception**, uncorrected congenital GI malformation, and the **strict maximum ages**.
- **Timing:** two live parenteral vaccines **same day or ≥4 weeks apart**; defer **~11 months after IVIG/blood products**.

**False contraindications — vaccinate anyway:**
- **Mild illness without fever (<38.5 °C)**, current antibiotics, contact with an infectious disease.
- **Family history of adverse events or seizures**; **personal history of convulsions, including febrile convulsions**.
- **Asthma, eczema, atopy, allergic rhinitis; inhaled/topical or replacement steroids.**
- **Prematurity — vaccinate by chronological, not corrected, age** once medically stable (very preterm infants may need respiratory monitoring for dose 1).
- **Breastfeeding**, neonatal jaundice, stable neurology (cerebral palsy, Down).
- ⚑ **Egg allergy is not a contraindication to MMR or influenza vaccine** (ASCIA), even after egg anaphylaxis; yellow fever remains specialist.
- Unknown history — assume unvaccinated and use a catch-up schedule.

Distinguish a **contraindication** from a **precaution** (moderate–severe febrile illness — defer briefly, don't cancel).

## 6.9 Needle-stick and post-exposure issues in brief
Community needle-stick (playground syringe) generates far more anxiety than risk — HIV transmission from a discarded needle is essentially unrecorded in Australia.

- **Immediate:** wash with soap and water (**don't squeeze or scrub**), irrigate eyes/mucosa with saline.
- **Risk, source-positive percutaneous: HBV up to ~30% > HCV ~3% > HIV ~0.3%** — the ordering is the examinable fact.
- **HBV:** fully NIP-vaccinated → reassurance; unvaccinated → **HBIG within 72 h + start vaccination**. **HCV:** no vaccine, **no PEP** — baseline and follow-up RNA/serology, curable with direct-acting antivirals.
- **HIV** (ASHM): an unknown, usually old, community source is **generally not an indication**; if indicated, start **within 72 h (ideally <2 h)** for 28 days.
- Tetanus per the Handbook; baseline serology repeated at **3 and 6 months**.

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 7: Paediatric Neurology and Neurodevelopment
*Australian sources: RCH Melbourne CPGs (febrile seizure, acute seizure management, infantile spasms), QPEC Queensland guidelines, Therapeutic Guidelines, AADPA ADHD guideline. Scope: written-finals recognition and first-line management; doses given because mg/kg figures are the highest-yield MCQ target.*

## 7.1 Febrile convulsions

Seizure with fever ≥38°C, age **6 months to 6 years**, **no CNS infection, no metabolic cause, no prior afebrile seizure**. Age outside that window is itself the discriminator: a seizure with fever at 4 months is meningitis until excluded; at 8 years it is epilepsy with fever as trigger.

**The short version**
- Simple febrile convulsion needs **no investigation beyond what the fever itself warrants** — no LP, no EEG, no imaging, no bloods.
- Complex = **focal, or >15 min, or recurs within the same illness** — any one suffices.
- Younger at first fit = higher recurrence; peak temperature does not predict it.
- **Antipyretics do not prevent recurrence** — a favourite short-answer point.

| Feature | Simple | Complex |
|---|---|---|
| Type | **Generalised** tonic-clonic | **Focal** onset/features (Todd's palsy) |
| Duration | **<15 min** | **>15 min** |
| Same illness | Single | **Recurs within the same febrile illness** (not a 24-hour rule — a second seizure on day 3 of the same illness is complex) |
| Recovery | Full within 1 h | Altered state >1 h |
| Investigation | For fever source only | Glucose, electrolytes, ECG; imaging after senior review |
| Later epilepsy | ~1–2% (near background) | 4–6% |

**What does NOT need investigation.** Classic stem: 18-month-old, 3 min generalised fit, febrile with otitis media, now alert and playing. Answer: **treat the otitis and reassure** — not LP, EEG, CT or electrolytes.

**When to LP:** age <12 months with a low threshold, meningism, petechial rash, drowsiness beyond an hour, complex features, or antibiotic pre-treatment (partially treated meningitis masks signs). ⚑ Older texts advised routine LP under 12–18 months; current Australian practice is **clinical judgement, not age alone**.

**Recurrence** ≈ 50% if the first fit is at age 1, ~30% at age 2. **Parent advice:** lie on side, nothing in mouth, time it, ambulance if >5 min or a second fit. No prophylactic anticonvulsant; rescue midazolam only if prior prolonged seizures.

## 7.2 Epilepsy syndromes of childhood — clue → syndrome

**The short version**
- **Blank stare, 10 s, no post-ictal state, reproduced by hyperventilation** → childhood absence.
- **Nocturnal hemifacial twitching, drooling, speech arrest, aware** → benign rolandic.
- **Flexor spasm clusters on waking at 3–7 months (but can occur at any age in childhood) + regression** → infantile spasms; urgent EEG.
- **Morning jerks spilling the cereal, tonic-clonic after sleep deprivation** → JME.

| Syndrome | Age | Semiology | EEG | Treatment | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Childhood absence | 4–10 y | 5–15 s staring, dozens daily, **abrupt on/off, no post-ictal confusion**, provoked by **hyperventilation** | **3 Hz spike-and-wave** | Ethosuximide or valproate | Remits by adolescence ~70% |
| Self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes ("rolandic") | 3–13 y | **Nocturnal/on waking** facial twitch, drooling, **speech arrest with retained awareness** | **Centrotemporal spikes, sleep-activated** | Often **none** | Resolves by mid-teens |
| Infantile epileptic spasms (West) | **3–7 months** | **Clusters** of neck/trunk/arm flexion on waking; **developmental arrest** | **Hypsarrhythmia** | **High-dose prednisolone**; **vigabatrin if tuberous sclerosis** ⚑ | Poor: >80% delay |
| Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy | 12–18 y | **Early-morning myoclonus**, GTCS after **sleep deprivation/alcohol**, photosensitive | **4–6 Hz polyspike-and-wave** | Valproate best but **avoid in females of childbearing potential** ⚑ — levetiracetam | **Lifelong** |

**Traps.** Absence vs focal seizure: absence has **no aura and no post-ictal confusion**, and is brief and frequent. Absence vs daydreaming: daydreaming stops when you touch the child. **Carbamazepine and phenytoin worsen absence and myoclonic seizures** — the standard "contraindicated drug" stem. Infantile spasms are usually mislabelled "colic" or "reflux", in a baby who has stopped smiling or rolling.

## 7.3 Status epilepticus — management by weight

**≥5 minutes** of continuous seizure, or recurrent seizures without recovery between (⚑ 5 minutes is the operational definition; 30 minutes is the older figure and refers to neuronal injury).

**The short version**
- Airway, oxygen, **check BGL immediately** — hypoglycaemia is reversible and examined.
- Benzodiazepine → wait 5 min → second benzodiazepine → **second-line agent**. Never a third benzo.

| Step | Drug | Dose (RCH) |
|---|---|---|
| 1st, IV access | **Midazolam** | 0.15 mg/kg IV/IM/IO, **max 10 mg** |
| 1st, no access | **Midazolam** buccal/intranasal | **0.3 mg/kg**, max 10 mg |
| Alternative | Diazepam | 0.3 mg/kg IV/IO, max 10 mg (**never IM**) |
| Repeat | Second benzodiazepine | after **5 min** |
| 2nd line | **Levetiracetam** | 40–60 mg/kg IV, max 4.5 g, over 5 min |
| 2nd line | **Phenytoin** | 20 mg/kg IV, max 2 g, at **max 1 mg/kg/min**, cardiac monitoring |
| 2nd line | Phenobarbitone | 20 mg/kg IV, max 1 g, over ≥30 min |
| Adjunct | **Pyridoxine 100 mg IV** | refractory seizures in infants **<6 months** |

Reversible causes: hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, sepsis/meningitis, toxins, non-accidental head injury, raised ICP.

## 7.4 Cerebral palsy

Non-progressive disorder of movement and posture from injury to the **developing** brain. The lesion is static though the clinical picture evolves (contractures, scoliosis). **Loss of previously acquired skills means it is not CP** — think neurodegenerative, metabolic or mitochondrial disease, or tumour.

| Type | % | Lesion | Clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spastic** | ~80 | Periventricular white matter (**PVL — prematurity**) | Velocity-dependent tone, clonus, scissoring, toe-walking; **spastic diplegia = classic ex-prem** |
| **Dyskinetic** | ~10 | Basal ganglia — **kernicterus, severe term asphyxia** | Fluctuating tone, writhing worse with intent; **intellect often preserved** |
| **Ataxic** | ~5 | Cerebellum, often genetic | Wide-based gait, intention tremor |

Most CP is **antenatal** (~80%): malformation, congenital infection, stroke, placental insufficiency, genetic. Intrapartum hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy accounts for only ~10% — resist the stem offering "difficult delivery" as the automatic cause. Prematurity is the biggest single risk factor.

**Early red flags:** persistent fisting past 3 months, **hand preference before 12 months** (asymmetry = hemiplegia until proven otherwise), retained primitive reflexes, "rolling too early" from spasticity.

**Associated problems:** epilepsy, intellectual disability, **dysphagia with aspiration and reflux**, failure to thrive, **hip subluxation (surveillance radiographs)**, scoliosis, constipation, drooling, strabismus, hearing and communication impairment, pain, sleep disorder. Function graded **GMFCS I–V**; intellect is normal in a substantial minority.

## 7.5 Headache in children — red flags for imaging

Most childhood headache is migraine or tension-type. Paediatric migraine is more often bilateral/frontal and shorter (1–2 h), with prominent nausea, pallor and a wish to sleep; abdominal migraine and cyclical vomiting are the childhood equivalents.

**The short version**
- **Wakes the child, worse lying flat or coughing, morning vomiting** → raised ICP → image.
- Papilloedema, focal signs, ataxia, new squint (**VI palsy is a false localising sign**) → image.
- Behaviour/school decline, or head circumference crossing centiles → image.
- Episodic pattern + normal exam, growth and fundi = **no imaging**.

Other red flags: occipital site, progressive headache, no family history of migraine, seizure, growth failure or endocrine features (craniopharyngioma), age **<3 years**, neurocutaneous stigmata, immunosuppression, VP shunt, thunderclap onset. Posterior fossa tumours (medulloblastoma, pilocytic astrocytoma, ependymoma) give **morning headache and vomiting, ataxia, head tilt, nystagmus**, often with obstructive hydrocephalus.

## 7.6 Hydrocephalus and raised ICP

The open fontanelle changes the picture: infants decompensate late but catastrophically. **Infant:** head circumference **crossing centiles**, tense fontanelle, splayed sutures, distended scalp veins, **"sunsetting" eyes**, irritability, high-pitched cry. **Older child:** morning headache worse lying flat, vomiting, papilloedema, VI palsy, declining GCS; **Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiration) is late and pre-terminal**.

**Obstructive (non-communicating)** = block within the ventricles — aqueduct stenosis, posterior fossa tumour, Chiari II with myelomeningocele. **Communicating** = impaired absorption at the arachnoid granulations — **post-haemorrhagic (IVH of prematurity)**, post-meningitis.

Imaging: **cranial ultrasound through the fontanelle** while open; MRI otherwise. Treat the cause, then CSF diversion by VP shunt or endoscopic third ventriculostomy. **A shunted child with headache has shunt failure until proven otherwise**; shunt infection is usually coagulase-negative staphylococcus within months of insertion.

## 7.7 Neuromuscular disease — Duchenne muscular dystrophy

X-linked dystrophin deletion, ~**1 in 5,000** male births (older texts say 1 in 3,500); a third are new mutations, so **a negative family history does not exclude it**.

**The short version**
- Boy 3–5 y, **waddling gait, toe-walking, stairs difficulty, calf pseudohypertrophy** → **CK first**.
- **CK is 10–100× normal**; a normal CK effectively excludes DMD.
- **Gowers sign** = walking the hands up the thighs to stand — proximal weakness.
- Confirm by **genetic testing**; biopsy only if genetics non-diagnostic ⚑ (biopsy-first is outdated).

Course: ambulation lost around 10–13 years, progressive scoliosis, **restrictive lung disease** and **dilated cardiomyopathy** as causes of death. **Corticosteroids prolong ambulation and delay scoliosis**; add cardiac surveillance with early ACE inhibitor, non-invasive ventilation, bone health, physiotherapy.

**Discriminators.** *Becker* — same gene, in-frame, later and slower. *Spinal muscular atrophy* — **hypotonia with tongue fasciculations and areflexia, alert face, bell-shaped chest**, CK normal, SMN1 testing (⚑ newborn screening and disease-modifying therapy have transformed prognosis). *Congenital myotonic dystrophy* — transmitted by an affected **mother**, facial diplegia, talipes, polyhydramnios; grip myotonia in the parent gives it away. A boy with delayed walking and "abnormal LFTs" has DMD — the transaminases are from muscle.

## 7.8 Autism spectrum disorder and ADHD — recognition

**ASD.** Deficits in **social communication and interaction** *plus* **restricted, repetitive behaviours/interests** (including sensory hyper- or hypo-reactivity), from early development, causing impairment. Early clues: **no babble or gesture by 12 months, no words by 16 months, no two-word phrases by 24 months, loss of skills at any age, absent joint attention/pointing to share, no response to name with normal hearing**. **Exclude hearing impairment — audiology is the reflex first investigation.** Diagnosis is multidisciplinary and behavioural; no blood test. Screen for treatable comorbidity: epilepsy, sleep disturbance, constipation, anxiety, ADHD. Management is early developmental/behavioural intervention, communication and family support, NDIS access; medication targets comorbidity only, never core features.

**ADHD.** **≥6 symptoms** of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, onset **before age 12**, in **≥2 settings** (home *and* school — the cross-setting rule is the exam discriminator), **≥6 months**, impairing. School collateral is mandatory. Mimics: hearing loss, **absence epilepsy** (the "daydreamer"), obstructive sleep apnoea, anxiety, learning disorder, trauma, iron deficiency, normal preschool activity. ⚑ Australian guidance (AADPA 2022) spans the lifespan and emphasises parent training and classroom supports alongside stimulants, which remain first-line pharmacotherapy for moderate–severe impairment.

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 8: Paediatric Haematology, Oncology and the Bruised Child
*Australian sources: RCH Melbourne CPGs (fever and neutropenia, anaemia, child abuse), Victorian Forensic Paediatric Medical Service bruising guideline, Children's Health Queensland ITP guideline, Therapeutic Guidelines. Scope: recognition, first investigation, and the abuse-versus-disease discriminators.*

## 8.1 Anaemia of childhood

**The short version**
- **Microcytic, milk-fed toddler, pale and irritable** → iron deficiency.
- **Microcytic with normal/high ferritin and an MCV disproportionately low for the Hb** → thalassaemia trait.
- **Age-specific Hb lower limits (RCH, g/L):** 2 mo ≥90 · 2–6 mo ≥95 · 6–24 mo ≥105 · 2–11 y ≥115 · ≥12 y ≥120 (F) / ≥130 (M). Physiological nadir at **6–8 weeks**. **Ferritin <20 µg/L** = low/borderline stores.
- **Iron studies before haemoglobin electrophoresis, always** — iron deficiency suppresses HbA₂ and gives a false-negative for β-thalassaemia trait.

**Iron deficiency in the milk-fed toddler** is the archetypal stem: 18-month-old on 1–1.5 L cow's milk daily, refusing solids, pale and tired, sometimes with pica. Cow's milk is iron-poor, causes occult gut blood loss and displaces iron-rich solids. Bloods: **low MCV and MCH, high RDW, low ferritin, low transferrin saturation, raised TIBC**, often reactive thrombocytosis. Management: **limit cow's milk to ~500 mL/day**, iron-rich solids with vitamin C, no bottle in bed, plus elemental iron **3–6 mg/kg/day**; expect reticulocytosis within a week and Hb rise by 2–4 weeks, continuing ~3 months beyond normalisation to refill stores. Non-response = non-adherence, ongoing loss, wrong diagnosis, or coeliac disease.

| | Iron deficiency | β-thalassaemia trait | Anaemia of inflammation |
|---|---|---|---|
| RBC count | **Low** | **Normal or high** (key splitter) | Low |
| RDW | **High** | Normal | Normal |
| Ferritin | **Low** | Normal/high | **Normal or high** (acute phase) |
| TIBC/transferrin | High | Normal | **Low** |
| Confirm | Response to iron | **HbA₂ >3.5%** once iron-replete | Treat underlying disease |

**Mentzer index** (MCV ÷ RBC): **<13 favours thalassaemia trait, >13 iron deficiency** — a hook, not a substitute for iron studies. **β-thalassaemia major** declares itself at **3–6 months** as HbF falls: severe anaemia, failure to thrive, hepatosplenomegaly, later frontal bossing. Lifelong transfusion plus chelation; death is from **iron-overload cardiomyopathy**, not anaemia.

## 8.2 Haemolytic anaemias

**Reticulocytosis + unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia + high LDH + low haptoglobin** = haemolysis. Then ask: membrane, enzyme, haemoglobin or immune?

| Condition | Genetics | Trigger / clue | Film | Test | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hereditary spherocytosis** | Autosomal dominant | **Family history, neonatal jaundice, splenomegaly, pigment gallstones**; aplastic crisis with **parvovirus B19** | **Spherocytes**, high MCHC | **EMA binding flow cytometry** ⚑ (replaced osmotic fragility) | Folate; splenectomy if severe |
| **G6PD deficiency** | **X-linked** — boys | **Episodic**: fava beans, infection, naphthalene, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, primaquine, dapsone | **Bite cells, blister cells, Heinz bodies** | Enzyme assay — **falsely normal acutely**; **repeat at 3 months** | Avoid triggers |
| **Autoimmune haemolysis** | Acquired | Post-viral; lupus | Spherocytes ± agglutination | **DAT/Coombs positive** | Steroids ± IVIG |

**Spherocytes occur in both HS and warm AIHA — the DAT separates them**: positive = immune, negative = hereditary spherocytosis. Highest-yield discrimination here.

## 8.3 Bleeding disorders

**The short version**
- **Mucocutaneous** bleeding (petechiae, epistaxis, menorrhagia, bruising) = **platelet or von Willebrand** problem.
- **Deep** bleeding (haemarthrosis, muscle haematoma, delayed post-surgical bleed) = **factor** problem.
- Petechiae **below the nipple line, or in a dependent/pressure distribution, are never ordinary trauma** — they mean thrombocytopenia or vasculitis. ⚑ Mechanical petechiae **above the nipple line** after forceful coughing, vomiting or crying, in a well child with normal platelets, are benign — a standard distractor.

| | ITP | Haemophilia A/B | von Willebrand disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genetics | Acquired, immune | **X-linked recessive** (boys) | **Autosomal dominant**; affects both sexes |
| Bleeding | Petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, wet purpura | **Haemarthrosis**, muscle and intracranial bleeds, post-circumcision | **Menorrhagia in the adolescent girl**, epistaxis, post-dental |
| Platelets | **Low, often <20**; otherwise normal film | Normal | Normal (low in type 2B) |
| APTT / PT | Normal / normal | **Prolonged** / normal | Normal or mildly prolonged / normal |
| Test | Diagnosis of exclusion | **Factor VIII assay (A) or factor IX assay (B)** — **A = VIII, B = IX** | **vWF antigen + activity (ristocetin cofactor) + FVIII** |
| Treatment | **Observation**; IVIG or prednisolone if bleeding | Factor concentrate; **emicizumab prophylaxis** ⚑ (A) | **Desmopressin** (not 2B), tranexamic acid, vWF concentrate |

**ITP:** typically 2–6 years, **1–3 weeks post-viral**, petechiae and bruising in an **otherwise well child with no hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy**, isolated thrombocytopenia. **Treat bleeding, not the number** — ~**80% have resolved by 12 months**, with ~20% still thrombocytopenic at 12 months (ANZCHOG/CHQ); intracranial haemorrhage <1%; no contact sport. **Marrow examination is not required** in the classic picture but **is required before steroids** if anything is atypical, so leukaemia is not partially treated and obscured. **The stem that is ALL, not ITP:** pallor, fever, bone pain, organomegaly, lymphadenopathy, or any other cell line involved.

## 8.4 The bruised child — bleeding disorder versus non-accidental injury

Answer on four axes: **site, pattern, developmental plausibility, and the history**.

**The short version**
- **"Those who don't cruise rarely bruise"** — bruising in a pre-mobile infant is abnormal whatever the explanation.
- **TEN-4-FACESp**: **T**orso, **E**ars, **N**eck in a child **<4 years**; **any** bruise in an infant **≤4 months**; or **F**renulum, **A**ngle of jaw, **C**heek, **E**yelid, **S**ubconjunctiva, or **p**atterned bruising → high concern.
- Accidental bruises sit **on the front, over bony prominences, in mobile children**.
- Still exclude a bleeding disorder — **FBE and film, PT/INR, APTT, fibrinogen** — the two are not mutually exclusive.

| Axis | Non-accidental injury | Bleeding disorder | Accident |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Site** | Torso, ears, neck, buttocks, genitals, cheeks, frenulum, subconjunctivae — **soft protected sites** | Anywhere, plus **mucosal bleeding** | Shins, knees, forehead, elbows |
| **Pattern** | **Imprinted shape** (hand, belt, cord, tram-track, bite, cigarette), clusters, **bruises of different ages** | Uniform ecchymoses ± **petechiae**, epistaxis, menorrhagia | Isolated, unpatterned |
| **Developmental plausibility** | Injury the child **cannot yet produce** — "rolled off the couch" at 6 weeks | Bruising **out of proportion** to real, trivial trauma | Mechanism matches the milestone |
| **History** | **Delayed presentation**, changing or inconsistent story, no explanation, child's disclosure | Consistent story, **family history**, prior bleeding with circumcision/dental work | Prompt and consistent |
| **Other** | **Posterior rib or metaphyseal corner fracture, any fracture in a non-ambulant infant**, retinal haemorrhages, burns, neglect | Isolated haematological abnormality, normal skeletal survey | Normal everything |

**Mimics:** HSP (palpable purpura, normal platelets), meningococcaemia (unwell, febrile, spreading petechiae — treat, don't investigate), leukaemia, coining/cupping, congenital dermal melanocytosis (bluish, sacral, from birth, does not evolve), osteogenesis imperfecta if fractures dominate.

**Actions:** don't interrogate the carer; document verbatim, photograph, examine fully including mouth (torn frenulum), fundi and genitalia; bleeding screen; **skeletal survey <2 years, neuroimaging in infants with suspicious bruising**; **mandatory report** — doctors are mandated reporters in every jurisdiction, but ⚑ **not for every category of harm**: WA mandates **sexual abuse only**; Vic, ACT and Qld mandate **physical and sexual**; only NSW, NT, SA and Tas cover all four. Reporting needs reasonable suspicion, not proof.

## 8.5 Leukaemia and the paediatric solid tumours

**ALL** is ~80% of childhood leukaemia, peaks at 2–5 years, and presents with marrow failure plus infiltration: **pallor and lethargy, bruising, fever, bone or joint pain with limp or refusal to weight-bear, hepatosplenomegaly, painless lymphadenopathy**, mediastinal mass in T-cell disease. Red flags: persistent unexplained bone pain, **bruising with any other cytopenia**, pallor with organomegaly, nodes **>2 cm, firm, fixed, supraclavicular, or persisting beyond 4–6 weeks**. First test **FBE and film**, then urgent oncology referral. Trisomy 21 raises risk substantially.

**Tumour lysis syndrome** — the complication to anticipate at induction, and highest-risk in a high-count ALL or a bulky T-cell mediastinal mass. Biochemistry: **↑K⁺, ↑phosphate, ↑urate, ↓calcium** (the low calcium is secondary to the high phosphate), with **AKI**. Prevention and treatment: **aggressive hydration without potassium**, **allopurinol** for standard risk or **rasburicase** for high risk or established TLS (contraindicated in G6PD deficiency), treat hyperkalaemia, and **do not give calcium for asymptomatic hypocalcaemia** — it drives calcium-phosphate deposition. Renal replacement if refractory.

| Tumour | Age | Presentation | Discriminator |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wilms** | 1–5 y | **Painless smooth flank mass not crossing the midline** in a **well** child; haematuria, hypertension | WAGR, Beckwith-Wiedemann, hemihypertrophy; **avoid repeated palpation** |
| **Neuroblastoma** | <2 y | **Irregular mass crossing the midline** in an **unwell** child; bone pain, **periorbital bruising and proptosis** | **Raised urinary VMA/HVA**; calcification; **MYCN amplification = poor prognosis** |
| **Retinoblastoma** | <5 y | **Leukocoria — absent red reflex**, squint | Bilateral = germline **RB1**, heritable |
| **Osteosarcoma / Ewing** | Adolescence | Night bone pain, mass | Osteosarcoma metaphyseal at knee, **sunburst + Codman triangle**; Ewing diaphyseal, **onion-skin**, mimics osteomyelitis |

**Wilms vs neuroblastoma** is near-guaranteed: **crosses the midline + unwell + catecholamines → neuroblastoma; does not cross + well + haematuria → Wilms**.

## 8.6 Febrile neutropenia

**The short version**
- Fever **≥38.5°C once, or ≥38.0°C sustained over an hour**, plus neutrophils **<0.5 ×10⁹/L** (or <1.0 and falling).
- **Antibiotics within 60 minutes — within 30 minutes if septic.** Do not wait for the FBE if the picture fits.
- Cultures from **every line lumen and peripherally**, but never let culture-taking delay antibiotics.
- **No PR examination, no rectal temperatures, no suppositories** — translocation risk.

First-line Australian empiric therapy: **piperacillin–tazobactam 100 mg/kg (max 4 g) IV 6-hourly**. Add an aminoglycoside if septic, on a high-risk protocol, or inpatient-onset — **RCH specifies amikacin 22.5 mg/kg (18 mg/kg if >10 y, max 1.5 g) IV daily**, though gentamicin is the commoner choice nationally. Add **vancomycin** only for suspected resistant Gram-positive infection (line-site cellulitis, proven Gram-positive bacteraemia, MRSA colonisation with skin breaks) — **persistent fever alone in a stable child is not an indication** (classic distractor). Consider antifungal cover beyond ~4–5 days. Live vaccines are contraindicated during therapy.

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 9: Paediatric Renal, Urology and Endocrine
*Australian sources: RCH Melbourne CPGs (UTI, enuresis, DKA, hypoglycaemia, HSP), Children's Health Queensland QPEC DKA/HHS guideline (July 2024), SA Health paediatric DKA guideline, KDIGO 2025 paediatric nephrotic syndrome guideline, APEG. Scope: thresholds, first-line management, and the paediatric-versus-adult differences that are deliberately examined.*

## 9.1 UTI and vesicoureteric reflux

**The short version**
- **Any febrile infant without a source needs a urine sample** — UTI is the commonest occult bacterial infection.
- **Bag urine is only useful when negative**; a positive bag must be confirmed by a clean method.
- **SPA is gold standard under 6 months**; catheter or clean catch/quick-wee otherwise; MSU once toilet-trained.
- Ultrasound is **not** required after a first uncomplicated UTI in an older child who responds promptly.

Diagnosis: any growth on **SPA**, or a single organism **>10⁸ CFU/L**; **10⁶–10⁸ CFU/L** from catheter/clean catch may be early infection. Nitrites are specific but insensitive in infants. *E. coli* dominates.

Treatment (RCH): **admit for IV therapy** (empiric **gentamicin**) if septic, **age <3 months**, or <6 months with structural abnormality. Otherwise oral **cefalexin**: **cystitis 3–5 days**, **pyelonephritis 7–10 days**. ⚑ Australian first-line oral therapy is cefalexin or trimethoprim, not the UK's co-amoxiclav-heavy regimens.

Imaging: **ultrasound** for atypical UTI (non-*E. coli*, poor response by 48 h, sepsis, poor urine flow, abdominal mass, raised creatinine), infants **<6 months** with recurrent UTI, and those without antenatal imaging. **MCUG** (grades reflux) only for abnormal ultrasound, recurrent febrile UTI or family history. **DMSA** detects scarring ≥4–6 months later.

**VUR** is graded I–V; most low-grade reflux resolves with growth. ⚑ **Routine antibiotic prophylaxis is no longer recommended** — reserve it for high-grade reflux, recurrent febrile UTI or significant voiding dysfunction. **Treat constipation and bladder-bowel dysfunction**, often the real driver. Surgery for high-grade reflux with breakthrough infection or progressive scarring.

## 9.2 Nephrotic versus nephritic syndrome

**The short version**
- **Oedema + heavy proteinuria + hypoalbuminaemia, normal BP, no haematuria, age 2–6** → minimal change → **steroids without biopsy**.
- **Cola urine + hypertension + oliguria 1–2 weeks after a sore throat** → post-streptococcal GN.
- **Post-strep GN: C3 is low and normalises by 8 weeks. Still low at 8 weeks means it is not post-strep** — think C3 glomerulopathy, MPGN or lupus.

| | **Nephrotic** (minimal change) | **Nephritic** (post-strep GN) |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 2–6 y, boys > girls | 5–12 y |
| Presentation | **Periorbital oedema on waking**, scrotal/leg oedema, ascites, frothy urine | **Macroscopic haematuria (cola urine)**, puffiness, headache |
| Proteinuria | **Massive** (uPCR >200 mg/mmol) | Mild–moderate |
| Haematuria | Usually absent | **Present with red cell casts** |
| Blood pressure | Normal | **Raised** |
| Albumin | **<30 g/L** (KDIGO 2025; <25 g/L is the older ISKDC figure) | Normal or mildly low |
| Complement | **Normal C3** | **Low C3, normal by 8 weeks**; C4 normal |
| Latency | — | **1–2 weeks post-pharyngitis; 3–6 weeks post-impetigo** |
| Serology | — | **ASOT** (throat) / **anti-DNase B** (skin — relevant to APSGN in remote Australian communities) |
| Treatment | **Prednisolone 60 mg/m²/day (max 60 mg) × 4 weeks, then 40 mg/m² alternate days × 4 weeks**; salt restriction | **Supportive**: salt/fluid restriction, diuretics, antihypertensives; penicillin does not alter renal outcome |
| Prognosis | ~90% steroid-responsive; most relapse | >95% full recovery |

**Steroid resistance** = no remission after **4 weeks** of daily prednisolone → biopsy and nephrology (FSGS). **No biopsy at presentation** unless atypical: **age <1 or >12 years, macroscopic haematuria, low C3, sustained hypertension, impaired renal function, or extrarenal features**.

**Nephrotic complications:** **spontaneous bacterial peritonitis with *S. pneumoniae*** (urinary loss of immunoglobulin and complement), **thrombosis including renal vein thrombosis** (antithrombin loss), hypovolaemia despite oedema, hyperlipidaemia.

**IgA nephropathy vs post-strep GN:** IgA gives **synpharyngitic haematuria — within 1–2 days of the sore throat, normal C3**; post-strep has a **1–2 week latency and a low C3**.

## 9.3 Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis)

Commonest childhood vasculitis, peak **2–8 years**, often post-URTI. Clinical diagnosis: **palpable purpura (mandatory), gravity/pressure-dependent — buttocks and extensor legs — with a normal platelet count**, plus ≥1 of arthritis/arthralgia (50–75%), colicky abdominal pain (~50%), nephritis (25–50%).

**The short version**
- **Palpable purpura on buttocks and legs with normal platelets** = HSP; low platelets means something else.
- **Urinalysis is usually the only investigation needed** in a classic presentation.
- Acute danger: **intussusception** (ileo-ileal — may be missed by air enema, needs ultrasound) and GI bleeding.
- Chronic danger: **nephritis appearing weeks after the rash has gone** — hence prolonged surveillance.

Follow-up (RCH): **BP and urinalysis weekly for a month, fortnightly to week 12, then at 6 and 12 months.** Steroids may shorten severe abdominal pain and arthritis but **do not alter renal outcome** — classic distractor. Refer for hypertension, persistent proteinuria or impaired renal function.

## 9.4 Haemolytic uraemic syndrome

**Triad: microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia + thrombocytopenia + AKI**, typically **5–10 days after bloody diarrhoea** from Shiga-toxin-producing *E. coli* (undercooked mince, unpasteurised milk, petting zoos, contaminated water). Film shows **schistocytes**; **DAT negative** (separating it from autoimmune haemolysis); high LDH, oligoanuria, hypertension.

Management is **supportive** — fluid and electrolyte care, transfusion, dialysis in ~50%. **Antibiotics and antimotility agents are contraindicated in STEC diarrhoea: they increase toxin release and HUS risk** ⚑. Avoid platelet transfusion unless bleeding. Atypical HUS (complement-mediated, no diarrhoea, relapsing, familial) → **eculizumab**. Notifiable.

**HUS vs TTP vs DIC:** the coagulation screen is the splitter — HUS is **normal**, DIC has **prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen, high D-dimer**. TTP is an adult disease with prominent neurology and ADAMTS13 deficiency.

## 9.5 Nocturnal enuresis

Night continence normally arrives between **5 and 7 years**; treatment rarely starts before **age 6** (spontaneous resolution ~15%/year).

**The short version**
- **Primary** = never dry 6 consecutive months; **secondary** = relapse after ≥6 months dry (look for UTI, diabetes, constipation, OSA, psychosocial stress, abuse).
- **Monosymptomatic** = no daytime lower urinary tract symptoms (no urgency, frequency, straining, daytime wetting).
- **Conservative measures first, then the alarm** — the alarm is first-line active treatment with the best long-term cure rate.
- **Desmopressin** is for alarm failure or short-term cover (camp, sleepover); relapse on stopping 60–70%.

Assessment: daytime symptoms, bowel habit (**treat constipation — commonest reversible contributor**), fluids and caffeine, snoring, family history (strong). **Dipstick is not routinely needed** in primary monosymptomatic enuresis without red flags; no bloods, no imaging. Red flags: poor growth, hypertension, polyuria/polydipsia, recurrent UTI, poor stream or straining, soiling, neurological signs or sacral dimple (occult dysraphism), persistent secondary enuresis.

Conservative: daytime fluid loading with evening restriction, void before bed, no punishment, reward **agreed behaviours** (getting up and going to the toilet) **not dry nights** — the child cannot control dryness directly.

**Alarm:** needs a motivated child and family; the child must **wake fully** and finish voiding in the toilet. Allow **6–8 weeks**, abandon if no early response by ~4 weeks, continue to **≥14 consecutive dry nights**.

**Desmopressin:** sublingual 120 → 240 microgram or oral 200 → 400 microgram nocte, with **fluid restriction from 1 h before to 8 h after** — hyponatraemic seizure is the risk. ⚑ **Intranasal desmopressin is no longer recommended**.

## 9.6 Undescended testis and phimosis

**Cryptorchidism** affects ~3% of term and ~30% of preterm boys. Most descend by **3 months corrected**; refer if undescended at **6 months corrected**, with **orchidopexy at 6–12 months** ⚑ (earlier than older texts, which said 12–18 months) to protect fertility and allow examination. Untreated: subfertility, **raised testicular malignancy risk (orchidopexy reduces it and makes tumours detectable but does not abolish it)**, torsion, hernia. **Retractile testis** sits in the scrotum without tension with a brisk cremasteric reflex — no surgery, but review (may ascend). **Ascending testis** needs surgery; **impalpable** needs laparoscopy. **Bilateral impalpable testes with hypospadias is a red flag for a difference of sex development, particularly a virilised female with CAH — urgent endocrine review, not routine surgical referral.**

**Phimosis.** Physiological non-retractility is normal (retractile in ~10% at 1 year, ~50% by 10); **do not force retraction** — it scars. Pathological phimosis is a white scarred ring from **balanitis xerotica obliterans** with ballooning and recurrent balanitis — **topical corticosteroid first**, circumcision if it fails. **Paraphimosis** is an emergency needing reduction.

## 9.7 Type 1 diabetes and paediatric DKA

New presentation: **polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, lethargy**, often with secondary enuresis, candidal nappy rash, or a "chest infection" that is actually Kussmaul breathing. **Immediate finger-prick glucose and ketones**, same-day referral — never a fasting glucose next week.

**DKA diagnosis (RCH/QPEC), all three:** BGL **>11 mmol/L**; **venous pH <7.3 or bicarbonate <18 mmol/L**; **ketones ≥3 mmol/L** (or ≥2+ on urinalysis).

| Severity | Venous pH | Bicarbonate | Assumed deficit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild | <7.3 | <18 | <5% |
| Moderate | <7.2 | <10 | 5–9% |
| Severe | <7.1 | <5 | ≥10% |

**The short version — how paediatric DKA differs from adult DKA (this difference is the exam)**
- **Bolus only for shock**: **10 mL/kg 0.9% saline over 20–30 min, repeated to a maximum of 20 mL/kg** — not the adult litre-an-hour.
- **Deficit replaced evenly over 48 hours**, not 24.
- **Insulin starts ~1 hour after fluids, and there is NO insulin bolus** — a bolus in a child is wrong.
- **Insulin 0.1 unit/kg/hour**, reduced to **0.05 unit/kg/hour** if age **<5 years**, during transfer, or presenting BGL <15 mmol/L.
- **Cerebral oedema** is the leading cause of DKA death in children and is largely absent from adult practice.

**Fluids.** Maintenance **plus** deficit over **48 hours** with 0.9% sodium chloride; subtract resuscitation fluid beyond the first 10 mL/kg from the deficit. **Once BGL ≤15 mmol/L switch to 0.9% saline with 5% glucose**, targeting BGL **5–10 mmol/L**; go to 10% glucose if BGL falls below 5 or drops faster than 5 mmol/L/hour while still acidotic. **Do not stop insulin to treat a falling glucose — add glucose**, because insulin is what clears the ketosis.

**Potassium.** Total body potassium is always depleted even when the serum level looks normal or high. Start **KCl 40 mmol/L** in the fluid; if **K⁺ <3.0 mmol/L replace potassium before starting insulin**; recheck within an hour of starting insulin. **Do not give bicarbonate** — it worsens intracellular acidosis and is linked to cerebral oedema; reserve for life-threatening hyperkalaemia on senior advice.

**Cerebral oedema** usually appears **4–12 hours into treatment**. Risk factors: **new-onset diabetes, age <5, pH ≤7.1, marked hypocapnia, raised urea, over-rapid fluid or sodium correction**. Warning signs: **headache, recurrent vomiting, rising blood pressure with falling heart rate, irritability then drowsiness, cranial nerve palsy, falling GCS, new incontinence**. Treat **before imaging**: **3% sodium chloride 3 mL/kg IV over 10–15 min OR mannitol 20% 0.5 g/kg IV over 10–20 min**, **cut the fluid rate by one-third**, nurse head up and midline, call ICU. **Do not delay for a CT.**

Ongoing care: multiple daily injections or pump, carbohydrate counting, HbA1c target **<7% (53 mmol/mol)**, sick-day rules (**never omit insulin**, check ketones, increase fluids), coeliac and thyroid screening.

## 9.8 Hypoglycaemia

Thresholds (RCH): **<3.0 mmol/L if symptomatic, or <2.6 mmol/L regardless of symptoms**; **<2.6 mmol/L** in neonates under 48 hours. The examined instruction: **take the hypo screen before or while treating**, because the diagnosis is unobtainable once glucose is corrected — **glucose, beta-hydroxybutyrate, insulin, C-peptide, cortisol, growth hormone, lactate** (plus free fatty acids, ammonia, acylcarnitines, amino acids if enough blood) and the **first urine afterwards** for ketones and organic acids.

Interpretation: **ketotic** with appropriate cortisol/GH = **ketotic hypoglycaemia of childhood** (lean toddler, illness or long fast, outgrown) — or endocrine deficiency if cortisol/GH are low. **Hypoketotic** = **hyperinsulinism** (detectable insulin and C-peptide, suppressed ketones; C-peptide low if exogenous insulin) or a **fatty acid oxidation defect** such as MCAD.

Treatment: enteral rapid carbohydrate if conscious; **10% glucose 2 mL/kg IV bolus** if severe or unable to feed, then an infusion, recheck at 15–30 min; IM glucagon if no IV access. Never discharge without a cause.

## 9.9 Congenital hypothyroidism, CAH and puberty

**Congenital hypothyroidism** (~1 in 2,000–3,000) is the commonest preventable cause of intellectual disability, and **the newborn is usually asymptomatic — which is why we screen**. Australian newborn bloodspot screening (heel prick, ideally **48–72 hours**) measures TSH. Late signs: prolonged jaundice, poor feeding, constipation, hypotonia, large fontanelles, umbilical hernia, macroglossia, hoarse cry. Commonest cause is **thyroid dysgenesis** (agenesis, hypoplasia, ectopic sublingual gland), then dyshormonogenesis. **Thyroxine within the first 2 weeks of life** gives normal neurodevelopment — do not wait for imaging. ⚑ A TSH-based screen **misses central hypothyroidism**.

**Congenital adrenal hyperplasia.** >90% is **21-hydroxylase deficiency**, autosomal recessive: cortisol and aldosterone cannot be made, ACTH drives hyperplasia, and precursors shunt into androgens — **17-hydroxyprogesterone accumulates and is the diagnostic test**.
- **Female infant:** **virilised genitalia at birth** (clitoromegaly, labial fusion), detected on day 1.
- **Male infant:** looks normal, so presents in **salt-wasting crisis at 1–3 weeks** — vomiting, poor feeding, weight loss, shock, **hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia, acidosis**. The trap stem is "vomiting 2-week-old, ?pyloric stenosis": **pyloric stenosis gives a hypochloraemic hypokalaemic alkalosis; CAH gives a hyperkalaemic acidosis** — that flip is the discriminator.
- Crisis: **0.9% saline 20 mL/kg**, **IV hydrocortisone** (~25 mg infant / 50 mg child stat), glucose, treat hyperkalaemia. Long term hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone (+ salt in infancy), **stress dosing in illness**.
- ⚑ **CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency) IS now a target condition on the Australian newborn bloodspot screening panel** — this changed with the national panel expansion, so "male CAH is picked up clinically in salt-wasting crisis" is no longer the right exam answer. A positive screen triggers the ANZSPED follow-up pathway.

**Precocious puberty**: secondary sexual characteristics before **8 in girls, 9 in boys**. Central is usually **idiopathic in girls but pathological in boys — image the brain in a boy**. Peripheral: CAH, adrenal or gonadal tumour, McCune-Albright ("coast of Maine" café-au-lait + fibrous dysplasia), exogenous steroid. Work-up: **bone age (advanced), LH/FSH ± GnRH stimulation, oestradiol/testosterone, 17-OHP, pelvic ultrasound, MRI if central**. Treat central precocious puberty with a **GnRH agonist** to protect final height. **Growth velocity and bone age separate benign premature thelarche/adrenarche from true precocious puberty.**

**Delayed puberty**: no breast development by **13** (girls), testes <4 mL by **14** (boys). First tests: **bone age, LH/FSH, karyotype where indicated, coeliac serology, TFTs, inflammatory markers**.
- **Low LH/FSH:** constitutional delay (**commonest, boys, family history, delayed bone age, growth velocity normal for bone age** — reassure ± short testosterone course), chronic illness, coeliac disease, anorexia/overtraining, **Kallmann (anosmia)**, pituitary tumour.
- **High LH/FSH (primary gonadal failure):** **Turner 45,X** (short, webbed neck, coarctation/bicuspid valve, streak ovaries — **karyotype every short girl with delayed puberty**), **Klinefelter 47,XXY** (tall, small firm testes, gynaecomastia), chemo/radiotherapy.

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 10: Paediatric Cardiology
*Australian sources: RHDAustralia 2020 ARF/RHD guideline, RCH Melbourne CPGs, Queensland Paediatric Clinical Guidelines, AIHW. Scope: recognition, discriminators, thresholds, first drug.*

## 10.1 Fetal and transitional circulation — why it matters
Three fetal shunts bypass the non-ventilating lungs: ductus venosus, foramen ovale, ductus arteriosus. Placenta = low resistance, lungs = high; at birth this inverts. First breaths drop pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), cord clamping raises systemic resistance (SVR), LA pressure shuts the foramen, and rising PaO₂ with falling prostaglandin closes the duct (functionally by 24–72 h). The exam value is in the timing.

**The short version**
- **Left-to-right shunts (VSD, PDA) declare at 4–8 weeks**, as PVR falls — not at birth.
- **Duct-dependent lesions declare at day 2–14**, when the duct shuts.
- Pre-ductal (right hand) SpO₂ exceeding post-ductal (foot) by **>3%** (the CCHD pulse-oximetry screening threshold — normal is ≥95% at both sites with ≤3 points between them) = right-to-left ductal shunt (PPHN, coarctation, interrupted arch).
- Cyanosis at birth with a quiet chest and no respiratory distress = cardiac until proven otherwise.

## 10.2 Innocent vs pathological murmurs — the discharge decision
**Innocent = systolic, soft (≤2/6), short, positional, asymptomatic child, normal pulses, no thrill.**

| Feature | Innocent | Pathological |
|---|---|---|
| Timing, loudness | Systolic, ≤2/6, no thrill | **Any diastolic, pansystolic or continuous (bar venous hum)**; ≥3/6; **thrill** |
| Posture, S2 | Louder supine, softer standing; normal split | Fixed; **fixed split (ASD), single loud S2 (TGA/TOF)** |
| Context | Normal growth, **normal femorals** | Faltering growth, sweating on feeds, cyanosis, **absent femorals**, hepatomegaly, **age <1 y** |

Named innocent murmurs: **Still's** (2–7 y, low-pitched vibratory/musical, LLSB, louder supine); **pulmonary flow** (soft ESM upper LSE); **venous hum** (**continuous, right infraclavicular, abolished by lying flat, head-turn or jugular compression** — the discriminator from PDA, which persists); **peripheral PS of the newborn** (ESM to both axillae, gone by 3–6 months).

⚑ Australian practice: an asymptomatic child with normal saturations, femorals and growth needs **no routine CXR/ECG** — **echo is the only investigation of value**. Refer all murmurs in infancy, all diastolic murmurs, any murmur with symptoms.

## 10.3 Acyanotic congenital heart disease
**The short version**
- VSD = pansystolic LLSB, **loudest when smallest**; big VSDs are soft and sick.
- ASD = **fixed split S2**, often missed till school age.
- PDA = **continuous machinery murmur**, bounding pulses.
- Coarctation = **absent/delayed femorals**, arm-BP > leg-BP.

| Lesion | Clue → diagnosis | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **VSD** (commonest) | Well at birth; poor feeding, sweating, faltering growth at 4–8 wk; **harsh pansystolic LLSB, loud = small** | Small muscular defects close spontaneously; large: frusemide + ACEi, high-calorie feeds, closure before pulmonary vascular disease |
| **ASD** (secundum) | Asymptomatic or recurrent chest infections; **wide fixed split S2** + ESM upper LSE (pulmonary valve flow, not the ASD) | Device or surgical closure; prevents later AF, RV failure, paradoxical embolism |
| **PDA** | Preterm failing to wean off support, or bounding pulses + **continuous infraclavicular murmur** | Preterm: fluid restriction ± ibuprofen (⚑ watchful waiting increasingly accepted); older: device closure |
| **Coarctation** | Shocked neonate day 2–14, **or** older child with hypertension + leg claudication | Neonate **PGE1** then repair. Associations **bicuspid aortic valve, Turner** |

Association traps: **Down → AVSD**; **Turner → coarctation/bicuspid AV**; **22q11 → truncus, interrupted arch, TOF**; **rubella → PDA**; **maternal anti-Ro → complete heart block**; **maternal diabetes → TGA, HCM**. **Eisenmenger:** an unrepaired large shunt reverses once PVR exceeds SVR → cyanosis, clubbing, loud P2, **and the murmur disappears**; closure is then contraindicated, hence early repair.

## 10.4 Cyanotic congenital heart disease
Cyanosis is visible only at ≥30–50 g/L deoxygenated Hb, so **an anaemic infant can be profoundly hypoxaemic and look pink** — trust the probe. Australian newborn pre/post-ductal pulse-oximetry screening (~24 h) targets these lesions.

| Lesion | Clue → diagnosis |
|---|---|
| **Tetralogy of Fallot** | Cyanosis over weeks–months + **hypercyanotic "tet" spells**; harsh ESM upper LSE from RVOT obstruction — **the murmur gets SOFTER in a spell**; CXR boot-shaped heart, oligaemic lungs |
| **TGA** | **Cyanosis within hours**, profound, **unresponsive to oxygen**, often **no murmur**, **single loud S2**; CXR "egg on a string" |

**Tet spell:** knees-to-chest (older child squats — raises SVR, cuts right-to-left shunt) → calm → oxygen → **morphine** (abolishes hyperpnoea) → fluid bolus → **phenylephrine** (raise SVR) → **propranolol/esmolol** (infundibular spasm). Hook: **raise SVR, relax the infundibulum, stop the crying.** Never give inotropes that raise contractility — they worsen dynamic outflow obstruction.

## 10.5 Duct-dependent lesions and the collapsed neonate — prostaglandin E1
**Clue → diagnosis: a neonate well at discharge who collapses at day 2–14 with shock, absent femorals, metabolic acidosis and no response to fluid.** Indistinguishable from septic shock at first look. **Duct-dependent systemic** (HLHS, critical AS, critical coarctation, interrupted arch) → shock, absent femorals, acidosis. **Duct-dependent pulmonary** (pulmonary/critical PS, severe TOF, tricuspid atresia) → profound cyanosis.

- **Start prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) IV on suspicion, before the echo** — typically 5–10 nanogram/kg/min (higher if the duct has closed).
- **Anticipate apnoea** — the dose-limiting effect (also hypotension, fever). **Airway kit and an intubation-capable operator at the bedside; secure access before transfer.**
- **Give antibiotics concurrently** — sepsis cannot be excluded. Urgent retrieval (NETS/state service).
- **Avoid high-concentration oxygen** in duct-dependent systemic lesions: O₂ constricts the duct and drops PVR, stealing systemic flow. Target SpO₂ ~75–85%.

## 10.6 Heart failure in infancy
Infants cannot report dyspnoea — **feeding is the exercise test**. Clue → diagnosis: **feeds >30–40 min, forehead sweating on feeding, breathless pauses, downward centile crossing.** Signs: tachypnoea, tachycardia, **hepatomegaly (most reliable volume-overload sign — peripheral oedema and raised JVP are adult findings, unhelpful here)**, gallop. Manage: high-calorie feeds (130–150 kcal/kg/day, often NG), **frusemide**, **ACE inhibitor** for volume-loaded shunts, then repair.

## 10.7 The hyperoxia test — outline
Separates cyanotic CHD from lung disease. **Right radial (pre-ductal) arterial PaO₂ in air, repeated after 100% O₂ for ~10 min: >150 mmHg (~20 kPa) → lung cause; <100 mmHg (~13 kPa) → cyanotic CHD with a fixed right-to-left shunt; intermediate → mixing lesions or PPHN.** **Pulse oximetry cannot substitute.** ⚑ Superseded where 24-hour echo exists — answer "early echo if available".

## 10.8 Acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease
Autoimmune sequela of **group A streptococcal** infection — pharyngitis, and in northern Australia likely **skin sores** too — by molecular mimicry, 2–4 weeks later (chorea may be months). Rates in **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples** of northern and central Australia are among the world's highest; Māori and Pacific Islander populations are also elevated (AIHW). **Notifiable**; managed through state/territory RHD registers.

⚑ **Australia uses the 2020 RHDAustralia guideline, which modifies the 2015 Revised Jones criteria for high-risk populations.** UK/NICE texts give the low-risk criteria only — applying those to an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander child is the error to avoid. **High-risk population** = Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, especially rural/remote and northern Australia, and others in ARF-endemic settings (conventionally ARF incidence >30/100,000/yr at 5–14 y, or RHD prevalence >2/1000).

| | **High-risk (Australia)** | **Low-risk** |
|---|---|---|
| **Major** | Carditis **incl. subclinical valvulitis on echo**; **polyarthritis OR aseptic monoarthritis OR polyarthralgia**; Sydenham chorea; erythema marginatum; subcutaneous nodules | Carditis incl. subclinical; **polyarthritis only**; chorea; erythema marginatum; nodules |
| **Minor** | **Fever ≥38.0°C**; **monoarthralgia**; **ESR ≥30 mm/h or CRP ≥30 mg/L**; prolonged PR for age | **Fever ≥38.5°C**; polyarthralgia or aseptic monoarthritis; **ESR ≥60 mm/h or CRP ≥30 mg/L**; prolonged PR for age |

**Diagnosis** (both groups need evidence of preceding GAS — elevated/rising ASOT or anti-DNase B, or throat culture/PCR): **definite initial ARF = 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor. Definite recurrent ARF = 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor, or 3 minor.** **Probable/possible ARF** falls short by one manifestation or lacks serology but remains most likely — still register and give prophylaxis. **Chorea and indolent carditis need no strep evidence and no other criteria.** Hook — **J♥NES**: Joints, ♥ carditis, Nodules, Erythema marginatum, Sydenham chorea. Australian modification: **in high-risk children joint involvement is major even if only pain, and even if only one joint.**

**Acute treatment: benzathine benzylpenicillin G IM single dose — ≥20 kg 1,200,000 U (900 mg); <20 kg 600,000 U (450 mg)**. ⚑ **Lidocaine as diluent is now permitted and recommended** (older texts said contraindicated). Aspirin or naproxen for arthritis — ⚑ **withhold NSAIDs until the joint pattern declares**, since early analgesia aborts the migratory polyarthritis and hides the diagnosis. Echo all cases. **Secondary prophylaxis: the same IM dose every 28 days**, via the state register/recall system; **3-weekly** only for breakthrough ARF despite documented adherence. Erythromycin if true allergy.

| Category | Minimum duration |
|---|---|
| ARF, **no** carditis | **5 y or until age 21**, whichever longer ⚑ (was 10 y) |
| **Mild** RHD | 10 y or until **age 21** |
| **Moderate** RHD | 10 y or until **age 35** |
| **Severe** RHD / post-valve surgery | 10 y or until **age 40**; often lifelong |

Primary prevention = prompt treatment of GAS pharyngitis and, in endemic settings, skin sores.

## 10.9 Kawasaki coronary complications (cross-reference)
Full features in the fever/rash section. **Untreated, ~20–25% develop coronary aneurysms; IVIG 2 g/kg within 10 days of fever onset cuts this to ~4–5%.** Echo at diagnosis and at **4–6 weeks**, repeated earlier if high-risk or an abnormal baseline; **giant aneurysm (z-score ≥10 or ≥8 mm)** → warfarin + aspirin. **Suspect incomplete Kawasaki in any infant with ≥5 days fever and 2–3 criteria; AHA additionally advises echo in an infant ≤6 months with ≥7 days unexplained fever and systemic inflammation, with no criteria at all — infants have the fewest features and the highest aneurysm risk.**

## 10.10 Supraventricular tachycardia in infants
**Clue → diagnosis: poor feeding, pallor, irritability or shock with a rate that is fast and does not vary.** **SVT: infant >220/min, child >180/min, fixed and monotonous, abrupt on/off, P waves absent or buried.** **Sinus tachycardia: slower, varies with crying/fever/handling, 1:1 P waves, and has a cause.** Usually AV re-entry via an accessory pathway — check the post-conversion ECG for **WPW (short PR, delta wave)**. Infants tolerate it for days, presenting late with hepatomegaly and shock.

1. **Stable:** vagal manoeuvres — **ice/iced water to the face for 15–20 s** in infants, Valsalva in older children. **Never ocular pressure.**
2. **Adenosine** rapid push into a large proximal vein + flush, ECG running: **0.1 mg/kg (max 6 mg) → 0.2 mg/kg (max 12 mg) → 0.3 mg/kg**. Avoid in asthma.
3. **Unstable: synchronised DC cardioversion 1 J/kg then 2 J/kg**; don't delay for access.
4. Long-term propranolol, flecainide, ablation. **Avoid verapamil in infants** — cardiovascular collapse.

## 10.11 Syncope vs seizure in the older child
| Feature | Vasovagal | Cardiac | Seizure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Standing, heat, pain, blood | **During exercise, swimming, sudden fright/noise** | Any, or sleep |
| Prodrome | Nausea, warmth, greying vision | **None** | Aura ± |
| Motor, recovery | Brief jerks *after* collapse; orientated <1 min | Jerks if prolonged; rapid recovery | **Rhythmic, sustained >30 s**; **post-ictal confusion**; lateral tongue-bite |
| Other | Injury uncommon | **Family history of sudden death <40 y, congenital deafness** | — |

**Red flags → ECG ± cardiology: syncope during exercise, no prodrome, with palpitations or chest pain, while supine, family history of sudden cardiac death, unexplained drowning.** ⚑ **Every child with syncope gets a 12-lead ECG** — long QTc (>460–480 ms), WPW, Brugada, HCM, ARVC. Reflex anoxic seizure (toddler, brief asystole after pain/fright, pallor then stiffening) is a benign mimic.

| Continuous murmur abolished by lying flat or neck compression / one that persists | Venous hum / PDA |
| Well neonate collapses day 2–14, absent femorals, fluid-unresponsive | Duct-dependent lesion → **PGE1 + antibiotics** |
| Main adverse effect of PGE1 | **Apnoea** — prepare the airway |
| Cyanotic spell in which the murmur gets *softer* | Tet spell — knees to chest, O₂, morphine, phenylephrine |
| ARF major criteria unique to high-risk Australian populations | **Aseptic monoarthritis and polyarthralgia are MAJOR** (plus subclinical carditis) |
| Fever / ESR minor criteria, high vs low risk | **≥38.0°C, ESR ≥30** vs **≥38.5°C, ESR ≥60** (CRP ≥30 both) |
| Combination unique to *recurrent* ARF | **3 minor** + strep evidence |
| Secondary prophylaxis | Benzathine benzylpenicillin G IM **4-weekly**, 1.2 million U (≥20 kg) / 600,000 U (<20 kg) |
| Duration after ARF without carditis | **5 y or until 21**, whichever longer ⚑ |
| Infant HR 260, fixed, hepatomegaly | SVT — ice to face → adenosine 0.1 mg/kg → sync DCR 1 J/kg |

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 11: Paediatric Musculoskeletal and Orthopaedics
*Australian sources: RCH Melbourne CPGs (limping child, DDH, fractures), Children's Health Queensland, Therapeutic Guidelines, RACGP.*

## 11.1 The limping child — age bands and the must-not-miss list
**The short version**
- **Age band narrows the differential more than any single sign.**
- **Must not miss: septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, SUFE, malignancy, non-accidental injury, referred abdominal/testicular pathology.**
- **Acute refusal to weight-bear + fever = septic arthritis until excluded.**
- **Examine joint above and below, plus spine, abdomen and scrotum** — hip pain refers to the knee.

| Age | Common | Must not miss |
|---|---|---|
| **0–3 y** | Toddler's fracture (undisplaced spiral tibia), viral myositis | **Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, late DDH, NAI, leukaemia** |
| **3–10 y** | **Transient synovitis** (commonest), trauma, myositis | **Septic arthritis, Perthes (4–8 y), JIA, malignancy** |
| **10–16 y** | Osgood–Schlatter, Sever, patellofemoral pain, stress fracture | **SUFE, septic arthritis, bone tumour** |

Red flags: **night pain waking the child, no improvement over days, fever/weight loss/night sweats, bruising or pallor, a mass, limp without trauma in a non-verbal child.** **Leukaemia trap:** nocturnal bone pain with pallor or bruising → **FBC and film**; a normal WCC excludes nothing.

## 11.2 Septic arthritis vs transient synovitis — Kocher criteria
**Clue → diagnosis: a febrile, unwell child holding the hip flexed/abducted/externally rotated who screams on any passive movement has septic arthritis; a well, afebrile child limping after a URTI with some preserved range has transient synovitis.**

| | **Septic arthritis** | **Transient synovitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Systemic, weight-bearing | **Toxic, febrile; refuses to weight-bear** | Well; limps but usually will |
| Passive range | **Guards absolutely, pain through the whole arc** | Mild restriction (internal rotation) |
| Bloods, ultrasound | **WCC, CRP/ESR raised**; effusion — **presence does NOT distinguish the two** | Normal or mildly raised |
| Action | **Aspiration ± washout + IV antibiotics — emergency** | Rest, NSAIDs, review 24–48 h |

**Kocher criteria** (hip): **non-weight-bearing**, **fever >38.5°C**, **ESR >40 mm/h**, **WCC >12 × 10⁹/L**. Probability of septic arthritis ≈ **0 criteria <1%, 1 ~3%, 2 ~40%, 3 ~93%, 4 ~99%.** Hook: **"Fever, WCC, ESR, Won't Walk."** Many Australian units add **CRP >20 mg/L** as a fifth predictor.

⚑ Risk stratification, **not** a rule-out — **if the child looks septic, aspirate regardless of score.** Aspirate under imaging before antibiotics; empirical **IV flucloxacillin** (*S. aureus*), plus cover for ***Kingella kingae*** under 4 y, and **MRSA cover in northern Australia and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities**.

## 11.3 Developmental dysplasia of the hip
Risk factors — **the 4 F's plus family: Female, First-born, Frank breech, Family history**, plus oligohydramnios and packaging deformities. **Left hip most often.**
- **Ortolani** — the hip is **Out**; abduct and lift the trochanter → **clunk of reduction**. **Barlow** — adduct and push posteriorly → **it Bounces out**. **Both unreliable after ~3 months**, when **limited abduction becomes the most reliable sign**, with asymmetric thigh creases, **Galeazzi sign** (unequal knee heights on flexed hips) and Trendelenburg gait.
- **Imaging: <4.5–6 months → ultrasound** (cartilaginous head is radiolucent); **>4.5–6 months → AP pelvic X-ray**. ⚑ Australia uses **selective, risk-factor-based ultrasound screening, not universal newborn ultrasound**; scanning before 4–6 weeks yields false positives from physiological laxity.
- **Pavlik harness** (flexion + abduction) under 6 months, then closed reduction and spica, then open reduction. Complication: **AVN from excessive abduction in the harness**.

## 11.4 Perthes disease
**Idiopathic avascular necrosis of the capital femoral epiphysis.** Clue → diagnosis: **boy 4–8 y (M:F ~4–5:1), insidious painless or mildly painful limp over weeks–months, groin/thigh/knee pain, systemically well, loss of internal rotation and abduction.** **Bilateral disease should prompt thought of skeletal dysplasia or hypothyroidism instead.** X-ray (AP + frog-leg): small dense epiphysis, widened joint space, **subchondral crescent sign**, then fragmentation → reossification; MRI if films normal but suspicion high. Management = **containment** of the head in the acetabulum (activity restriction, physiotherapy for abduction; osteotomy if older or head-at-risk). **Younger onset = better prognosis.**

## 11.5 Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SUFE)
**Clue → diagnosis: overweight peri-pubertal adolescent (10–16 y, boys more often) with weeks of vague hip, thigh or KNEE pain, externally rotated leg, lost internal rotation, and obligate external rotation on hip flexion.** The epiphysis slips posteroinferiorly through the physis. **Atypical age (<10 or >16 y), non-obese or bilateral → screen for hypothyroidism, growth hormone therapy, hypogonadism, renal osteodystrophy.** 

**Loder classification drives everything: stable** = can weight-bear ± crutches, AVN risk low; **unstable** = **cannot weight-bear even with crutches**, **AVN risk up to ~50%** — an emergency.

⚑ **Imaging: AP pelvis PLUS cross-table (or true) lateral. Do NOT order a frog-leg lateral in a suspected unstable slip — the forced abduction and external rotation needed to position the leg can complete the slip and infarct the head.** Frog-leg views remain acceptable for stable slips and for Perthes. **Klein's line** — drawn along the superior femoral neck on AP, it should cut the epiphysis; if not, the head has slipped.

Management: **strict non-weight-bearing, analgesia, nil by mouth, urgent orthopaedics for in-situ screw fixation** — do not let the child walk to radiology. **Consider prophylactic contralateral fixation** (bilateral in ~20–40%). Complications: AVN, chondrolysis, early OA.

## 11.6 Osteomyelitis
Haematogenous, **metaphyseal**, classically distal femur/proximal tibia. ***S. aureus*** predominates; ***Kingella kingae*** under 4 y; **Salmonella** in sickle cell; GBS/*E. coli* in neonates. **Community-associated MRSA is prevalent enough in northern Australia and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities to change empirical choice.** Clue → diagnosis: **fever with focal tenderness over the metaphysis (not the joint line) and reluctance to use the limb**; neonates may show only pseudoparalysis. **CRP is the best monitoring marker**; **blood cultures before antibiotics**; **plain X-ray is normal for the first 7–10 days — a normal film excludes nothing**; **MRI is the imaging of choice**. IV **flucloxacillin** per Therapeutic Guidelines — ⚑ **the RCH bone-and-joint guideline now gives cefazolin 50 mg/kg (max 2 g) 8-hourly** as the empirical choice, so say which you are quoting — (clindamycin/vancomycin if MRSA or allergy); ⚑ **early oral switch (often 2–3 days by response and CRP), 3–4 weeks total** is now Australian practice, not 6 weeks IV.

## 11.7 Juvenile idiopathic arthritis
**Arthritis in ≥1 joint, ≥6 weeks, onset before the 16th birthday, other causes excluded** — a diagnosis of exclusion (rule out septic/reactive arthritis and **leukaemia, which masquerades as "JIA that won't respond"**).

| Subtype | Pattern | Associations |
|---|---|---|
| **Oligoarticular** (~50%) | ≤4 joints in 6 months; young girls; knees/ankles | **ANA positive → highest chronic uveitis risk** |
| **Polyarticular RF-neg / RF-pos** | ≥5 joints symmetrical / adolescent girls, small joints | Moderate uveitis risk / adult-RA-like, erosive, anti-CCP |
| **Systemic (Still)** | **Quotidian spiking fever ≥2 wk, salmon-pink evanescent rash with the spikes, hepatosplenomegaly, serositis**; arthritis may lag | **Macrophage activation syndrome — falling ESR with rising ferritin, cytopenias, coagulopathy** |
| **Enthesitis-related** | Older boys, enthesitis, sacroiliitis, asymmetric lower limb | **HLA-B27**; **acute painful red-eye uveitis** |
| **Psoriatic** | Arthritis + psoriasis, dactylitis, nail pitting | Uveitis risk |

**Uveitis screening:** chronic anterior uveitis in JIA is **asymptomatic, with a white painless eye**, and blinds. **All subtypes need slit-lamp screening; frequency by risk — ~3-monthly for young, ANA-positive, early-onset oligoarticular girls.** Enthesitis-related arthritis is the exception: acute, painful, red. Ladder: NSAIDs → intra-articular steroid → **methotrexate** → biologics.

## 11.8 Fractures and non-accidental injury patterns
Children's bones are porous, with thick periosteum and open physes — they bend and buckle. **Remodelling is best in young children, near the physis, in the plane of joint movement; rotational malalignment does NOT remodel.**
- **Greenstick** — one cortex breaks, the other bends. **Buckle/torus** — metaphyseal compression, classically distal radius; ⚑ **removable splint ~3 weeks, no cast, no routine repeat X-ray**. **Plastic deformation** — bowing, no visible break. **Toddler's fracture** — undisplaced spiral distal tibia, often occult.
- **Salter–Harris (SALTR):** I **S**traight across; II **A**bove (metaphyseal fragment, **commonest**); III be**L**ow (epiphysis, intra-articular); IV **T**hrough both; V c**R**ush (**worst prognosis**). **III–V need orthopaedics; III/IV need anatomical reduction.**

| Pattern suggesting abuse | Why |
|---|---|
| **Any fracture in a non-mobile infant** | Strongest single red flag — babies who cannot roll or cruise do not fracture themselves |
| **Posterior/lateral rib fractures** | AP chest compression from squeezing; highly specific |
| **Metaphyseal corner / "bucket-handle" fractures** | Shearing from violent pulling or shaking; highly specific |
| Multiple fractures of different ages; scapular, sternal, spinous process; complex or suture-crossing skull fractures | Repeated injury, or energy far beyond the stated mechanism |
| **Bruising in a non-mobile baby**; ears/neck/torso/buttocks/genitalia; patterned marks; **frenulum tear**; cigarette burns; **immersion scalds with sharp tide lines, no splash** | Site and pattern inconsistent with an accidental mechanism |

Concerning histories are covered in §12.1. Workup: skeletal survey ± repeat films at ~2 weeks, head imaging and ophthalmology (retinal haemorrhages) in infants. Mimics: **osteogenesis imperfecta** (blue sclerae, Wormian bones), rickets, coagulopathy. Reporting → §12.2.

## 11.9 Scoliosis
**Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis** (~80%): girls, growth spurt, painless, right thoracic curve. **Adams forward bend test — a rib hump persisting on flexion means structural scoliosis; postural asymmetry disappears.** Scoliometer >5–7° trunk rotation → erect PA X-ray for **Cobb angle**. **<20–25° observe; ~25–40° with growth remaining → brace; >40–50° → surgery.** **Refer regardless of curve size for pain, rapid progression, onset <10 y, LEFT thoracic curve, neurological signs, café-au-lait macules or midline skin lesions** — tumour, syringomyelia, tethered cord, neurofibromatosis; MRI indicated.

## 11.10 Normal variants — and when they aren't
**The short version**
- Most in-toeing, bow legs and flat feet are **physiological, symmetrical, painless and self-resolving**.
- Refer if **asymmetrical, painful, progressive, associated with short stature, or outside the expected age window**.

| Variant | Normal course | Refer when |
|---|---|---|
| **In-toeing** | **Metatarsus adductus** (infant) → **internal tibial torsion** (1–3 y, gone ~4–6 y) → **femoral anteversion** (3–8 y, "W" sitting, gone ~10 y) | Asymmetry, pain, worsening tripping, persistence beyond window |
| **Bow legs / knock knees** | Bowing physiological to ~2 y, then valgus peaking 3–4 y, resolving ~7 y | **Asymmetry, progression after 2 y, short stature** → rickets, **Blount disease**; valgus persisting >8 y |
| **Flat feet** | Flexible flat foot is normal; **arch returns on tiptoeing (heel-rise test), or on passive dorsiflexion of the great toe (Jack test / Hubscher manoeuvre)** | **Rigid** flat foot → tarsal coalition; also pain, stiffness, unilateral |
| **Toe-walking** | Idiopathic under ~3 y | Persistent, unilateral, tight heel cords → cerebral palsy, **Duchenne (CK in boys)** |

# Paediatrics Notes — Section 12: Child Protection, Social and Ethical Paediatrics
*Australian sources: state/territory child protection legislation, AIFS resource sheets, AIHW, Red Nose Australia, Australian Immunisation Handbook/NCIRS, RACGP, RCH CPGs. Law is jurisdiction-specific — verify locally.*

## 12.1 Recognising abuse and neglect
Four categories — **physical, sexual, emotional/psychological, neglect** — plus **exposure to family and domestic violence**, harm in its own right in most jurisdictions. ⚑ **Emotional abuse is the most commonly substantiated primary category nationally (AIHW, ~55%), then neglect (~21%), physical (~14%), sexual (~10%)**. Neglect is second and the easiest to miss: there is no single event.

**The short version**
- **A bruise or fracture in a non-mobile infant is the highest-yield red flag in paediatrics.**
- **The history is the diagnostic test** — inconsistency, delay and developmental impossibility outrank the injury's appearance.
- You report a **reasonable suspicion**, not a diagnosis. **You are not the investigator.**

| Domain | Concerning features |
|---|---|
| History | Delay without explanation; account inconsistent with the child's developmental stage; story changes between tellings or carers; blame on a sibling; repeat ED presentations |
| Physical | **Bruising in a non-mobile infant**; bruises to **ears, neck, torso, buttocks, genitalia**; patterned marks (belt, hand, cord); **frenulum tear**; bites; **cigarette burns**; **immersion scalds — sharp tide lines, glove-and-stocking, no splash**; **retinal haemorrhage + subdural in an infant (abusive head trauma)** |
| Skeletal, sexual | Fracture in a non-ambulant child; posterior rib and metaphyseal corner fractures; fractures of different ages (§11.8). Age-inappropriate sexualised behaviour; anogenital injury; **STI or pregnancy in a child**; **disclosure — itself sufficient grounds to report; do not "test" it with repeated questioning** |
| Emotional, neglect | Belittling, scapegoating, **frozen watchfulness**, indiscriminate affection, unexplained developmental delay; faltering growth that corrects in hospital; untreated medical/dental problems; missed immunisations; inadequate supervision |

Document the mimics: **congenital dermal melanocytosis (bluish sacral "Mongolian" patches, common in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander, Asian and Māori/Pasifika infants and routinely misread as bruising)**, osteogenesis imperfecta, coagulopathy or leukaemia, bullous impetigo, and practices such as cupping or coining.

## 12.2 Mandatory reporting in Australia — state-based
⚑ **There is no national mandatory reporting law. Each state and territory sets who must report, what is reportable, and the threshold.** UK-oriented texts describe a unitary safeguarding duty; Australia has none. Safe exam answer: *"I have a mandatory reporting obligation under my state's child protection legislation and would report to the relevant child protection authority, and to police if a criminal offence or immediate danger."*

Common to all: **doctors and registered nurses are mandated reporters in every jurisdiction** (⚑ **midwives are not a listed category in Queensland**, though they are elsewhere); the trigger is **belief on reasonable grounds — proof is not required and you do not investigate**; **family consent is not required**, the duty overriding confidentiality; good-faith reporting carries **legal and identity protection**.

| Jurisdiction | Legislation | Reportable types (mandated reporters) |
|---|---|---|
| **NSW** | Children and Young Persons (Care and Protection) Act 1998 — *"risk of significant harm"* | Broad — physical, sexual, emotional, neglect |
| **Vic** | Children, Youth and Families Act 2005 (s182) | **Physical injury and sexual abuse only** |
| **Qld** | Child Protection Act 1999 | **Physical and sexual abuse** |
| **WA** | Children and Community Services Act 2004 | **Sexual abuse only** under the mandatory provision |
| **SA** | Children and Young People (Safety) Act 2017 | Broad |
| **Tas** | Children, Young Persons and Their Families Act 1997 | Broad, incl. exposure to family violence |
| **ACT** | Children and Young People Act 2008 | **Physical and sexual abuse** |
| **NT** | Care and Protection of Children Act 2007 | Broad — **and the duty applies to EVERY person, not just professionals** |

⚑ **NT is the only jurisdiction imposing a universal duty on every adult**; elsewhere the statutory duty is profession-based. ⚑ **Victoria has a separate criminal "failure to disclose" offence (Crimes Act 1958):** **any adult** who forms a reasonable belief that an adult has committed a sexual offence against a **child under 16** must report it **to police**, independent of the profession-based duty; Victoria also has a **"failure to protect"** offence for people in positions of authority in organisations.

**Process:** document the history verbatim in the child's words plus findings (body maps, photographs per policy) → discuss with a senior clinician or the hospital child protection unit → **telephone report to the state helpline** (police if immediate danger) → ensure the child is safe before discharge (**admission is a legitimate safety measure**) → assess **siblings**. Be honest and non-accusatory **unless disclosure would increase risk**; family agreement is not required.

## 12.3 Consent and competence in children
Default: a parent or guardian consents, in the child's best interests. **Parents cannot refuse clearly life-saving treatment** — *parens patriae*, or the emergency doctrine.

**Gillick / mature minor:** adopted in Australia in **Secretary, Department of Health and Community Services v JWB and SMB ("Marion's case", HCA 1992)** — a child may consent once they have **sufficient understanding and intelligence to fully understand what is proposed**. It is **decision-specific and risk-proportionate**: a competent 15-year-old may consent to contraception but not to a high-risk irreversible procedure. **Once competent, parental consent is not additionally required and parents should not be told without the young person's agreement.**

⚑ **Statute displaces or supplements the common law in some jurisdictions:**
- **South Australia** (Consent to Medical Treatment and Palliative Care Act 1995): **16 and over consents as an adult**; **under 16, the treating practitioner must form the opinion that the child understands the nature, consequences and risks and that treatment is in their best interests, and a second medical practitioner who has personally examined the child must record a supporting opinion in writing** — the written requirement attaches to the second doctor. A two-doctor requirement unique to SA.
- ⚑ **Only SA and NSW have statute on minors’ consent.** Every other jurisdiction, the **Northern Territory included**, runs on the common-law *Gillick* / *Marion’s case* test. (The NT’s *Emergency Medical Operations Act 1973* governs operating without parental consent in an emergency; it does not set a consent age.)
- **NSW** (Minors (Property and Contracts) Act 1970, s49) gives a doctor a **defence to assault/trespass** where a child **aged 14+** consented — a protective provision, not a consent age; the common law test still operates.

Marion's case also established that some decisions sit **outside parental power and need court authorisation** — non-therapeutic sterilisation of a child with intellectual disability is the classic. **A competent minor's refusal of life-saving treatment is legally weaker than their consent**; courts have overridden it.

## 12.4 Confidentiality with adolescents
Adolescents get the same confidentiality as adults; **fear of parental disclosure is the main barrier to their seeking care**.
- **Set the frame before the sensitive material:** confidential, with three exceptions — **risk of serious harm to them, risk to someone else, or a mandatory reporting obligation** — and you will tell them first if that arises.
- **See the young person alone for part of every consultation** from early adolescence, framed as routine.
- Other limits: sexual activity with a significantly older person, or below the jurisdiction's age of consent (**again state-based**). Watch **Medicare item-history visibility to parents**; use confidential services (headspace, family planning).

## 12.5 Immunisation refusal
**Explore, don't confront.** Identify the actual concern — safety, ingredients, distrust, previous reaction, needle distress, or **access barriers, far more common than ideology**. Give absolute-risk information, use motivational interviewing rather than data-dumping, offer catch-up scheduling, document, and **keep the door open**. Distinguish true **contraindications** (anaphylaxis to a prior dose or component; live vaccines in significant immunosuppression or pregnancy) from **false** ones (mild intercurrent illness, family history of reactions, prematurity, breastfeeding, stable neurological disease, egg allergy for influenza vaccine).

Australian context: the **National Immunisation Program**, recorded on the **Australian Immunisation Register**; **"No Jab, No Pay"** links family assistance payments, and state **"No Jab, No Play"** laws childcare enrolment, to immunisation status. ⚑ **Conscientious objection is no longer an accepted exemption** — only certified medical contraindication. These are financial levers, **not compulsion**: a competent parent may still decline.

## 12.6 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander child health and social determinants
The gap is produced by **colonisation, dispossession, intergenerational trauma including the Stolen Generations, racism, and inequitable access to housing, education, food security and healthcare** — not by culture or behaviour. Frame answers in **structural determinants and strengths**, never deficit.

Burden (AIHW): **ARF and RHD** (§10.8); **early-onset persistent otitis media with conductive hearing loss and educational impact**; **bronchiectasis** after severe pneumonia; **impetigo and scabies with post-streptococcal sequelae including APSGN**; trachoma in some remote regions; anaemia and faltering growth from food insecurity; higher low birthweight, preterm birth and **SUDI**; **over-representation in out-of-home care**.

Practice: **record Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander identity for every child** (drives additional NIP vaccines, the annual **715 health assessment**, Closing the Gap PBS measures); **work with ACCHOs and Aboriginal Health Workers**; use interpreters; apply the **Child Placement Principle** if out-of-home care arises; and recognise that **kinship-based childrearing is a strength, not neglect**.

## 12.7 Sudden unexpected death in infancy and safe sleeping
⚑ **SUDI** is now the preferred Australian umbrella term, covering **SIDS** (unexplained after autopsy and scene review) and **fatal sleep accidents** (suffocation, wedging, overlay) — replacing the older "cot death"/SIDS-only framing and emphasising the **modifiable sleep environment**. Peak **2–4 months**; rare after 12 months.

Risk: **prone or side sleeping, soft or loose bedding, pillows, cot bumpers, sleeping on a sofa or in an adult bed, sharing a sleep surface with an adult who has used alcohol or sedatives, tobacco exposure ante- or postnatally, overheating and head covering, prematurity.** Breastfeeding, immunisation and room-sharing without bed-sharing are protective.

**Red Nose Australia — six safe sleep recommendations**
1. **Sleep baby on the back** from birth — never tummy or side.
2. **Keep head and face uncovered** (feet-to-foot, firm tucked-in bedding, no pillows/bumpers/loose blankets).
3. **Keep baby smoke-free**, before and after birth.
4. **Safe sleeping environment night and day** — safe cot to Australian Standard, firm flat mattress, safe bedding.
5. **Own safe sleep space in the parent's room for at least the first 6 months** — room-share, don't bed-share.
6. **Breastfeed** where possible.

**Responding to a SUDI:** resuscitate as indicated; be explicit and kind; **it is a reportable death to the coroner — autopsy and scene investigation are mandatory, not a sign of suspicion**; preserve the scene, involve social work and Red Nose bereavement support. **Scene investigation routinely assesses unsafe sleep and, where indicated, inflicted injury.**

## 12.8 Adolescent health and HEEADSSS
Most adolescent morbidity and mortality is **behavioural and psychosocial** — injury, suicide, substance use, sexual health, mental illness — so the psychosocial interview *is* the examination. **HEEADSSS** grades from least to most sensitive, **with the young person alone**, after setting confidentiality limits (§12.4).

| Domain | Ask about |
|---|---|
| **H**ome | Who's at home, relationships, moves, safety, out-of-home care, violence |
| **E**ducation/Employment | Attendance, engagement, change in performance, bullying |
| **E**ating | Body image, dieting, binge/purge, weight change, food security |
| **A**ctivities | Friends, sport, screens/social media, online safety |
| **D**rugs | Tobacco/**vaping**, alcohol, cannabis, others — self, peers, household |
| **S**exuality | Relationships, orientation and gender identity, activity, consent, contraception, STI risk, sexting/coercion |
| **S**uicide/mood | Mood, sleep, anhedonia, self-harm — **ask directly about ideation and plan; asking does not create risk** |
| **S**afety | Injury, helmets/seatbelts, driving, violence, weapons, abuse, exploitation |

**A sudden drop in school attendance or performance is the most sensitive general marker of psychosocial pathology**; ⚑ **vaping is now the dominant nicotine exposure among Australian adolescents**; and **sexual activity with a substantially older partner, coercion, or a partner below the age of consent triggers §12.2.**

## 13.1 Why children are not small adults

Paediatric dosing is not adult dosing scaled down by mass. Four physiological facts drive nearly every exam-relevant difference.

**Body composition.** Neonates are ~75% water (adults ~60%) with a proportionally larger extracellular compartment, so water-soluble drugs (aminoglycosides, β-lactams) have a **larger volume of distribution per kg** — the mg/kg loading dose is *higher* than in adults, not lower. Fat-soluble drugs behave oppositely.

**Protein binding.** Neonatal albumin is lower with reduced affinity, so free (active) drug fraction rises. Sulfonamides and ceftriaxone displace bilirubin from albumin — hence avoidance in jaundiced neonates and the risk of kernicterus.

**Hepatic clearance.** Phase II conjugation, especially **glucuronidation (UGT)**, matures last, reaching adult activity around 2–3 years. This explains **chloramphenicol grey baby syndrome** and neonatal paracetamol reduction. Sulfation partially compensates, which is why paracetamol is comparatively *safer* in toddlers than adults per kg. CYP enzymes mature over 6–12 months then **overshoot** — young children clear many drugs *faster* per kg than adults, so paediatric mg/kg doses often look startlingly high.

**Renal clearance.** GFR at term is ~20–30% of adult values, reaching adult levels by ~1–2 years. Renally cleared drugs need **extended intervals** in neonates — the interval stretches, and for some drugs the mg/kg dose falls too — gentamicin is **5 mg/kg** in the neonate against 7.5 mg/kg at 1 month–10 years (see §13.6).

**The neonatal exception.** Exam rule of thumb: *if the patient is <1 month old, assume the standard paediatric regimen does not apply and a neonatal protocol exists.* True for paracetamol, gentamicin, all sepsis antibiotics, and steroids in meningitis.

**The short version**
- Neonates: bigger Vd, immature glucuronidation, low GFR → **same or higher mg/kg, longer interval**.
- Toddlers/young children: mature-to-supranormal clearance → **higher mg/kg than adults**.
- Dose by **actual weight**; use adjusted body weight in obesity for aminoglycosides.
- Always cap at the adult dose.

## 13.2 Principles of weight-based prescribing

Doses are written **mg/kg/dose**, never mg/kg/day, unless stated otherwise — confusing the two is the commonest paediatric prescribing error and produces a 3–4 fold overdose. Every prescription needs a **documented, recently measured weight**; estimation is for resuscitation only, where ANZCOR/APLS prefer a **length-based tape** over age formulae.

Other recurring safety rules: write **micrograms in full**; no trailing zeros ("5 mg" not "5.0 mg") and always a leading zero ("0.5 mL"); and specify the **liquid concentration**, since paracetamol suspension comes as 24 mg/mL (labelled 1 month–2 y and 1–5 y) and 48 mg/mL (labelled 5–12 y) — a swap halves or doubles the dose. Volumes under 0.1 mL cannot be measured reliably and are the classic setting for **10-fold errors**; ASCIA flags this for adrenaline under 7.5 kg.

## 13.3 Fluids

### Maintenance: the Holliday–Segar / 4-2-1 rule

| Weight band | Per hour | Per day |
|---|---|---|
| First 10 kg | 4 mL/kg/h | 100 mL/kg/day |
| Second 10 kg | + 2 mL/kg/h | + 50 mL/kg/day |
| Each kg > 20 kg | + 1 mL/kg/h | + 20 mL/kg/day |

Worked example: 26 kg = (10×4) + (10×2) + (6×1) = **66 mL/h**. Ceilings apply — RCH ~100 mL/h / 2400 mL/day; SCHN 83 mL/h / 2000 mL/day at ≥50 kg.

**Limits of the rule.** Holliday–Segar was derived in 1957 from *healthy* children's energy expenditure. Hospitalised children are **non-osmotically ADH-secreting** (pain, nausea, vomiting, positive pressure ventilation, bronchiolitis, meningitis, post-operative state) and cannot excrete a free water load, so full maintenance causes dilutional hyponatraemia. Give **restricted maintenance (commonly two-thirds)** in at-risk children and check **electrolytes before starting and at least daily**.

### ⚑ Isotonic, not hypotonic

**The single most examinable change from older textbooks.** Hypotonic maintenance fluid (0.18% NaCl + 4% glucose, 0.45% NaCl) is **no longer recommended for routine paediatric maintenance** in Australia. Standard is **sodium chloride 0.9% + glucose 5%, ± potassium chloride 20 mmol/L**, in children ≥1 month.

Mechanism: high ADH → free water retention → **hospital-acquired hyponatraemia**. Children have a higher brain-to-cranial-vault ratio and less room to swell, so acute hyponatraemia causes cerebral oedema, seizures and death at sodium levels adults tolerate. Glucose stays in the bag — children have limited glycogen stores and become ketotic quickly — but the *sodium* must be isotonic.

### Resuscitation and deficit

**Bolus:** 10–20 mL/kg sodium chloride 0.9% (or balanced crystalloid) rapidly, reassessing after each. Convention is **20 mL/kg in children**, **10 mL/kg in neonates** and where fluid overload is dangerous — **DKA, cardiogenic shock, severe malnutrition, head injury**. Once **≥40 mL/kg** has been given without response, start **inotropes** (adrenaline first-line, from 0.1 microgram/kg/min) and involve PICU rather than bolusing indefinitely.

**Deficit:** deficit (mL) = weight (kg) × % dehydration × 10. A 22 kg child, 5% dehydrated → **1100 mL**, replaced over 24 h *in addition to* maintenance — over ≥48 h in hypernatraemic dehydration and DKA to avoid rapid osmolar shift.

**The short version**
- 4-2-1 for rate; 100-50-20 for daily volume; cap at adult maintenance.
- ⚑ **Isotonic (0.9% NaCl + 5% glucose) is standard**; hypotonic fluid causes fatal hyponatraemia.
- Bolus 20 mL/kg (10 in neonates, DKA, cardiac, malnutrition); inotropes after 40 mL/kg.
- Restrict maintenance and check electrolytes in any ADH-stimulated child.

## 13.4 DKA — the paediatric-specific rules

Paediatric DKA is deliberately managed **more slowly** than adult DKA because of **cerebral oedema** — the leading cause of death, precipitated by rapid osmolar correction.

| Parameter | Paediatric rule |
|---|---|
| Bolus | **10 mL/kg** 0.9% NaCl over 20–30 min, only if shocked; repeat to max 20 mL/kg |
| Rehydration | Deficit + maintenance **evenly over 48 h** ⚑ (not 24 h) |
| Insulin start | **1 hour after** fluids — never before, and **never a bolus** ⚑ |
| Insulin rate | **0.1 units/kg/h**; **0.05 units/kg/h** if <5 years, in transfer, or BGL <15 mmol/L |
| Add glucose | At **BGL ≤15 mmol/L**, switch to 5% (then 10%) glucose-containing fluid |
| Potassium | **40 mmol/L** once K⁺ known and passing urine; withhold if K⁺ >5.5 or anuric; correct first if K⁺ <3.0 |
| Cerebral oedema | **3% sodium chloride 3 mL/kg over 10–15 min**, or **mannitol 0.5 g/kg over 10–20 min** |

The logic: insulin is delayed and the deficit spread because *falling osmolality* — not hyperglycaemia — drives cerebral oedema. Bicarbonate is not given. Suspect cerebral oedema on **headache, bradycardia with hypertension (Cushing response), falling GCS, irritability** — typically 4–12 h into treatment.

## 13.5 Analgesia and antipyretics

| Drug | Dose | Frequency | Max daily |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paracetamol** oral | **15 mg/kg** (max 1 g) | 4–6 hourly | **60 mg/kg/day** (max 4 g) |
| Paracetamol rectal | 15–20 mg/kg (max 1 g) | 6 hourly | as above |
| Paracetamol, severe pain, inpatient | up to **90 mg/kg/day** | — | **48 hours only** |
| **Ibuprofen** oral (≥3 months) | **10 mg/kg** (max 400 mg) | 6–8 hourly, with food | **30 mg/kg/day** (max 2.4 g) |

Two traps. First, the **90 mg/kg/day paracetamol ceiling is a short-term inpatient allowance for severe pain, capped at 48 hours** — the routine maximum is 60 mg/kg/day, and repeated supratherapeutic dosing is a recognised route to hepatotoxicity in unwell, poorly-fed children. Second, **ibuprofen's maximum is 30 mg/kg/day**, not the 40 mg/kg/day older and overseas texts quote.

**Avoid NSAIDs in:** dehydration or renal hypoperfusion (gastroenteritis, sepsis, shock), renal impairment, GI bleeding, **bronchiolitis and infants <3 months**, coagulopathy, and — importantly — **varicella**, where NSAIDs are linked to necrotising soft tissue infection. Ibuprofen is *not* contraindicated in asthma generally, only in documented NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease.

**Opioids.** Morphine, oxycodone and intranasal fentanyl are all used; intranasal fentanyl is the standard non-cannulated option for acute severe pain (e.g. fracture) in ED. Infants under 3 months, and any child with obstructive sleep apnoea or neuromuscular disease, are far more sensitive to respiratory depression. Tramadol shares codeine's CYP2D6 problem.

### ⚑ Codeine

**Codeine is contraindicated under 12 years** in Australia, and additionally under 18 for analgesia after **tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy for obstructive sleep apnoea**. The TGA acted after deaths in **CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolisers** — codeine is a prodrug needing CYP2D6 conversion to morphine, and duplicated functional alleles produce a morphine surge with fatal respiratory depression. Ultra-rapid metaboliser mothers can transfer toxic morphine in breast milk. All codeine products are now prescription-only. Codeine should never be the correct answer in a paediatric analgesia question.

## 13.6 Antibiotics — Australian first-line by indication

| Indication | First-line (Australian) | Dose | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acute otitis media** (if indicated) | Amoxicillin | 15 mg/kg (**max 500 mg**) **8-hourly** | 5 days |
| **Strep pharyngitis/tonsillitis** | Phenoxymethylpenicillin | 15 mg/kg (max 500 mg) **12-hourly** | **10 days** |
| — IM alternative (adherence, ARF risk) | Benzathine benzylpenicillin | Weight-banded IM, single dose | once |
| **CAP, low severity** | Amoxicillin | **25 mg/kg** (max 1 g) 8-hourly | 3 days |
| **UTI — cystitis** | Cefalexin (or trimethoprim) | 20 mg/kg (**max 750 mg**) tds — the 25 mg/kg / max 1 g dose belongs to pyelonephritis under 12 months | 3–5 days |
| **UTI — pyelonephritis** | Cefalexin oral, or IV gentamicin if unwell | 25–45 mg/kg/dose oral; gentamicin below | 7–10 days |
| **Cellulitis, mild** | Cefalexin | 20–30 mg/kg (max 750 mg–1 g) tds | 5 days |
| — MRSA suspected | Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole | 4/20 mg/kg (max 160/800 mg) bd | 5–10 days |
| **Meningitis, >2 months** | Ceftriaxone **or** cefotaxime | **Ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg (max 4 g) daily**; cefotaxime 50 mg/kg (max 2 g) 6-hourly | per organism |
| **Sepsis, >2 months** | Ceftriaxone ± flucloxacillin/vancomycin | Ceftriaxone 50–100 mg/kg | — |
| **Sepsis/meningitis, neonate** | Benzylpenicillin (or ampicillin) **+** gentamicin **or** cefotaxime | Neonatal protocol — intervals extend at lower postmenstrual age | — |

**Gentamicin (once-daily):** **7.5 mg/kg (max 320 mg)** for 1 month to <10 years; **6 mg/kg (max 560 mg)** for ≥10 years; **5 mg/kg** in neonates at an interval set by postmenstrual age (24 h at ≥35 weeks, 36 h at 30–34 weeks, 48 h at <30 weeks).

**Dexamethasone in meningitis:** **0.15 mg/kg (max 10 mg)**, **with or just before the first antibiotic dose**, continued 6-hourly for 4 days in confirmed bacterial meningitis beyond the neonatal period. **Not advised in neonates.** Benefit is reduced sensorineural hearing loss, chiefly in *Haemophilus influenzae* type b; give it with or just before the first dose; if that is missed it is **still indicated within 4 h, and acceptable up to 12 h** — do not withhold it at 6–8 hours.

Two traps: **AOM amoxicillin is 15 mg/kg, CAP amoxicillin is a higher 25 mg/kg** — commonly transposed. And **strep throat is 10 days**, a duration chosen for acute rheumatic fever prevention, not symptom relief; in high-ARF-risk populations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in central and northern Australia) the threshold to treat sore throat is far lower.

### ⚑ Age restrictions

**Tetracyclines** chelate calcium in developing enamel and were traditionally avoided under 8 years (permanent discolouration, enamel hypoplasia). ⚑ This has softened: **short courses (<21 days) of doxycycline do not cause clinically significant staining**, and doxycycline is now used at any age where it is drug of choice — rickettsial infection, Q fever, severe *Mycoplasma*. **Prolonged or repeated courses** in under-8s are still avoided.

**Fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin) cause arthropathy in juvenile animal models and carry tendinopathy risk; **not first-line in children**, reserved for *Pseudomonas* in cystic fibrosis, complicated multiresistant Gram-negative UTI, and meningococcal contact prophylaxis. Human cartilage injury is unproven, so this is a relative, indication-driven restriction, not an absolute ban.

## 13.7 Corticosteroids: croup and asthma

### Croup

| Severity | Steroid |
|---|---|
| Mild–moderate | **Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg oral** (some services 0.15–0.3 mg/kg, max 12 mg) |
| If dexamethasone unavailable | **Prednisolone 1 mg/kg** oral |
| Severe / life-threatening | **Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg (max 12 mg)** PO/IM/IV |
| Severe, adjunct | **Nebulised adrenaline 5 mL of undiluted 1:1000** |

Steroid goes to **every** child with croup, including mild — oral dexamethasone works within 30 minutes and reduces re-presentation. Dexamethasone beats prednisolone: a longer half-life covers the illness in one dose, with less vomiting.

**Nebulised adrenaline** is symptomatic only and wears off in ~2 hours. Because of rebound, a child who receives it is **observed for 3 hours**, the clock restarting after a second dose.

### Asthma

**Prednisolone 1 mg/kg (max 50 mg) daily for 3 days** is the standard Australian regimen. Some services front-load with **2 mg/kg (max 50 mg) day 1, then 1 mg/kg days 2–3**. Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg as a single dose is an accepted alternative where vomiting or adherence is the concern. Courses ≤2 weeks need no taper. Preschool children with viral-induced wheeze and no interval symptoms gain little from oral steroids — an important negative.

⚑ **Bronchiolitis (<12 months) gets none of this.** The Australasian (PREDICT) guideline recommends **against** salbutamol, systemic and inhaled corticosteroids, adrenaline, routine antibiotics and nebulised hypertonic saline. Management is oxygen and feeding support. A wheezy 6-month-old with coryza is testing whether you will prescribe inappropriately.

## 13.8 Asthma drugs and delivery

| Age | Salbutamol (MDI + spacer) | Ipratropium (MDI + spacer) |
|---|---|---|
| **<6 years** | **6 puffs** | 4 puffs (84 microgram) |
| **≥6 years** | **12 puffs** | 8 puffs (168 microgram) |

Salbutamol MDI delivers 100 microgram/puff; ipratropium MDI 21 microgram/puff. Give a "burst" — **three doses at 20-minute intervals in the first hour** — then reassess. Nebulised equivalents: salbutamol 2.5 mg (<6 years) / 5 mg (≥6 years); ipratropium 250 / 500 microgram, added only in severe presentations and stopped after the first hour.

**Spacer, not nebuliser.** An MDI with a valved spacer is at least as effective as a nebuliser, causes less tachycardia and hypokalaemia, and avoids aerosol generation. Technique: **one puff at a time** into the spacer, then **4–6 tidal breaths**; never fire multiple puffs into the chamber at once. Under ~4 years, add a **well-fitting mask**.

**Escalation:** IV magnesium sulfate **0.2 mmol/kg = 50 mg/kg** over 20 min (max 8–10 mmol by jurisdiction); IV salbutamol **15 microgram/kg over 10 min (max 300 microgram)** then infusion; IV hydrocortisone **4 mg/kg (max 100 mg)** or methylprednisolone **1 mg/kg (max 60 mg)**. Aminophylline is third-line, ICU only.

**Preventer principles:** low-dose ICS is first-line; montelukast is an alternative carrying a **boxed neuropsychiatric warning** (sleep disturbance, agitation, mood change) that must be discussed with parents. ICS growth velocity reduction is small (~1 cm total) and outweighed by benefit — a favourite counselling question. LABAs are never used without concurrent ICS.

## 13.9 Adrenaline

### Anaphylaxis — ASCIA

**IM adrenaline 1:1000, 10 microgram/kg = 0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg per dose, outer mid-thigh.** Repeat every 5 minutes if no response. No absolute contraindication. Shorthand: **0.1 mL of 1:1000 per 10 kg**.

| Age (approx) | Weight | 1:1000 volume |
|---|---|---|
| <1 y | <7.5 kg | 0.1 mL |
| 1–2 y | 10 kg | 0.1 mL |
| 2–3 y | 15 kg | 0.15 mL |
| 4–6 y | 20 kg | 0.2 mL |
| 7–10 y | 30 kg | 0.3 mL |
| 10–12 y | 40 kg | 0.4 mL |
| >12 y / adult | ≥50 kg | 0.5 mL |

**Autoinjectors:** EpiPen Jr **150 microgram** for **7.5–20 kg** (~1–5 years); EpiPen **300 microgram** for **>20 kg**; Anapen **500 microgram** for **>50 kg**. Below 7.5 kg use drawn-up adrenaline — the volume is under 0.1 mL and the 10-fold error risk is real.

Adjuncts are just that: lie the child flat (**standing or sudden upright posture can precipitate fatal empty-ventricle arrest**), give oxygen, then consider salbutamol for bronchospasm. **Antihistamines and corticosteroids do not treat anaphylaxis** and must never delay adrenaline. Refractory cases go to an infusion.

### Cardiac arrest — ANZCOR

**Adrenaline 10 microgram/kg IV/IO (max 1 mg)**:
- **Non-shockable (asystole/PEA): immediately**, then **every second loop** (~4 minutes).
- **Shockable (VF/pVT): after the second shock** ⚑, then every second loop.

**Defibrillation 4 J/kg** for all shocks. **Amiodarone 5 mg/kg (max 300 mg) after the third shock.** Compressions:ventilations **15:2** (two-rescuer). Fluid bolus 10 mL/kg titrated to response.

⚑ Note the ANZCOR timing: **adrenaline after the second shock**, where UK/ERC gives it after the third. An Australian paper expects the ANZCOR answer.

## 13.10 Antiepileptics

### Convulsive status epilepticus

| Step | Drug | Dose |
|---|---|---|
| First-line (max **2** benzo doses total, incl. prehospital) | **Midazolam** | **0.15 mg/kg IV/IM/IO (max 10 mg)**; **0.3 mg/kg buccal/intranasal (max 10 mg)** |
| — alternative | Diazepam | 0.1–0.4 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg); PR 0.3–0.5 mg/kg |
| Second-line | **Levetiracetam** | **40–60 mg/kg IV (max 4.5 g)** over 5 min |
| Second-line | **Phenytoin** | **20 mg/kg IV (max 1–2 g by jurisdiction)**, rate **≤1 mg/kg/min** (max 50 mg/min) |
| Second-line | Phenobarbitone | 20 mg/kg IV (max 1 g), ≤1 mg/kg/min |
| Third-line | RSI, thiopentone/midazolam infusion, PICU | — |

Key exam points: **only two benzodiazepine doses** before moving on — further doses cause respiratory depression without terminating the seizure. Levetiracetam has largely displaced phenytoin as second-line in Australian centres ⚑ — it infuses in 5 minutes with no cardiovascular toxicity, and the ConSEPT/EcLiPSE trials showed no efficacy difference. Phenytoin must never be given rapidly (hypotension, arrhythmia) or in glucose-containing fluid (precipitation). Give **IV pyridoxine** for refractory seizures under 6 months, and always check **BGL and sodium** early.

**Maintenance therapy** is syndrome-directed: **levetiracetam** or **valproate** for generalised epilepsies; **carbamazepine, levetiracetam or lamotrigine** for focal; **ethosuximide or valproate** for childhood absence; **vigabatrin** for infantile spasms (especially with tuberous sclerosis) with mandatory visual field monitoring. **Sodium valproate must be avoided in girls of childbearing potential** wherever an alternative exists — neural tube defects, neurodevelopmental impairment. Carbamazepine and lamotrigine **worsen myoclonic and absence seizures**, so the syndrome must be right before choosing.

## 13.11 Drugs to avoid or use with caution in children

| Drug | Problem | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspirin** | **Reye syndrome** — encephalopathy + fatty liver, triggered by viral illness (influenza, varicella) | Avoid as analgesic/antipyretic **under 16**. **Exceptions: Kawasaki disease, antiplatelet indications** |
| **Codeine** (and tramadol) | CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolism → morphine surge, fatal respiratory depression | **Contraindicated <12 years**; also post-tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy for OSA <18 |
| **Tetracyclines** | Enamel hypoplasia, tooth staining | Traditionally avoid <8 years; ⚑ **short doxycycline courses now accepted at any age** where indicated |
| **Fluoroquinolones** | Animal arthropathy, tendinopathy | Not first-line; reserved indications only |
| **Promethazine** | Aggression, hallucinations, psychomotor hyperactivity; apnoea in infants | ⚑ TGA: **not to be used under 6 years** (was under 2) |
| **Metoclopramide** | Extrapyramidal reactions — oculogyric crisis, acute dystonia | Avoid <1 year, caution to 20 years; **ondansetron is the paediatric antiemetic of choice** |
| **Chloramphenicol (systemic)** | **Grey baby syndrome** — immature UGT glucuronidation + poor renal excretion → circulatory collapse, ashen cyanosis, vomiting, hypothermia | Avoid systemically in neonates; topical eye drops fine |
| **Ceftriaxone in neonates** | Bilirubin displacement (kernicterus); precipitates with calcium | Use **cefotaxime** |
| **High-dose/prolonged steroids** | Growth and adrenal suppression | Lowest effective dose; consider stress dosing |

⚑ Two threshold changes worth memorising: **promethazine moved from under-2 to under-6**, and **codeine to under-12**. Older textbooks and decks still carry the old numbers.

## 13.12 Immunisation pharmacology in brief

**Inactivated** vaccines (killed, subunit, toxoid, conjugate, polysaccharide, mRNA) cannot replicate, cannot cause the disease, and are **safe in immunosuppression** — though the *response* may be blunted, a reason to vaccinate before planned immunosuppression. Schedule examples: DTPa, IPV, hepatitis B, Hib, pneumococcal conjugate, meningococcal ACWY and B, HPV, influenza.

**Live attenuated** vaccines replicate in the host and can cause disseminated vaccine-strain infection in the significantly immunocompromised: **MMR, MMRV, varicella, rotavirus, BCG, yellow fever, oral typhoid, zoster (Zostavax)**.

Practical consequences:
- Live vaccines are **contraindicated** in significant immunocompromise — congenital immunodeficiency (rotavirus is contraindicated in SCID), malignancy on chemotherapy, transplant recipients, high-dose or prolonged systemic corticosteroids, biologic immunosuppressants. Consult the Australian Immunisation Handbook for the corticosteroid dose and duration thresholds defining "significant"; the cut-offs vary by agent and should not be quoted from memory.
- **Household contacts should be fully vaccinated, including with live vaccines** — safe, and provides cocooning. Only caveat: hand hygiene around nappies after infant rotavirus vaccine.
- Two live parenteral vaccines: **same day or ≥4 weeks apart**. Defer after immunoglobulin or blood products. Contraindicated in pregnancy.
- **Egg allergy is not a contraindication to MMR or routine influenza vaccine**; anaphylaxis to a previous dose or component is.
- Minor intercurrent illness is **not** a reason to defer.

## 13.13 Poisoning and overdose in toddlers

Toddler exploratory ingestion is typically a **single small quantity** — usually harmless, but a defined group of agents can kill a 10 kg child at **one or two tablets**. **Poisons Information Centre: 13 11 26.**

**"One pill can kill" — the classic list:**

| Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | **Calcium channel blockers** (verapamil, diltiazem — worst offenders), β-blockers, clonidine, digoxin |
| Endocrine | **Sulfonylureas** — delayed, prolonged hypoglycaemia; observe ≥12–24 h |
| Opioids | **Methadone**, buprenorphine, oxycodone, any slow-release form |
| Psychotropic | **Tricyclics** (seizures, broad-complex arrhythmia), chlorpromazine |
| Antimalarials | **Chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, quinine** |
| Other | **Camphor and essential oils**, naphthalene, theophylline, iron (deceptive latent phase), **button batteries** (same "one is enough" logic — oesophageal perforation within hours) |

### ⚑ Paracetamol overdose in children

**Threshold for assessment: acute ingestion of ≥200 mg/kg or ≥10 g, whichever is less** ⚑ — this replaced the older 150 mg/kg figure in the 2020 Australia/NZ guideline. Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion is significant at ≥200 mg/kg over 24 h, or ≥300 mg/kg over 48 h.

**Timing of the level:** **4 hours** post-ingestion for tablets; **from 2 hours** for liquids, which absorb faster and are what toddlers actually swallow. Plot on the **Rumack–Matthew nomogram** and treat if above the line. In a well child **under 6 years** after a liquid ingestion, a **2–4 hour concentration below 150 mg/L excludes toxicity**.

**Acetylcysteine — the two-bag regimen** ⚑ (replacing the three-bag 21-hour protocol, which caused more anaphylactoid reactions and dosing errors): **200 mg/kg IV over 4 hours**, then **100 mg/kg IV over 16 hours**. If the level is more than double the nomogram line, the second bag is doubled.

Children are dosed on **actual body weight**, with absolute maxima (first bag capped near 22 g). Charcoal is rarely useful under 6. Anaphylactoid reactions to acetylcysteine are **rate-related, not true allergy** — slow or pause, antihistamine, restart; do not abandon treatment.
