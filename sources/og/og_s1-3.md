# O&G Notes — Section 1: Physiology of Pregnancy and the Menstrual Cycle
*Australian finals level. Anchored to RANZCOG statements, Queensland Clinical Guidelines, Australian Pregnancy Care Guidelines, and standard physiology reference (StatPearls, GLOWM, Deranged Physiology). Numbers are consensus ranges — where sources genuinely differ this is flagged. Scope: normal physiology and the clinical traps it creates, not pathology (covered in later sections).*

## 1.1 The HPO axis

GnRH is released in **pulses** from the arcuate nucleus. The pulse *frequency* — not the amount — determines which gonadotrophin dominates: **slow pulses favour FSH, fast pulses favour LH**. Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH exposure downregulates pituitary receptors and shuts the axis down entirely — this is the mechanism behind GnRH agonists in endometriosis and fibroids, and explains the initial "flare" (1–2 weeks of stimulation) before suppression.

Ovarian steroidogenesis is the **two-cell, two-gonadotrophin** system: theca cells respond to LH and make androgens (androstenedione, testosterone); granulosa cells respond to FSH and **aromatise** those androgens to oestradiol. Granulosa cells also make **inhibin B** (follicular phase, suppresses FSH) and **inhibin A** (luteal phase), and **AMH** (from small antral follicles — hence AMH as an ovarian reserve marker, cycle-independent).

Feedback is dose- and duration-dependent, which is the single most counterintuitive point in the cycle: **oestradiol is normally inhibitory, but sustained high oestradiol (roughly >700–1100 pmol/L for ≥36–48 h) flips to positive feedback** and triggers the LH surge. Progesterone is inhibitory throughout (basis of combined and progestogen-only contraception).

**The short version**
- GnRH pulse frequency selects FSH vs LH; continuous GnRH = shutdown.
- Theca makes androgen (LH-driven); granulosa aromatises it to oestradiol (FSH-driven).
- Low/moderate E2 = negative feedback; sustained high E2 = positive feedback = LH surge.

## 1.2 The menstrual cycle

| Phase | Days (28-d cycle) | Dominant hormone | Ovary | Endometrium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Menstrual | 1–4 | all low | follicular recruitment begins | shedding of functionalis |
| Follicular / proliferative | 1–13 (**variable length**) | oestradiol (rising) | cohort recruited → dominant follicle selected ~day 7 | glands straight, stroma compact, mitoses; regenerates to 8–10 mm |
| Ovulation | ~14 | LH surge | oocyte released ~36 h after surge onset | peak thickness, triple-line on USS |
| Luteal / secretory | 15–28 (**fixed 14 ± 2 d**) | progesterone | corpus luteum | tortuous glands, subnuclear vacuoles (d16–17), stromal oedema → predecidualisation |

**The short version**
- **Follicular phase length is variable; the luteal phase is fixed at ~14 days.** All cycle-length variation is follicular. This is why a "day 21 progesterone" must actually be timed **7 days before the expected period**, not literally day 21 — in a 35-day cycle you test on day 28.
- Ovulation is **~36 h after LH surge onset, ~10–12 h after LH peak** — the basis of LH urine kits and IVF trigger timing.
- Dominant follicle selection: rising E2 suppresses FSH; only the follicle with the most FSH receptors survives the falling FSH. Give exogenous FSH and you override selection — hence multifollicular development in ovulation induction.
- Mid-luteal progesterone **>30 nmol/L** ≈ ovulation occurred.

Corpus luteum lifespan is ~14 days unless **rescued by hCG** from the implanting trophoblast (hCG binds the LH receptor). No hCG → luteolysis → progesterone withdrawal → spiral artery vasoconstriction, prostaglandin and MMP release, functionalis breakdown → menses.

**Clue → answer.** Thin, clear, stretchy cervical mucus with spinnbarkeit and ferning = **peri-ovulatory (oestrogen)**. Thick, scant, tacky mucus = **luteal (progesterone)** — and the mechanism the POP exploits. A **0.3–0.5 °C sustained basal temperature rise** = progesterone thermogenesis, i.e. ovulation has already happened (retrospective, useless for timing intercourse prospectively).

## 1.3 Fertilisation, implantation and placentation

Fertilisation occurs in the **ampulla** of the tube. Morula by day 3, blastocyst day 5, hatching from zona then **implantation days 6–10 post-ovulation**, typically upper posterior uterine wall. Syncytiotrophoblast erodes decidua and secretes **hCG**, detectable in serum ~day 8–10 post-ovulation (i.e. before a missed period).

hCG kinetics matter for early pregnancy problems: in a viable intrauterine pregnancy hCG **rises by at least ~50–66% over 48 h**, peaks at **8–10 weeks (~100,000 IU/L)**, then falls to a plateau by ~20 weeks. Suboptimal rise → ectopic or failing pregnancy; a fall of <15% in 48 h is also abnormal.

**Trophoblast invasion occurs in two waves** — decidual spiral artery segments at ~8–10 weeks, myometrial segments at ~16–18 weeks. Invasion strips the spiral arteries of smooth muscle and endothelium, converting high-resistance, vasoactive vessels into flaccid, high-capacitance conduits delivering ~500–750 mL/min at term. **Failure of the second wave is the root lesion of pre-eclampsia and placental FGR**, and is why uterine artery Doppler notching/high PI at 20–24 weeks predicts both.

**Luteoplacental shift:** the corpus luteum supplies progesterone until the placenta takes over at **7–9 weeks**. Removing a corpus luteum (e.g. ovarian cystectomy) before ~8 weeks therefore requires progesterone support; after 10 weeks it does not.

**hPL** (human placental lactogen) rises steadily through pregnancy and is powerfully **anti-insulin and lipolytic** — the reason gestational diabetes is a *second half* of pregnancy disease, and the reason insulin requirements in pre-existing T1DM climb steeply from ~20 weeks while hypoglycaemia risk is highest in the first trimester.

## 1.4 Cardiovascular adaptation

| Parameter | Change | Timing / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiac output | **↑ 30–50%** (≈5 → 7 L/min) | rises from 5 weeks, plateau by ~24 weeks |
| Stroke volume | ↑ ~25–30% | dominant early contributor |
| Heart rate | ↑ 10–20 bpm | dominant late contributor |
| SVR | **↓ 25–40%** | progesterone, NO, prostacyclin, relaxin |
| Blood pressure | ↓, **nadir 20–24 weeks**, back to baseline by term | |
| Blood volume | ↑ ~40–50% (~1.2–1.5 L) | |
| Immediate postpartum CO | **further ↑ 60–80%** | autotransfusion from contracting uterus + relief of caval compression |

**The short version**
- The BP nadir at 20–24 weeks **masks chronic hypertension**: a booking BP of 118/72 taken at 22 weeks in a woman with pre-existing hypertension looks normal. Always seek a pre-pregnancy or <20-week reading.
- **Aortocaval compression from ~20 weeks** — supine hypotension. Left lateral tilt 15–30° for resuscitation, transfer and CTG.
- The most dangerous haemodynamic moment for a woman with severe mitral stenosis or pulmonary hypertension is **immediately after delivery**, not during labour.

Physiological (do not over-investigate): ejection systolic flow murmur (up to 90%), S3, ectopics, laterally displaced apex, mild peripheral oedema, ECG left axis deviation, T-wave inversion in III/V1–V3, small Q in III. **A diastolic murmur is never physiological.**

## 1.5 Respiratory adaptation

Progesterone directly stimulates the central respiratory centre → **tidal volume ↑ 30–50%** with essentially **unchanged respiratory rate** → minute ventilation ↑ 30–50%. Mechanically, the diaphragm rises ~4 cm and the subcostal angle widens.

| Parameter | Change |
|---|---|
| Tidal volume | ↑ 30–50% |
| Minute ventilation | ↑ 30–50% |
| Respiratory rate | unchanged |
| FRC, RV, ERV | **↓ ~20–25%** |
| VC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC | **unchanged** |
| O₂ consumption | ↑ 20–30% |
| PaCO₂ | **↓ to ~28–32 mmHg** |
| HCO₃⁻ | ↓ to ~18–22 mmol/L (renal compensation) |
| pH | 7.40–7.47 (mild compensated respiratory alkalosis) |

**The short version**
- **A PaCO₂ of 40 mmHg in a breathless pregnant asthmatic is not reassuring — it is impending respiratory failure.** Same logic as a "normal" bicarbonate in DKA.
- **↑ O₂ consumption + ↓ FRC = desaturation within seconds of apnoea** — the reason pregnant intubation is a high-risk airway and pre-oxygenation is non-negotiable.
- **Spirometry does not change in pregnancy.** An abnormal FEV1/FVC is never "just pregnancy."
- Physiological dyspnoea affects 60–70%, starts in the first/second trimester and is not progressive on exertion. Red flags: orthopnoea, PND, sudden onset, hypoxia, chest pain.

## 1.6 Haematological adaptation

**Plasma volume ↑ 40–50% but red cell mass only ↑ 20–30%** → dilutional ("physiological") anaemia. The critical exam point is timing: **plasma expansion accelerates from the second trimester and the Hb nadir is 28–32 weeks — NOT 12 weeks.** A woman with Hb 98 g/L at 11 weeks does not have physiological anaemia; she has pathology (usually iron deficiency, sometimes haemoglobinopathy) and needs a ferritin and a film.

| Threshold | Value |
|---|---|
| Anaemia, 1st trimester | Hb **<110 g/L** |
| Anaemia, 2nd trimester | Hb **<105 g/L** |
| Anaemia, 3rd trimester | **sources differ: <105 (SA Health) vs <110 g/L (KEMH/WA)** — see note |
| Anaemia, postpartum | Hb **<100 g/L** |
| Iron deficiency in pregnancy | ferritin **<30 µg/L** (vs <15 non-pregnant) |
| Total iron requirement over pregnancy | ~1000 mg |

⚑ **Source disagreement:** third-trimester Hb cut-off. SA Health applies <105 g/L to both second and third trimesters; KEMH (WA) reverts to <110 g/L in the third trimester (matching the CDC pattern of 110/105/110). Either is defensible in a written answer — state <110 first trimester, <105 second, and note the third-trimester figure varies 105–110 by jurisdiction. The ferritin <30 µg/L cut-off is uniform: **ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, so the non-pregnant <15 threshold under-diagnoses in pregnancy.**

Other haematological shifts:
- **Gestational thrombocytopenia** — 5–10% of pregnancies, third trimester, usually **>70–80 ×10⁹/L**, asymptomatic, resolves postpartum. **Discriminator:** platelets <70, onset before the third trimester, or any other cytopenia/organ dysfunction → think ITP, pre-eclampsia/HELLP, TTP, APS.
- **Hypercoagulability** — fibrinogen roughly doubles (4–6 g/L), factors VII, VIII, IX, X, XII and vWF rise, **protein S falls**, acquired activated protein C resistance develops, and fibrinolysis is suppressed (PAI-1, plus placental PAI-2). Net VTE risk ↑ **4–6×**, and is **highest postpartum**, peaking in the first week and elevated for 6 weeks.
- **D-dimer rises physiologically** → useless as a rule-out in pregnancy. Suspected PE goes straight to imaging (CXR then V/Q or CTPA), not to a D-dimer.
- **WCC rises** (up to ~15 ×10⁹/L antenatally, 25–30 ×10⁹/L in labour) and **ESR rises** (fibrinogen). Use **CRP** for infection.

## 1.7 Renal adaptation

- **RPF ↑ 60–80%, GFR ↑ 40–50%**, both established by the end of the first trimester.
- **Creatinine falls to ~35–70 µmol/L**; urea falls. ⚑ **A creatinine of 90 µmol/L in pregnancy is significant renal impairment** even though most lab reference ranges call it normal — pregnancy-specific ranges are essential.
- Urate falls early, then rises with gestation; used as an adjunct marker in pre-eclampsia.
- **Physiological hydronephrosis**, right > left in 80–90% (progesterone-mediated ureteric relaxation plus dextrorotation of the uterus compressing the right ureter over the iliac vessels). Right loin pain in pregnancy is therefore common and non-specific.
- **Glycosuria is common** (reduced tubular reabsorption threshold) and **must not be used to screen for GDM**.
- Proteinuria up to 300 mg/24 h is normal; **PCR ≥30 mg/mmol** = significant.
- Osmostat resets down ~10 mOsm/kg → serum Na runs ~4–5 mmol/L lower.
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** affects 2–7%; untreated, **20–30% progress to pyelonephritis**, with associated preterm birth and low birthweight. This is why urine MCS is a booking test and why treatment is mandatory in pregnancy but not in non-pregnant adults.

## 1.8 GI, hepatic and other adaptation

Progesterone reduces lower oesophageal sphincter tone and slows gastric emptying and gut transit; the enlarging uterus adds mechanical displacement. Result: **reflux in up to 80% by the third trimester**, constipation, and — critically for anaesthesia — a **"full stomach" assumption from ~16–20 weeks**, mandating rapid sequence induction and antacid prophylaxis.

Liver biochemistry traps:
- **ALP rises 2–4×** because of the **placental isoenzyme**. A raised ALP in pregnancy means nothing on its own.
- **ALT, AST, GGT and bilirubin should remain within (or below) non-pregnant limits.** Any rise is pathological — think obstetric cholestasis, pre-eclampsia/HELLP, AFLP, viral hepatitis.
- **Albumin falls** by dilution → **total calcium falls but ionised calcium is unchanged**. Correct before calling hypocalcaemia.
- Gallbladder contractility falls and bile becomes more cholesterol-saturated → gallstones.

Thyroid: **hCG is a weak TSH-receptor agonist** (shared α-subunit), so **TSH dips in the first trimester**, sometimes below the non-pregnant lower limit. Oestrogen raises TBG so **total T4/T3 rise while free T4 is roughly unchanged**. Iodine requirement rises (hence 150 µg/day supplementation). **Clue → answer:** hyperemesis + suppressed TSH + raised fT4, no goitre, no eye signs, negative TRAb, settling by 20 weeks = **gestational transient thyrotoxicosis**, not Graves — treat supportively, not with carbimazole.

Other: cortisol and CBG rise (Cushing's is hard to diagnose in pregnancy); prolactin rises 10–20×; relaxin and progesterone cause ligamentous laxity, lumbar lordosis and symphysis pubis dysfunction; a **Th2 shift with reduced cell-mediated immunity** makes listeria, TB, varicella, influenza and malaria more severe (RA often remits, SLE often flares).

# O&G Notes — Section 2: Anatomy

*Written as prose and tables — deliberately image-independent. Aligned to RANZCOG and the ACSQHC Third and Fourth Degree Perineal Tears Clinical Care Standard for tear classification; SA Health PPG for repair detail. Scope: what is examinable in a written paper — relations, supply, drainage, diameters and the clinical consequences of each.*

## 2.1 The bony pelvis

The **true pelvis** (below the pelvic brim) is the birth canal. Its three planes have different shapes, and this is the whole explanation for the mechanism of labour.

| Plane | AP diameter | Transverse diameter | Widest axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inlet (brim) | true conjugate ~11 cm; **obstetric conjugate ~10.5 cm** (sacral promontory → nearest/posterior point of symphysis — the true limiting diameter); diagonal conjugate ~12.5 cm (**the only one clinically measurable**, promontory → inferior symphysis) | ~13 cm | **Transverse** |
| Mid-cavity | ~12 cm | ~12 cm (interspinous ~10.5 cm — **narrowest pelvic diameter overall**) | round |
| Outlet | ~12.5–13 cm | intertuberous ~11 cm | **AP** |

**Memory hook:** widest **transverse at the inlet**, widest **AP at the outlet**. The fetal head therefore **engages in the transverse, then rotates to occipito-anterior** to deliver — the rotation exists because the pelvis changes shape, not because the head "prefers" it.

The **ischial spines** are the reference point for everything: station 0 by definition, the narrowest interspinous diameter, the landmark for pudendal block, and the attachment of the sacrospinous ligament (used in sacrospinous fixation for vault prolapse).

| Pelvis type | Inlet shape | Obstetric relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Gynaecoid** (~50%) | round/slightly oval transversely | most favourable |
| Android | heart-shaped, narrow forepelvis, prominent spines | deep transverse arrest, OP position, arrest of descent |
| Anthropoid | oval AP > transverse | favours **direct OA or OP** delivery |
| Platypelloid | flat, wide transverse, short AP | head engages transverse, **fails to rotate** |

**The short version**
- Obstetric conjugate is the true limiting inlet diameter; diagonal conjugate is the one your finger can reach.
- Interspinous distance is the narrowest diameter in the entire pelvis.
- Inlet transverse → outlet AP explains internal rotation.

## 2.2 The pelvic floor

The **pelvic diaphragm** = **levator ani** (pubococcygeus, puborectalis, iliococcygeus) + **coccygeus**, slung from the tendinous arch of the obturator fascia. Innervation: direct branches from **S3–S5 (nerve to levator ani)** with a variable contribution from the pudendal nerve.

**Puborectalis** forms the sling around the anorectal junction and maintains the anorectal angle — the principal mechanism of faecal continence, more so than the external anal sphincter at rest. Avulsion of the puborectalis from the pubic ramus occurs in ~10–15% of first vaginal births (higher with forceps) and is a major driver of later prolapse.

Below the levators, the **perineal membrane** separates the **deep perineal pouch** (external urethral sphincter, compressor urethrae, deep transverse perineal muscle) from the **superficial perineal pouch** (bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, superficial transverse perineal muscle, vestibular bulbs, **Bartholin's [greater vestibular] glands** — which drain at 4 and 8 o'clock in the vestibule, hence the classic posterolateral Bartholin's cyst).

The **perineal body** is the fibromuscular central tendon between vagina and anus, into which bulbospongiosus, superficial and deep transverse perineal muscles, the external anal sphincter and the levators all insert. **It is the structure destroyed in a second-degree tear or episiotomy, and its integrity underpins both posterior compartment support and anal continence.**

**Uterine support** — the discriminator that matters:

| Ligament | Composition | Does it actually support? |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardinal (transverse cervical, Mackenrodt's)** | fibromuscular, cervix/upper vagina → lateral pelvic wall; **carries the uterine vessels** | **Yes — principal lateral support** |
| **Uterosacral** | cervix → sacrum | **Yes — principal posterior support**; the structure plicated in prolapse surgery |
| Pubocervical | cervix → pubis | Yes, anterior (weak) |
| Round | cornu → inguinal canal → labium majus (contains artery of Sampson) | **No** — maintains anteversion only; source of round ligament pain |
| Broad | peritoneal fold | **No** — a mesentery, not a ligament |
| Suspensory (infundibulopelvic) | ovary → pelvic sidewall; **contains ovarian vessels** | supports ovary; **the pedicle clamped in oophorectomy — the ureter runs close beneath it** |
| Ovarian ligament | ovary → cornu (gubernaculum remnant) | no |

## 2.3 The genital tract

Uterus: fundus, body, isthmus (becomes the lower segment in pregnancy — the site of LSCS incision), cervix. Layers: endometrium (functionalis shed, basalis regenerates), myometrium, perimetrium. Normally **anteverted and anteflexed** (~80%); retroversion is a normal variant but relevant to incarceration of a retroverted gravid uterus at 12–16 weeks.

Cervix: endocervix lined by columnar epithelium, ectocervix by stratified squamous. The **squamocolumnar junction** everts onto the ectocervix under oestrogen (puberty, pregnancy, COCP) and the exposed columnar epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia — the **transformation zone**. This is where HPV-driven dysplasia arises and therefore what a cervical screening sample and colposcopy must sample. It regresses into the canal after menopause, which is why colposcopy is more often unsatisfactory in older women.

Fallopian tube: interstitial (intramural) → isthmus → **ampulla** (fertilisation site; **commonest site of ectopic, ~70%**) → infundibulum/fimbriae. **Interstitial (cornual) ectopics present later (~8–12 weeks) and bleed catastrophically** because of the surrounding myometrial vascularity.

Vagina: stratified squamous, no glands; lubricated by transudate and cervical mucus. Upper third from Müllerian duct, lower part from urogenital sinus — the embryological seam that explains the **watershed at the hymen** in both lymphatic drainage and nerve supply.

**Key surgical relation — "water under the bridge":** the **ureter passes beneath the uterine artery** approximately **1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix at the level of the internal os**, within the cardinal ligament. This is the site of ureteric injury at hysterectomy and at uterine artery ligation for PPH. The ureter is also at risk at the pelvic brim (crossing the bifurcation of the common iliac) and where it runs beneath the infundibulopelvic ligament.

## 2.4 Blood supply

| Structure | Arterial supply | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| Ovary (and lateral tube) | ovarian artery | **abdominal aorta, ~L2** (below the renal arteries) |
| Uterus, medial tube, upper vagina | uterine artery | **anterior division of internal iliac** |
| Vagina (mid) | vaginal artery | internal iliac (or uterine) |
| Vulva, perineum, lower vagina | internal pudendal artery | anterior division of internal iliac |
| Lower vagina/perineum (accessory) | inferior rectal, posterior labial | internal pudendal |

The uterine and ovarian arteries **anastomose freely within the broad ligament** — which is why unilateral internal iliac ligation rarely produces uterine necrosis, and why uterine artery embolisation for fibroids can still fail via ovarian collaterals.

Venous drainage is the classic asymmetry: **right ovarian vein → IVC; left ovarian vein → left renal vein.** This underlies the left-sided predominance of pelvic congestion syndrome and the right-sided predominance of postpartum ovarian vein thrombosis (right vein is longer, crosses the ureter, and drains directly to IVC).

## 2.5 Lymphatic drainage

Lymphatics follow arteries, so **ovarian drainage is high (para-aortic), not pelvic** — which is why ovarian cancer staging requires para-aortic nodal assessment and why an ovarian cancer can have para-aortic nodes with clean pelvic nodes.

| Structure | Primary nodes |
|---|---|
| **Ovary, fallopian tube (lateral)** | **para-aortic** (follow ovarian vessels to L2) |
| Uterine fundus | para-aortic, plus a minor route **along the round ligament to superficial inguinal** |
| Uterine body | external iliac |
| **Cervix** | **obturator, internal and external iliac, then common iliac and para-aortic**; sacral |
| Upper ⅔ vagina | internal and external iliac |
| **Lower ⅓ vagina (below hymen), vulva, perineum** | **superficial inguinal → deep inguinal (node of Cloquet) → external iliac** |

**Clue → answer:** an inguinal node metastasis from a gynaecological primary means the tumour is **at or below the hymen** — vulva, lower vagina, or (rarely, via the round ligament) fundal.

## 2.6 Nerve supply and the anatomy of labour analgesia

| Structure | Segmental supply | Pain in labour |
|---|---|---|
| Uterine body, cervix (stretch) | **T10–L1** sympathetic via inferior hypogastric plexus | **first stage** — visceral, poorly localised, referred to back/lower abdomen |
| Cervix, upper vagina | S2–S4 parasympathetic (pelvic splanchnic) | |
| Lower vagina, vulva, perineum | **pudendal nerve, S2–S4** | **second stage** — somatic, sharp, well localised |
| Anterior vulva/mons | ilioinguinal, genitofemoral (L1) | not covered by pudendal block |

Consequences: an **epidural must cover T10–L1 for the first stage and extend to S2–S4 for the second stage**; a **pudendal block (needle guided to the ischial spine, injecting medial and inferior to it, targeting the nerve as it re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen) covers only the perineum** — adequate for outlet forceps or perineal repair, useless for uterine pain, and it will **miss the anterior vulva** (L1 territory), which needs local infiltration.

## 2.7 The fetal skull

Bones: 2 frontal, 2 parietal, 2 temporal, 1 occipital. Sutures: sagittal, coronal, lambdoid, frontal (metopic). Fontanelles: **anterior (bregma)** — diamond-shaped, 4 sutures, closes 18–24 months; **posterior (lambda)** — triangular, 3 sutures, closes ~6–8 weeks. **On vaginal examination, feeling a triangular fontanelle in the anterior pelvis = occiput anterior; feeling a diamond fontanelle anteriorly = occiput posterior or deflexed.**

Landmarks: occiput, vertex (between fontanelles, bounded by parietal eminences), sinciput/brow, bregma, mentum (chin), glabella.

| Diameter | Measurement | Attitude / presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **Suboccipito-bregmatic (SOB)** | **9.5 cm** | **fully flexed vertex — the optimal diameter** |
| Suboccipito-frontal | 10.0–10.5 cm | partially deflexed vertex |
| Occipito-frontal (OF) | **11.5 cm** | deflexed / "military" — the OP diameter |
| **Mento-vertical (MV)** | **13.5 cm** | **brow — largest, will not deliver vaginally at term** |
| Submento-vertical | 11.5 cm | partially extended face |
| **Submento-bregmatic (SMB)** | **9.5 cm** | **fully extended face — mento-anterior can deliver vaginally** |
| Biparietal (BPD) | 9.5 cm | transverse, all vertex presentations |
| Bitemporal | 8.0–8.2 cm | transverse, smallest |

**Memory hook:** the head presents its smallest diameter when it is either **fully flexed (SOB 9.5)** or **fully extended (SMB 9.5)**. Everything in between is worse, and the halfway point — **brow, MV 13.5 cm — is the worst possible attitude**. Hence: **face mento-anterior may deliver vaginally; face mento-posterior and brow may not** (mento-posterior requires the neck to extend further than anatomically possible).

**Moulding** (overlapping at sutures) and **caput succedaneum** (diffuse subcutaneous oedema, **crosses suture lines**, present at birth, resolves in days) are physiological. Discriminate from **cephalhaematoma** (subperiosteal, **does not cross sutures**, appears hours after birth, jaundice risk) and **subgaleal haemorrhage** (boggy, crosses sutures, shifts with position, **can exsanguinate — associated with ventouse**).

## 2.8 The perineum and classification of tears

The perineum is the diamond between the pubic symphysis, ischial tuberosities and coccyx, divided by an intertuberous line into the **urogenital triangle** (anterior) and **anal triangle** (posterior, containing the ischiorectal fossae and the pudendal canal of Alcock on the lateral wall of each).

**Classification (ACSQHC Clinical Care Standard / RANZCOG, adopting the RCOG–Sultan system):**

| Degree | Definition |
|---|---|
| **First** | perineal or vaginal **skin/mucosa only** |
| **Second** | into the **perineal muscles**, anal sphincter intact (**an episiotomy is by definition a second-degree injury**) |
| **3a** | **<50%** of external anal sphincter (EAS) thickness torn |
| **3b** | **>50%** of EAS thickness torn |
| **3c** | **EAS and internal anal sphincter (IAS)** both torn |
| **Fourth** | EAS + IAS + **anal epithelium** breached |

⚑ Older texts describe "third degree = any sphincter involvement" without subclassification; **the 3a/3b/3c split is now standard and must be documented**, because it determines repair technique and prognosis. Third- and fourth-degree tears together are termed **OASIS** (obstetric anal sphincter injuries).

**Every woman after vaginal birth requires a systematic perineal and digital rectal examination before repair** — the standard exists because 3a and IAS injuries are otherwise missed, and missed OASIS is the leading preventable cause of later faecal incontinence.

Risk factors: **first vaginal birth, instrumental delivery (forceps > ventouse), midline episiotomy, birthweight >4 kg, prolonged second stage, OP position, shoulder dystocia, induction, epidural.** A **mediolateral episiotomy cut at 60° from the midline at crowning** reduces OASIS risk; **midline episiotomy increases it** by directing extension into the sphincter.

Repair principles: theatre, regional or general anaesthesia, good lighting and assistance. **IAS — end-to-end with interrupted 3-0 PDS; EAS — overlapping repair preferred for full-thickness (3b/3c/4th), end-to-end mandatory for 3a (there is insufficient torn tissue to overlap), using 2-0 PDS with buried knots.** Post-repair: broad-spectrum antibiotics (IV cephazolin + metronidazole, then oral amoxicillin–clavulanate for 5 days; clindamycin ± gentamicin if penicillin-anaphylactic), **laxatives and bulking agents for ~2 weeks** (constipation disrupts the repair), physiotherapy, and follow-up with consideration of endoanal ultrasound and manometry before a subsequent birth.

# O&G Notes — Section 3: Antenatal Care and Screening

*Australian practice. Anchored to the Australian Pregnancy Care Guidelines, RANZCOG statements, RACGP Red Book (first antenatal visit), National Blood Authority anti-D guideline (2024), Australian Immunisation Handbook, ADIPS 2025 consensus and Queensland Clinical Guidelines. Where Australian practice differs from UK/NICE, this is stated. ⚑ marks a change from older textbooks.*

## 3.1 The booking visit

Ideally **within the first 10 weeks**. Purpose is risk stratification, dating, screening and shared decision-making about model of care.

**Booking bloods (routine):** FBC; **blood group and antibody screen**; rubella IgG; syphilis serology; HBsAg; **hepatitis C antibody**; HIV. **Ferritin** is increasingly routine (risk-based in older guidance, universal in many Australian services). Risk-based additions: haemoglobin electrophoresis/HPLC (ethnicity, low MCV/MCH, family history), TSH (thyroid disease, T1DM, symptoms — **not universal**), vitamin D (dark skin, veiling, low sun exposure, BMI >30).

**Other booking tests:** **urine MCS for asymptomatic bacteriuria** (culture, not dipstick — dipstick is insufficiently sensitive); **chlamydia NAAT if aged <30 or with risk factors** (⚑ this is an Australian point — the UK does not routinely screen for chlamydia in pregnancy); cervical screening test if due (safe in pregnancy).

**Clinical assessment:** height, weight, BMI; BP; **EPDS depression screen (and repeat at least once later in pregnancy)**; domestic/family violence enquiry; smoking, alcohol and other drug use; medication review for teratogens; **pre-eclampsia risk assessment**; **GDM risk assessment**.

Acting on the risk assessment: high pre-eclampsia risk → **low-dose aspirin 100–150 mg nocte, commenced between 12 and 16 weeks** and continued to ~36 weeks, plus calcium supplementation if dietary intake is low.

**Dating.** Use LMP only if cycles are regular and dates certain. Otherwise, or if there is discrepancy: **CRL is the most accurate parameter between 7 and 13+6 weeks (±5 days)**; after 14 weeks use HC/BPD/FL (±7–10 days, degrading with gestation). ⚑ **Ultrasound dating overrides LMP if the discrepancy exceeds ~5–7 days in the first trimester.** Never re-date on a third-trimester scan — a small fetus late in pregnancy is FGR until proven otherwise, not a wrong date.

**The short version**
- Booking by 10 weeks; dating by CRL if <14 weeks.
- Urine **culture** for asymptomatic bacteriuria — untreated it causes pyelonephritis and preterm birth.
- Aspirin decision is made at booking and started 12–16 weeks.

## 3.2 Visit schedule

RANZCOG accepts **~10 visits for an uncomplicated nulliparous woman and ~7 for a multiparous woman**; exact gestations vary between states and models of care.

| Gestation | Content |
|---|---|
| **<10 weeks (booking)** | history, exam, booking bloods, urine MCS, EPDS, risk assessment, dating scan if needed |
| 11+0–13+6 | **cFTS** (NT + serum) if chosen; NIPT can be done from 10 weeks |
| 16 | review screening results, BP, urine; commence aspirin by now if indicated |
| **18–22 (usually 19–20)** | **morphology scan** ± cervical length, placental site |
| 24 | BP, urine, **SFH from 24 weeks onwards** |
| **24–28** | **75 g OGTT for GDM** |
| **28** | FBC (± ferritin), repeat antibody screen, **anti-D dose 1 (625 IU)** |
| 31 (nullip) | BP, urine, SFH |
| **34** | **anti-D dose 2 (625 IU)** |
| **35–37** | **GBS swab** (if universal screening used); repeat depression screen |
| 36 | **confirm presentation** (palpation ± USS); discuss ECV if breech |
| 38, 40, 41 | BP/urine/SFH; **discuss IOL at 41 weeks**, membrane sweep |

BP, urinalysis and SFH at every visit from 24 weeks; fetal heart auscultation from ~24 weeks (of reassurance value only, not diagnostic).

## 3.3 RhD status and anti-D prophylaxis

Alloimmunisation occurs when an RhD-negative woman is exposed to RhD-positive fetal cells; the resulting IgG anti-D crosses the placenta in *subsequent* pregnancies and causes haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn.

**Australian regimen (National Blood Authority, 2024):**

| Situation | Dose |
|---|---|
| **Routine antenatal prophylaxis** | **625 IU IM at 28 weeks AND 625 IU IM at 34 weeks** |
| **Sensitising event ≤12+6 weeks** | **250 IU IM** |
| **Sensitising event ≥13+0 weeks** | **625 IU IM** |
| **Postnatal (baby confirmed RhD positive)** | **625 IU IM** |
| Large FMH ≥6 mL fetal red cells | 625 IU **plus 100 IU per additional mL** of fetal red cells beyond 6 mL |

Timing: **give within 72 hours**; if missed, still give up to **10 days** (partial protection). Routine doses may be given within ~2 weeks of the scheduled gestation if logistics require.

**Sensitising events:** miscarriage or threatened miscarriage (with heavy/repeated bleeding or pain), termination, ectopic, molar pregnancy, CVS/amniocentesis/fetal blood sampling, **abdominal trauma**, **antepartum haemorrhage**, **external cephalic version**, intrauterine death, and delivery itself.

**Postnatal FMH quantitation (Kleihauer or flow cytometry) is required for every RhD-negative woman delivering an RhD-positive baby**, to identify the large bleeds needing supplementary dosing. Repeat testing 48 h after IV or 72 h after IM administration when a large FMH has been treated.

⚑ **RhD NIPT (cell-free fetal DNA fetal blood group genotyping)** is now available in Australia through Lifeblood **from 15 weeks**, **MBS-funded/bulk-billed** for non-alloimmunised RhD-negative women (not validated in multiple pregnancy). **If the fetus is predicted RhD negative, antenatal anti-D is not indicated.** This is a genuine change from the universal-prophylaxis model in older texts.

⚑ **Anti-D does not treat established alloimmunisation.** If the antibody screen already shows anti-D, giving more is pointless — refer to MFM for antibody titres/quantitation and **MCA peak systolic velocity Dopplers (>1.5 MoM = significant fetal anaemia → consider intrauterine transfusion)**.

**Australia vs UK:** Australia uses **two doses of 625 IU at 28 and 34 weeks**. The UK (NICE) offers either **a single 1500 IU dose at 28 weeks** or **two 500 IU doses at 28 and 34 weeks**. Quoting the UK figures in an Australian paper is a marked error.

## 3.4 Aneuploidy and structural screening

Screening is **opt-in after counselling**, not automatic. All screening results are risks, not diagnoses.

| Test | Timing | Components | Performance for T21 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cFTS** | **NT at 11+0–13+6 (CRL 45–84 mm)**; serum from 9–13+6 | NT + free β-hCG + PAPP-A + maternal age | DR **~85–90%**, FPR ~5% |
| **NIPT (cfDNA)** | **from 10 weeks** | placental cfDNA fraction | DR **>99%**, FPR **<0.1%** |
| Second trimester serum (quad) | 15–20 weeks | AFP, hCG, uE3, inhibin A | DR ~75–80% |
| **Morphology scan** | **18–22 weeks** (commonly 19–20 in Australia) | structural survey, placental site, ± cervical length | structural anomalies |

**The short version**
- **NIPT is a screening test, not diagnostic** — every high-risk result needs confirmation by CVS or amniocentesis. Its **PPV depends on prior risk**: in a 25-year-old, PPV for T21 may be ~50–70% and for rare trisomies/microdeletions considerably lower.
- ⚑ **NIPT is not MBS-funded in Australia** (typically $400–500 out of pocket), unlike the separately funded RhD NIPT. cFTS is partly rebatable. This cost difference drives real inequity in access and is worth stating in a short-answer response.
- **Failed/"no-call" NIPT (1–5%)** is usually **low fetal fraction**: high maternal BMI, too-early gestation, twin/vanishing twin, some anticoagulants, and — importantly — **aneuploid placentas (T13, T18, triploidy)**. A no-call is itself a mild risk marker; repeat or offer diagnostic testing rather than reassure.

**Diagnostic testing:** **CVS from 11–14 weeks** (never before 10 weeks — earlier sampling is linked to limb-reduction defects), **amniocentesis from 15 weeks**. ⚑ **Procedure-related miscarriage risk in contemporary series is ~0.1–0.2% above background, not the 1% (amniocentesis) or 1–2% (CVS) quoted in older textbooks.** CVS carries a 1–2% chance of **confined placental mosaicism**, which amniocentesis does not.

**Increased NT ≥3.5 mm** with a normal karyotype/NIPT still warrants **detailed anatomy scan, fetal echocardiography and chromosomal microarray** — the association is with cardiac defects and genetic syndromes, not only aneuploidy. ⚑ **Chromosomal microarray has replaced karyotype as first-line** when a structural anomaly is present, because it detects copy-number variants that karyotype misses.

Rh-negative women undergoing CVS or amniocentesis require anti-D (dose by gestation as above).

## 3.5 Gestational diabetes screening ⚑

⚑ **The ADIPS 2025 consensus raised all three diagnostic thresholds.** Australian laboratories transitioned during 2025 (Victoria state-wide from 4 August 2025). Older notes quoting 5.1/10.0/8.5 are now out of date.

| 75 g OGTT at 24–28 weeks | **ADIPS 2025** | ADIPS 2014 (superseded) |
|---|---|---|
| Fasting | **≥5.3 mmol/L** | ≥5.1 |
| 1 hour | **≥10.6 mmol/L** | ≥10.0 |
| 2 hour | **≥9.0 mmol/L** | ≥8.5 |

One abnormal value is sufficient. Values above ~7.0 fasting / 11.1 at 2 h suggest **overt diabetes in pregnancy**, not GDM.

Early testing: risk factors (prior GDM, BMI ≥30, age ≥30, family history, PCOS, high-risk ethnicity, previous macrosomia) → **first-trimester HbA1c**; **prior GDM or HbA1c 6.0–6.4% → OGTT before 20 weeks, ideally 10–14 weeks**. Treatment targets: **fasting <5.3, 1 h post-prandial <7.8, or 2 h post-prandial <6.7 mmol/L**.

**Do not screen with urinalysis** — glycosuria is physiological (Section 1.7).

## 3.6 Group B streptococcus

**Australia has no national consensus.** RANZCOG accepts **either** universal culture-based screening **or** a risk-factor-based approach, and services follow local policy. ⚑ Contrast: **the US (CDC/ACOG) mandates universal screening; the UK (RCOG) and New Zealand use risk-based.** Saying "universal screening is standard" is only half-right in an Australian answer.

- **Swab at 35–37 weeks**: a single swab taken **low vaginal then anorectal** (introitus first, then anus), into standard transport medium.
- **Indications for intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP):** positive GBS swab; **GBS bacteriuria at any point in this pregnancy**; **previous infant with invasive GBS disease** (both of the latter override swab results); preterm labour <37 weeks; **ROM ≥18 hours**; intrapartum fever ≥38 °C.
- **Regimen:** **benzylpenicillin 3 g IV loading dose, then 1.8 g IV 4-hourly** until birth. Non-severe penicillin allergy: **cephazolin 2 g then 1 g 8-hourly**. Anaphylaxis: **clindamycin 900 mg 8-hourly** (or vancomycin if resistance unknown).
- **"Adequate" prophylaxis = commenced ≥4 hours before birth** — the figure that determines neonatal observation and workup.
- **Elective caesarean with intact membranes and not in labour does not require GBS IAP** (surgical prophylaxis is a separate indication).

## 3.7 Vaccination in pregnancy

| Vaccine | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Influenza (inactivated)** | **any trimester, every pregnancy** | NIP-funded; reduces stillbirth, preterm birth and severe maternal illness |
| **dTpa** | **20–32 weeks, every pregnancy** | ⚑ Australian window is **20–32 weeks** (UK: 16–32). Timing optimises transplacental IgG transfer to protect the infant until their 6-week dose |
| **RSV (RSVpreF, Abrysvo)** | ⚑ **28–36 weeks**, single dose **each pregnancy** | ⚑ **New — NIP-funded from 2025.** Registered from 24 weeks but **not routinely recommended before 28 weeks**. ~70% reduction in severe infant RSV disease. Alternative infant pathway: nirsevimab |
| **COVID-19 (mRNA)** | any trimester | |
| Others as indicated | any trimester | hepatitis A/B, pneumococcal, meningococcal, tetanus, rabies post-exposure — all inactivated, all safe |

**Contraindicated — live attenuated vaccines:** **MMR, varicella, zoster (live formulation), BCG, live oral typhoid, yellow fever** (relative — may be given if travel is unavoidable and risk is high).

- Advise avoiding pregnancy for **28 days** after MMR or varicella.
- **Inadvertent administration of a live vaccine in pregnancy is NOT an indication for termination** — actual fetal harm has not been demonstrated. This is a favourite exam point.
- **Non-immune to rubella or varicella → vaccinate postpartum before discharge.** Live vaccines are safe while breastfeeding.

## 3.8 Nutrition and supplementation

| Nutrient | Dose | Timing / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Folic acid** | **400–500 µg/day** | from **≥1 month preconception to 12 weeks** |
| **High-dose folic acid** | **5 mg/day** | previous NTD-affected pregnancy, family history of NTD, **pre-existing diabetes**, **BMI >30**, antiepileptics (valproate, carbamazepine), other folate antagonists (methotrexate, trimethoprim, sulfasalazine), malabsorption/bariatric surgery |
| **Iodine** | **150 µg/day** | preconception, pregnancy and lactation. Australia has mandatory iodine fortification of bread, but supplementation is still recommended. Seek advice first in known thyroid disease |
| **Vitamin B12** | **2.6 µg/day** (2.8 lactation) | ⚑ **essential in vegans/strict vegetarians** |
| Vitamin D | 1000 IU if 25(OH)D 30–49 nmol/L; **2000 IU if <30 nmol/L** | risk-based testing; recheck at 28 weeks |
| Calcium | 1000 mg/day (1300 mg if ≤18 y) | supplement reduces pre-eclampsia risk where intake is low |
| Iron | **not routine** | treat if Hb below threshold or **ferritin <30 µg/L**; ~100 mg elemental daily. ⚑ **alternate-day dosing improves absorption** (hepcidin kinetics). IV iron generally avoided in the first trimester |

**Clue → answer:** an exclusively breastfed infant of a **vegan mother** with developmental regression, hypotonia and macrocytic anaemia = **maternal/infant B12 deficiency**. This is the classic reason B12 is asked about, and why B12 status must be checked and supplemented in vegan pregnancies rather than assumed adequate.

**Food and lifestyle:** avoid listeria risks (soft/unpasteurised cheese, pre-prepared salads, deli meats, pâté, cold smoked seafood); avoid high-mercury fish (**shark/flake, swordfish, broadbill, marlin — no more than one serve per fortnight**, FSANZ); **no safe level of alcohol (NHMRC — abstinence)**; caffeine **≤200 mg/day**. Recommended gestational weight gain (IOM ranges): underweight 12.5–18 kg; normal BMI 11.5–16 kg; overweight 7–11.5 kg; obese 5–9 kg.

## 3.9 Fetal growth surveillance

**Symphysis-fundal height** from **24 weeks** at every visit, measured in cm with the tape reversed to blind the measurer, and **plotted on a customised (e.g. GROW) chart** rather than judged against "cm ≈ weeks ± 2–3". Sensitivity for SGA is poor (~30% alone) — it is a trigger for imaging, not a test.

**Serial growth ultrasound** (typically 4-weekly from 26–28 weeks) is indicated for: previous SGA baby or stillbirth, chronic hypertension or pre-eclampsia, **pre-gestational diabetes**, smoking, maternal age >40, BMI extremes, **low PAPP-A (<0.4 MoM)**, abnormal uterine artery Dopplers, APH, multiple pregnancy, renal disease, SLE/APS. Also whenever SFH plots off-centile or palpation is unreliable (obesity, fibroids, polyhydramnios).

Definitions and surveillance:
- **SGA** = EFW or AC **<10th customised centile**. **FGR** = **<3rd centile**, or <10th centile **with abnormal Dopplers or poor interval growth**.
- ⚑ **Umbilical artery Doppler is the only surveillance tool proven to reduce perinatal mortality in high-risk pregnancies** — but it is **useless as a screening test in low-risk women.** Deterioration sequence: raised PI → **absent end-diastolic flow → reversed end-diastolic flow**.
- **MCA Doppler / cerebroplacental ratio** detects "brain sparing" and is more useful in **late-onset** FGR, where umbilical artery Dopplers often remain normal.
- **Ductus venosus a-wave loss or reversal** is a late sign of cardiac decompensation in **early-onset** FGR and, with the TRUFFLE approach, guides timing of delivery before 32 weeks.
- **Reduced fetal movements** → same-day assessment: CTG plus ultrasound for growth and liquor. ⚑ **Formal kick-counting charts are no longer recommended** — the AFFIRM trial showed no reduction in stillbirth and increased obstetric intervention. Advise awareness of *change*, not counting.
- **Routine third-trimester ultrasound in low-risk pregnancy is not recommended** by RANZCOG; some Australian units nevertheless offer a ~36-week scan for presentation and growth, and practice varies.
