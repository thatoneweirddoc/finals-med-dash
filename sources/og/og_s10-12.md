# O&G Notes — Section 10: Gynaecological Oncology and Cervical Screening
*Anchored to the National Cervical Screening Program (NCSP) National Guidelines (Cancer Council Australia, April 2025 revision), RANZCOG clinical guidance, Cancer Australia and FIGO 2018 staging. Australian settings throughout; UK/NICE divergence flagged where it changes the answer. Written-finals depth: recognition, thresholds, next best step.*

## 10.1 The National Cervical Screening Program

Australia screens for the **cause** (oncogenic HPV), not the **consequence** (dysplastic cells). This inverts the old logic and every downstream rule follows from it.

**Current settings (verified August 2026):**

| Parameter | Current NCSP |
|---|---|
| Test | HPV PCR with **partial genotyping** (16/18 reported separately from "other oncogenic types") |
| Age | **25–74** |
| Interval | **5-yearly** if HPV not detected |
| Start | Age 25, provided ever sexually active — **regardless of HPV vaccination status** |
| Exit | Exit test at **70–74**; if HPV not detected, screening ceases |
| Collection | Clinician-collected cervical sample **or patient self-collected vaginal swab** — universally available, equivalent sensitivity |
| Reflex cytology | Laboratory performs LBC **only if HPV detected** — cytology is never the primary test |
| Immunosuppressed | **3-yearly**, and colposcopy if any oncogenic HPV detected |

⚑ **What changed from the old Pap program (1 December 2017):** the pre-2017 program was **2-yearly conventional cytology (Pap smear) from age 18–20 (or 2 years after first intercourse) to 69**. It became **5-yearly HPV testing, 25–74**. Four things drove the change: HPV causes essentially all cervical cancer, HPV testing is far more sensitive than cytology (so the interval can safely lengthen), the vaccinated cohort has collapsing cytological abnormality rates (making cytology's positive predictive value worse), and cancer under 25 is rare and not reduced by screening. Modelled outcome is a ~20–30% further reduction in incidence and mortality despite screening half as often.

⚑ **Self-collection (universal since 1 July 2022):** originally restricted to under-screened/never-screened women aged 30+ who declined a speculum. Now offered to **every** eligible screening participant as a routine choice. It is a **vaginal** swab (no speculum, no cervical visualisation). Key limitation: **a self-collected sample cannot have reflex LBC performed on it** — if HPV (not 16/18) is detected on a self-collected sample the patient must return for a clinician-collected LBC.

⚑ **Self-collection is for asymptomatic screening only.** A symptomatic woman (postcoital, intermenstrual or postmenopausal bleeding, persistent unusual discharge, visibly abnormal cervix) is **not** screening — she needs a speculum, a **co-test** (HPV + LBC simultaneously, not reflex) and referral irrespective of the result. *Clue → action: "screening test negative last year, now postcoital bleeding" → the negative screen is irrelevant; examine and refer.*

⚑ **April 2025 guideline revision** refined the immunosuppression definition, post-hysterectomy pathways, post-treatment surveillance (see 10.2), and the pathway for self-collected HPV (not 16/18) results where the patient does not return for LBC.

**The short version**
- HPV test, 5-yearly, 25–74, self-collect or clinician-collect — vaccination status does not alter eligibility.
- HPV 16/18 detected → **straight to colposcopy**, whatever the cytology.
- Symptoms override screening: co-test and refer, never self-collect.

### Management pathway

| Result | Risk category | Action |
|---|---|---|
| HPV not detected | Low | Repeat in 5 years |
| HPV **16/18** detected (any cytology) | Higher | **Refer for colposcopy** (LBC taken at colposcopy) |
| HPV not-16/18, LBC negative / pLSIL / LSIL | Intermediate | **Repeat HPV test in 12 months** |
| HPV not-16/18, LBC pHSIL / HSIL / glandular / cancer | Higher | **Refer for colposcopy** |
| HPV not-16/18 on self-collected sample | — | Return for clinician-collected LBC to triage |
| 12-month repeat: HPV not detected | — | Return to 5-yearly |
| 12-month repeat: HPV still detected | — | See below ⚑ |

⚑ **The intermediate-risk trap (changed 1 February 2021).** Older teaching: persistent HPV at the 12-month repeat with negative cytology → colposcopy. Current rule: **repeat HPV again at 24 months** rather than referring, because national data showed a low yield of CIN2+ in this group. **But four groups still go straight to colposcopy if HPV persists at 12 months:** age **≥50**, **Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander** people, those **overdue for screening by ≥2 years**, and those in whom the repeat is again HPV-positive at 24 months. This exact carve-out is examinable — the discriminator is not the result, it is *who* has it.

## 10.2 CIN, HSIL and management

Terminology has split. **Cytology** (LBC) reports pLSIL/LSIL/pHSIL/HSIL. **Histology** (biopsy) reports LSIL (CIN1) or HSIL (CIN2/CIN3). Finals stems mix them; read whether the sample was a smear or a biopsy.

| Histology | Natural history | Management |
|---|---|---|
| LSIL / CIN1 | ~60% regress spontaneously; a transient HPV infection, not a precancer | **Observe** — repeat HPV/co-test at 12 months. Do not ablate |
| HSIL / CIN2 | ~40–50% regress; in women <25 or wanting fertility, observation is reasonable | Treat, or observe with 6-monthly colposcopy in selected young women |
| HSIL / CIN3 | ~30% progress to invasion over 10–30 years untreated | **Treat** |
| Adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) | Skip lesions, endocervical, poorly sampled by cytology | Excisional treatment (cone/cylindrical), not ablation; often hysterectomy once family complete |

**Treatment of HSIL:** excision (**LLETZ / large loop excision of the transformation zone**, or cone biopsy) is preferred because it yields histology and excludes occult invasion. Ablation (laser, cryo) only when the whole lesion and entire transformation zone are visible, colposcopy–cytology agree, and invasion/glandular disease is excluded.

**Complications of LLETZ** — examinable because they drive counselling: haemorrhage, infection, **cervical stenosis**, and dose-dependent risk of **preterm birth and PPROM** in future pregnancy (risk rises with excision depth; a single shallow LLETZ carries modest risk). *Clue → answer: "23-year-old, CIN2 on biopsy, wants children" → offer observation with 6-monthly colposcopy rather than immediate LLETZ.*

⚑ **Test of cure after treatment for HSIL/AIS** — the 2025 revision replaced annual **co-testing** with **annual HPV testing** (clinician- or self-collected), starting **12 months post-treatment**, continuing until **two consecutive negative HPV tests**, after which the patient returns to routine 5-yearly screening. Older texts still describe co-test-based "test of cure" for 2 years.

## 10.3 Cervical cancer

Squamous ~70%, adenocarcinoma ~25% and rising proportionally (adenocarcinoma arises higher in the canal and is under-detected by cytology — another argument for HPV primary testing). HPV 16 and 18 cause ~70–80% of cases; 16 dominates squamous, 18 is over-represented in adenocarcinoma.

**Presentation:** postcoital bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, offensive watery/blood-stained discharge, and — late — pelvic/back pain, leg oedema, hydronephrosis, uraemia (obstructive renal failure is the classic terminal event). Advanced disease: fistulae (vesicovaginal, rectovaginal).

**FIGO 2018 staging (⚑ superseded the 2009 clinical-only system):**

| Stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| IA1 | Stromal invasion **<3 mm** depth |
| IA2 | Invasion **≥3 to <5 mm** |
| IB1 | Invasion ≥5 mm, tumour **<2 cm** |
| IB2 | **2 to <4 cm** |
| IB3 | **≥4 cm** |
| IIA1 / IIA2 | Upper two-thirds vagina, no parametrium; <4 cm / ≥4 cm |
| IIB | Parametrial invasion |
| IIIA / IIIB | Lower third vagina / pelvic sidewall or **hydronephrosis** |
| **IIIC1 / IIIC2** | **Pelvic** / **para-aortic node** involvement, any tumour size |
| IVA / IVB | Bladder or rectal mucosa / distant |

⚑ Three changes worth memorising: **imaging and pathology may now assign stage** (previously clinical exam only); **stage IB was split into IB1/IB2/IB3** at 2 cm and 4 cm; and **stage IIIC was created** so nodal disease upstages regardless of tumour size. The old **7 mm lateral-extension criterion for microinvasion was deleted** — depth alone defines IA.

**Treatment in outline:** IA1 with no lymphovascular invasion → cone biopsy or simple hysterectomy (fertility-sparing possible). IA2–IB2 → radical hysterectomy + pelvic lymphadenectomy, or radical trachelectomy if fertility desired. IB3 and above → **chemoradiotherapy** (cisplatin-sensitised external beam plus brachytherapy). ⚑ Open radical hysterectomy is preferred over minimally invasive surgery for early-stage disease following the LACC trial's inferior survival with laparoscopic/robotic approach.

**Prevention:** Gardasil 9 covers 16/18 (cancer) plus 6/11 (genital warts) plus 31/33/45/52/58. ⚑ **Since 6 February 2023 the National Immunisation Program gives a SINGLE dose** at ~12–13 years through school programs, with **free catch-up to age 25** (previously two doses, catch-up to 19). **Immunocompromised people still receive three doses.** A single documented dose before 26 counts as fully vaccinated.

## 10.4 Endometrial cancer and postmenopausal bleeding

**Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB) = endometrial cancer until proven otherwise.** Roughly 10% of women with PMB have endometrial cancer; the remainder are atrophy (commonest), polyps, hyperplasia, cervical lesions or MHT-related.

**Pathophysiology to reason from:** type 1 (endometrioid, ~80%) is **oestrogen-driven** — every risk factor is a source of unopposed oestrogen. Type 2 (serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma) is oestrogen-independent, occurs in older thinner women, is p53-mutant and behaves aggressively.

**Risk factors (all type 1 unless stated):** obesity (peripheral aromatisation of androgens in adipose tissue — the dominant modifiable driver), nulliparity, early menarche/late menopause, PCOS with chronic anovulation, **unopposed oestrogen therapy**, **tamoxifen** (anti-oestrogen in breast, partial agonist in endometrium), oestrogen-secreting tumours (granulosa cell), diabetes, hypertension. **Lynch syndrome** (MMR mutations) — lifetime endometrial cancer risk up to ~40–60%, and endometrial cancer is frequently the **sentinel cancer**, presenting before colorectal cancer. Protective: combined pill, multiparity, smoking (the only such association — do not offer it as advice).

**The short version**
- PMB → TVUS. **Endometrial thickness >4 mm → sample it.** ≤4 mm with a single episode → atrophy is likely, but persistent/recurrent bleeding still needs sampling.
- Pipelle first; hysteroscopy + directed biopsy if pipelle is insufficient, if the cavity is focally abnormal, or if bleeding persists despite a benign pipelle.
- On tamoxifen, thickness is uninterpretable — biopsy on symptoms.

### Investigation

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| First-line | **Transvaginal ultrasound**, measuring endometrial thickness (double-layer, sagittal) |
| Threshold | **>4 mm** in a postmenopausal woman with bleeding → proceed to endometrial sampling. Queensland Health categorises ET >4 mm as urgent (≤30 days) and ≤4 mm as ≤90 days |
| Sampling | **Pipelle** (outpatient, no anaesthetic). Sensitivity for cancer ~80–99% depending on series; good rule-in, imperfect rule-out |
| Definitive | **Hysteroscopy + directed biopsy ± curettage** — needed for focal lesions (polyps, submucous fibroids), failed/insufficient pipelle, cervical stenosis, or persistent bleeding with benign sampling |
| Staging | MRI pelvis for myometrial/cervical invasion; CT chest/abdomen/pelvis for extra-uterine disease; **MMR immunohistochemistry on all specimens** to triage for Lynch syndrome |

⚑ **Threshold caveat.** 4 mm is the operative Australian figure and the one to write. Published thresholds range **3–5 mm**: an Irish series argues 3 mm, older Australian commentary quoted <5 mm as reassuring, and a 2024 meta-analysis questioned whether 4 mm performs adequately across all populations. **Tamoxifen users are exempt from thickness-based triage entirely** — the endometrium is routinely thickened with subepithelial cysts, so investigate on symptoms.

**Endometrial hyperplasia:**
- **Without atypia** — low progression risk (<5% over 20 years). Treat the driver (weight, remove unopposed oestrogen) and give progestogen; **LNG-IUS is first-line** (higher regression rates than oral). Re-biopsy at 6 and 12 months.
- **With atypia (EIN)** — approximately a quarter to a third progress or harbour concurrent carcinoma. **Total hysterectomy + BSO** is standard. LNG-IUS with close surveillance only if fertility is desired or surgery unsafe.

**Treatment of endometrial cancer:** total hysterectomy + BSO ± sentinel/pelvic node assessment; adjuvant vaginal brachytherapy or EBRT ± chemotherapy by risk group. ⚑ Molecular classification (POLE-ultramutated, MMR-deficient, p53-abnormal, no-specific-molecular-profile) now guides adjuvant therapy — POLE tumours do exceptionally well and may be de-escalated. Prognosis is comparatively good because bleeding presents early: stage I 5-year survival ~90%.

## 10.5 Ovarian cancer

**The problem is silence.** No effective screening exists — **CA-125 and TVUS are not recommended even in high-risk women in Australia** (UKCTOCS showed no mortality benefit at population level). Symptoms are non-specific and late: bloating, early satiety, abdominal/pelvic pain, urinary urgency/frequency, altered bowel habit, weight loss. *Persistent, new, frequent (>12 days/month) symptoms in a woman over 50 → image her.*

| Type | Age | Features / markers |
|---|---|---|
| **High-grade serous** (~70% of epithelial) | 55–65 | Arises from **fallopian tube fimbria** (STIC lesion) ⚑ — hence opportunistic salpingectomy as prevention. TP53 mutant, chemosensitive, presents advanced. CA-125 high |
| Low-grade serous | Younger | Indolent, chemoresistant, KRAS/BRAF |
| Endometrioid / clear cell | 40s–50s | **Associated with endometriosis**; clear cell is chemoresistant and thrombogenic |
| Mucinous | Any | Large unilateral mass; exclude GI primary; **CEA** may be raised |
| **Germ cell** (dysgerminoma, yolk sac, teratoma) | **Teens–20s** | Rapid growth, torsion. **AFP** (yolk sac), **β-hCG** (choriocarcinoma), **LDH** (dysgerminoma). Highly chemosensitive, fertility-sparing surgery |
| **Sex cord–stromal** (granulosa cell) | Any | **Oestrogen-secreting** → precocious puberty, PMB, endometrial hyperplasia. **Inhibin** marker. Sertoli–Leydig → virilisation |
| Metastatic (**Krukenberg**) | — | Bilateral, signet-ring cells, from **gastric** (or colorectal) primary |

**CA-125 caveats — the examinable part.** CA-125 is a coelomic epithelium antigen, so it rises in anything that irritates the peritoneum: **endometriosis, fibroids, adenomyosis, PID, menstruation, pregnancy, ascites of any cause, cirrhosis, heart failure, pancreatitis, TB peritonitis, recent surgery**. It is **normal in up to half of early-stage disease** and is frequently normal in mucinous and germ cell tumours. Therefore: **a normal CA-125 does not exclude ovarian cancer**, and a raised CA-125 in a 28-year-old with dysmenorrhoea is more likely endometriosis. Its real value is monitoring treatment response and recurrence in a patient with known disease.

**Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI I):**

> **RMI = U × M × CA-125 (U/mL)**
> **U** = ultrasound score: 1 point each for multilocular cyst, solid areas, bilateral lesions, ascites, intra-abdominal metastases. U = 0 for 0 points, **1** for 1 point, **3** for 2–5 points.
> **M** = **1** premenopausal, **3** postmenopausal.

⚑ **Threshold disagreement:** NICE uses **RMI ≥250** to trigger referral to a specialist gynaecological cancer MDT; the original Jacobs 1990 derivation used **≥200**. Australian services use both — quote 200 as the classic cut-off and 250 as the NICE referral trigger, and say which you mean.

**Genetics:** lifetime ovarian cancer risk is ~1.2% in the general population, **~44% for BRCA1** and **~17% for BRCA2** (to age 80). **Risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy at 35–40 for BRCA1** and **40–45 for BRCA2**, once childbearing is complete; Lynch syndrome carriers at ~40. RRBSO also reduces breast cancer risk in premenopausal women and mandates MHT until the natural age of menopause (see 11.6).

**Management:** cytoreductive surgery (TAH + BSO, omentectomy, debulking to no macroscopic residual) plus platinum–taxane chemotherapy (carboplatin + paclitaxel), either primary debulking or interval debulking after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. ⚑ **PARP inhibitors** (olaparib, niraparib) as maintenance therapy in BRCA-mutated / homologous-recombination-deficient disease substantially prolong progression-free survival — a genuine change from older textbooks.

## 10.6 Vulval cancer and lichen sclerosus

**Lichen sclerosus** — chronic inflammatory dermatosis, bimodal (prepubertal girls, postmenopausal women), autoimmune associations (thyroid disease, vitiligo, pernicious anaemia).
- *Clue → diagnosis:* **intractable vulval itch, worse at night**, with **porcelain-white atrophic "cigarette-paper" skin**, a **figure-of-eight distribution around vulva and perianus**, purpura/fissuring from scratching, and **architectural loss** — resorption of labia minora, clitoral phimosis, introital narrowing. **The vagina is spared** (contrast with erosive lichen planus, which involves the vagina and causes a discharge).
- **Treatment:** ultrapotent topical corticosteroid **ointment** — clobetasol propionate 0.05% (or mometasone 0.1%) applied daily for ~12 weeks, reviewed at 3 months, then **maintenance once or twice weekly indefinitely**. Emollients as soap substitute. When de-escalating, reduce **frequency**, not the amount applied.
- **Malignant potential: up to ~5% lifetime risk of vulval SCC**, via **differentiated VIN (dVIN)** — HPV-independent, p53-mutant, arising in chronically inflamed skin. Annual vulval examination; **biopsy any area that is fixed, ulcerated, hyperkeratotic, or unresponsive to adequate steroid.**

**Two routes to vulval SCC:**

| Route | Precursor | Age | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| HPV-dependent (~40%) | **HSIL / usual-type VIN** | Younger, smokers | HPV 16 |
| HPV-independent (~60%) | **dVIN on lichen sclerosus** | Older | Chronic inflammation, p53 |

**Presentation:** persistent lump, ulcer, pruritus or bleeding; often long delay before presentation. Spreads to **inguinofemoral nodes** — node status is the dominant prognostic factor. **Treatment:** wide local excision with margins, plus **sentinel node biopsy** for unifocal tumours <4 cm with clinically negative groins (sentinel technique has substantially reduced the lymphoedema burden of full inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy ⚑). Melanoma is the second commonest vulval malignancy — pigmented vulval lesions need excision, not observation. **Paget's disease of the vulva** presents as an eczematous red plaque with white islands ("cake-icing") and warrants a search for underlying adenocarcinoma.

## 10.7 Gestational trophoblastic disease in brief

| | Complete mole | Partial mole |
|---|---|---|
| Karyotype | **Diploid, entirely paternal** (46,XX from duplication of a single sperm in an empty ovum, ~75%; dispermy ~25%) | **Triploid** (69,XXX/XXY — two sperm + one ovum) |
| Fetal parts | **Absent** | Present (fetal RBCs, malformed fetus) |
| hCG | Very high | Modestly raised |
| USS | "Snowstorm"/cluster-of-grapes, theca lutein cysts | Cystic placental change, growth-restricted fetus |
| p57 immunohistochemistry | **Negative** (no maternal genome) | Positive |
| **Progression to GTN** | **15–25%** | **0.5–4%** |

**Presentation:** first-trimester bleeding, uterus large for dates, hyperemesis, and — from hCG's structural similarity to TSH and LH — **hyperthyroidism** and **early-onset pre-eclampsia before 20 weeks** (a stem featuring pre-eclampsia at 16 weeks is a mole until disproven).

**Management:** **suction evacuation** regardless of uterine size; avoid oxytocics until evacuation is under way (theoretical embolisation risk); **anti-D if Rh-negative**; send tissue for histology; register with a state trophoblast centre.

**Surveillance:** serial quantitative **β-hCG weekly until three consecutive normal results**, then — for a **complete mole** — **monthly for 6 months**; a **partial mole** needs no further surveillance once normalised. **Avoid pregnancy during surveillance** (a new pregnancy makes hCG uninterpretable); the COCP is acceptable, though commencing hormonal contraception before normalisation is associated with more chemotherapy.

**GTN (invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumour):** diagnosed by an hCG **plateau (<10% fall over 3 weeks)** or **rise (>10% over 2 weeks)**, or metastases. Choriocarcinoma follows a complete mole in 25–50% of cases but can follow any pregnancy; it is intensely vascular and metastasises to **lung, vagina, brain, liver** — *clue → diagnosis: haemoptysis or a seizure in a woman with recent pregnancy loss and a positive hCG*. Treatment is **single-agent methotrexate (with folinic acid) or actinomycin D** for low-risk disease by WHO score, multi-agent (EMA-CO) for high risk. Cure rates approach 100% in low-risk disease even with metastases — this is one of the most chemosensitive solid tumours in medicine.

# O&G Notes — Section 11: Menopause, Urogynaecology and the Pelvic Floor
*Anchored to RANZCOG C-Gyn 9 (Managing Menopausal Symptoms), Australasian Menopause Society information sheets, ESHRE POI guideline 2024, Australian Prescriber and TGA regulatory positions. Emphasis on the progestogen rule, regimen choice, and the stress-vs-urge discriminators.*

## 11.1 Menopause: diagnosis and symptoms

**Menopause is a retrospective clinical diagnosis** — the final menstrual period, confirmed only after **12 months of amenorrhoea** with no other cause. Average age in Australia ~51. **Perimenopause** spans from the first cycle irregularity to 12 months after the FMP. **Early menopause** = 40–45; **premature ovarian insufficiency** = <40.

**Do not order FSH in a woman over 45 with typical symptoms** — the diagnosis is clinical and FSH fluctuates wildly across the perimenopause, so a "normal" value is uninformative. FSH (elevated, plus low oestradiol, on **two occasions**) is useful only when the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain: age <45, post-hysterectomy without oophorectomy, or suspected POI. ⚑ **AMH is not recommended to diagnose or predict menopause.** FSH is also uninterpretable on the COCP.

**Symptoms:** ~80% experience symptoms, ~25% severe. Vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats) — mean duration ~7 years, longer if onset is early in the transition. Sleep disturbance, mood lability, anxiety, brain fog, arthralgia/myalgia, palpitations, reduced libido, and **genitourinary syndrome of menopause** (11.5), which is the only cluster that **progresses** rather than resolving with time.

**Longer-term consequences of oestrogen loss:** accelerated bone loss (fastest in the first 5 years post-FMP) → osteoporosis; adverse lipid shift and rising cardiovascular risk; urogenital atrophy.

## 11.2 Menopausal hormone therapy: the progestogen rule

**The single most examinable fact in this section: oestrogen unopposed by a progestogen, in a woman with an intact uterus, causes endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial carcinoma.** Oestrogen is mitogenic to endometrium; a progestogen opposes it by inducing secretory transformation, downregulating oestrogen receptors and driving apoptosis/shedding. Therefore:

- **Uterus present → oestrogen + progestogen, always.**
- **Hysterectomy → oestrogen alone** (no progestogen needed, and avoiding it removes the progestogen-attributable breast cancer signal).
- **Exception — subtotal hysterectomy** with residual endometrium in the cervical stump, or a history of **endometriosis** with possible residual deposits: consider adding a progestogen.
- **Exception — low-dose vaginal oestrogen** for GSM: systemic absorption is negligible, and **a progestogen is not required**, even with a uterus.

**The short version**
- Intact uterus = progestogen, no exceptions worth risking in an exam.
- Still bleeding / <12 months since FMP → **sequential**. More than ~12 months postmenopausal → **continuous combined**.
- Continuous combined given too early in the transition causes erratic breakthrough bleeding — that is the classic wrong answer.

### Sequential vs continuous combined

| | **Sequential (cyclical)** | **Continuous combined** |
|---|---|---|
| Regimen | Daily oestrogen + progestogen for **12–14 days per month** | Daily oestrogen + daily progestogen |
| Bleeding | **Predictable monthly withdrawal bleed** | Aims for **amenorrhoea** — ~90% amenorrhoeic by 12 months |
| Who | **Perimenopausal**: still having periods, or <12 months since FMP | **Postmenopausal**: >12 months since FMP (or ≥1 year of sequential therapy, or age >54) |
| Why not the other one | Long-term sequential use carries a small excess endometrial cancer risk vs continuous combined | Given too early → unpredictable breakthrough bleeding and poor adherence |

**Progestogen options:** **micronised progesterone** (100 mg nightly continuous, or 200 mg nightly for 14 nights sequentially) — favoured because it appears to carry a lower breast cancer and VTE signal than synthetic progestins, and it is sedating (useful at night); medroxyprogesterone acetate, norethisterone, dydrogesterone; or the **52 mg LNG-IUS**, which provides endometrial protection *and* contraception in the perimenopause — often the neatest answer for a 47-year-old with heavy bleeding who also needs both.

**Oestrogen route:** oral oestradiol, or **transdermal patch/gel**. Transdermal avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism.

**Choose transdermal when:** ⚑ **VTE risk** (obesity, thrombophilia, previous VTE, immobility), **migraine**, hypertriglyceridaemia, gallbladder disease, malabsorption, or on enzyme-inducing drugs.

**Bleeding on MHT:** unscheduled bleeding in the first 3–6 months of starting or switching is common and can be observed. **Persistent, new or heavy unscheduled bleeding beyond 6 months requires investigation** exactly as for PMB (TVUS ± pipelle/hysteroscopy) — do not attribute it to the MHT.

## 11.3 Risks, benefits and contraindications

**Benefits:** MHT is the **most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms**, and it treats GSM, improves sleep and quality of life, and **prevents fracture** (reduces vertebral and hip fracture, with benefit lost after cessation).

| Risk | Magnitude / nuance |
|---|---|
| **Breast cancer** | Combined MHT increases risk with duration; **oestrogen-only carries little or no increase** (WHI oestrogen-only arm suggested a possible reduction). Absolute excess is small and comparable in magnitude to that from obesity, physical inactivity or ~2 standard drinks/day. Review continuation beyond ~5 years individually |
| **VTE** | **Oral** oestrogen roughly doubles VTE risk. **Transdermal at standard dose does not appear to increase VTE risk** in women at baseline low risk ⚑ — the single most useful prescribing fact here |
| **Stroke** | Small increase with oral MHT; transdermal appears neutral. Risk is age-dependent |
| **Coronary heart disease** | ⚑ **Timing hypothesis**: started **within 10 years of menopause / before age 60**, MHT does not increase CHD risk and may reduce all-cause mortality. Started >10 years out or after 60, risk–benefit turns unfavourable. **MHT is never indicated for primary prevention of CVD** |
| **Gallbladder disease** | Increased with oral MHT (~12 extra cases per 1000 women over 5 years) |
| **Endometrial cancer** | Only with **unopposed** oestrogen in an intact uterus, or inadequate progestogen dose/duration |

⚑ **The WHI correction.** The 2002 headline caused mass discontinuation worldwide. The trial population had a mean age of 63 — over a decade past menopause — and used conjugated equine oestrogens with MPA. Re-analysis by age stratum showed a favourable profile in women aged 50–59. Modern practice: **lowest effective dose, started close to menopause, individualised, no arbitrary stop date.**

**Absolute contraindications:** current or past **breast cancer**; **oestrogen-dependent malignancy**; **undiagnosed vaginal bleeding**; active VTE or arterial thromboembolic disease; active liver disease with deranged LFTs; known thrombophilia (for oral); pregnancy. *Prior VTE contraindicates oral MHT; transdermal may be considered with haematology input.*

## 11.4 Non-hormonal management of vasomotor symptoms

**Non-pharmacological with evidence:** **CBT** and **clinical hypnosis** reduce the bother and frequency of vasomotor symptoms. Weight loss, reduced alcohol, smoking cessation, layered clothing, cool sleeping environment. ⚑ **Phytoestrogens, black cohosh, evening primrose oil, yoga and acupuncture lack convincing evidence** — do not choose them as best answer.

**Pharmacological:**
- **SSRIs/SNRIs** — desvenlafaxine, venlafaxine, escitalopram, citalopram, paroxetine. ⚑ **Avoid paroxetine and fluoxetine in women on tamoxifen** — potent CYP2D6 inhibition blocks conversion of tamoxifen to endoxifen. Venlafaxine or desvenlafaxine is the safe choice in a breast cancer survivor.
- **Gabapentin / pregabalin** — useful where night sweats dominate.
- **Clonidine** — modest efficacy, poorly tolerated (dry mouth, sedation, rebound hypertension).
- **Oxybutynin** — some evidence for flushes; anticholinergic burden limits use.
- ⚑ **Fezolinetant** — a neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist targeting the KNDy neurons of the hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre; a genuinely new non-hormonal class, TGA-registered, requiring baseline and periodic LFT monitoring. Not in older textbooks.

## 11.5 Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)

⚑ Replaces the terms "vulvovaginal atrophy" and "atrophic vaginitis" because the syndrome includes **urinary** features. Oestrogen receptors are dense in vagina, urethra, trigone and pelvic floor.

**Features:** vaginal dryness, dyspareunia (introital, superficial), burning, pruritus, postcoital spotting, urinary urgency and frequency, dysuria, and **recurrent UTI**. Examination: pale thin friable mucosa, loss of rugae, petechiae, narrowed introitus, urethral caruncle.

**Discriminator:** GSM **does not resolve spontaneously** and **progresses** without treatment — unlike vasomotor symptoms which abate over years. Systemic MHT may be insufficient for GSM; local therapy is often needed in addition.

**Treatment:** non-hormonal **vaginal moisturisers** (regular, not just for intercourse) and **lubricants** (silicone-based outperformed water-based in trial) for mild symptoms; **low-dose vaginal oestrogen** (pessary, cream or ring) is the mainstay — effective, with negligible systemic absorption, **no progestogen required**, and no demonstrated increase in endometrial hyperplasia, VTE, breast cancer or cardiovascular events over 1–2 years of use. **Vaginal oestrogen also reduces recurrent UTI in postmenopausal women** — a high-yield answer in a geriatric/ID stem. Vaginal DHEA (prasterone) and oral ospemifene are alternatives. ⚑ **Vaginal laser therapy is not supported by evidence** and carries regulatory warnings.

## 11.6 Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)

**Diagnosis (ESHRE 2024):** age **<40**, with **oligo/amenorrhoea for at least 4 months**, plus **FSH >25 IU/L**. ⚑ A single elevated FSH with ≥4 months of menstrual disturbance is now sufficient; if the result is equivocal, **repeat in 4–6 weeks**. FSH does not need to be timed to a cycle day. **AMH must not be used as the primary diagnostic test.**

**Causes:** idiopathic (majority); **iatrogenic** (chemotherapy — especially alkylating agents, pelvic radiotherapy, bilateral oophorectomy); **genetic** — **Turner syndrome (45,X)**, **fragile X premutation (FMR1)**, other X abnormalities; **autoimmune** — commonly with autoimmune adrenal insufficiency, thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes; infection (mumps oophoritis).

**Work-up:** **karyotype** and **FMR1 premutation testing in all women with POI** (implications for the woman's relatives and for future fragile X offspring); **21-hydroxylase (adrenal) antibodies** — the highest-yield autoantibody; TFTs; bone densitometry.

**Management — the key distinction from ordinary menopause:** these women are **oestrogen-deficient for decades**, so hormone therapy is **replacement, not optional symptom relief**. Offer HT **at least until the average age of natural menopause (~50–51)**, using **higher doses** than standard MHT (e.g. transdermal oestradiol 75–100 µg vs 25–50 µg). The usual MHT risk framing (breast cancer, CVD) **does not apply** — these women are being restored to physiological normality, not exposed to excess. Untreated POI causes osteoporosis, increased cardiovascular mortality and probable cognitive impact. Either MHT or a combined pill can be used; MHT is generally preferred for bone. **Contraception is still required if pregnancy is not desired** — spontaneous ovulation occurs in ~5% of women with POI, and MHT is not contraceptive.

## 11.7 Urinary incontinence

**The short version**
- Leaks **with a cough** and no warning → stress. Leaks **with an overwhelming urge**, key-in-the-door, running water → urge/OAB.
- Both start with lifestyle + pelvic floor/bladder training; treat coexisting GSM with vaginal oestrogen.
- Urodynamics are for pre-surgical or complicated cases, not before conservative therapy.

| | **Stress (SUI)** | **Urge / OAB** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Urethral sphincter/support failure — intra-abdominal pressure exceeds urethral closure pressure | **Detrusor overactivity** — involuntary detrusor contraction during filling |
| Trigger | **Cough, sneeze, laugh, lifting, running** | **Urgency** — key-in-door, running water, cold, on standing |
| Warning | **None** — leak is simultaneous with the effort | **Sudden compelling urge** preceding the leak |
| Volume | Small spurts | Larger volumes; may empty the bladder |
| Nocturia / nocturnal enuresis | Uncommon | **Common** |
| Risk factors | Vaginal delivery, parity, obesity, chronic cough, menopause, prior pelvic surgery | Age, neurological disease (MS, stroke, Parkinson's), bladder irritants, idiopathic |
| Bedside test | Leak on cough with a comfortably full bladder | Bladder diary showing frequency, urgency, small volumes |

**Always exclude the mimics first:** UTI (dipstick/MSU), **overflow incontinence** (incomplete emptying, dribbling, palpable bladder — think diabetic neuropathy, anticholinergics, opioids, prolapse-related obstruction; check post-void residual), **fistula** (continuous painless leak after pelvic surgery or obstructed labour), functional incontinence (mobility, cognition), diuretics, caffeine, poorly controlled diabetes. **Mixed incontinence is very common** — treat the more bothersome component first.

**Assessment:** history + **3-day bladder diary**, examine for prolapse and atrophy, cough stress test, **post-void residual**, urinalysis. **Urodynamics are not required before conservative management**; indicate them before surgery for SUI, in mixed or complicated pictures, prior failed continence surgery, suspected voiding dysfunction or neurogenic bladder.

**Management ladder — SUI:**
1. Weight loss (strong evidence), smoking cessation, treat chronic cough and constipation, reduce caffeine, treat GSM with vaginal oestrogen.
2. **Supervised pelvic floor muscle training for at least 3 months** (e.g. 8 contractions three times daily) with a continence physiotherapist — first-line, and the single best answer in most stems.
3. Continence pessary or intravaginal device.
4. **Surgery**: **mid-urethral sling** (retropubic or transobturator), **colposuspension**, **autologous fascial sling**, or **urethral bulking agents** (lower efficacy, useful in frail or high-risk patients).
   ⚑ **Duloxetine is not TGA-approved in Australia for stress incontinence**, though it appears in UK/NICE pathways as a second-line option where surgery is declined. This is a genuine Australia–UK divergence.

**Management ladder — urge/OAB:**
1. Fluid modification, reduce caffeine and alcohol, weight loss, treat GSM with vaginal oestrogen.
2. **Bladder retraining** (timed voiding with progressive interval extension, urge-suppression techniques) for at least 6 weeks; plus pelvic floor training.
3. **Antimuscarinics** — solifenacin 5–10 mg daily or darifenacin 7.5 mg (M3-selective, less dry mouth/constipation) rather than oxybutynin. ⚑ **Avoid oxybutynin in older people** — it crosses the blood–brain barrier and impairs attention/cognition; anticholinergic burden is linked to dementia risk. Contraindicated in narrow-angle glaucoma; caution in impaired emptying.
   **Mirabegron** — β3-adrenoceptor agonist; no anticholinergic burden, so preferred where cognition, glaucoma or polypharmacy are concerns. Monitor blood pressure; avoid in severe uncontrolled hypertension.
4. **Third line:** **intravesical botulinum toxin A** (cystoscopic injection; effect lasts ~6–9 months; **counsel about urinary retention and the need to perform intermittent self-catheterisation**) or **sacral / percutaneous tibial nerve neuromodulation**.

## 11.8 Pelvic organ prolapse

**Symptoms:** vaginal bulge or dragging sensation (worse at end of day and on standing, relieved lying down — the cardinal discriminator), obstructed defaecation requiring **digitation/splinting** (posterior compartment), incomplete emptying or the need to reduce the prolapse to void (anterior), sexual dysfunction. **Pain is not typical** — pelvic pain should prompt a different diagnosis.

**Compartments:** anterior (cystocele), posterior (rectocele, enterocele), apical (uterine descent or vault prolapse after hysterectomy).

**POP-Q staging** uses the **hymen** as the fixed reference point:

| Stage | Leading edge |
|---|---|
| 0 | No prolapse |
| I | More than 1 cm **above** the hymen |
| II | Within **1 cm above to 1 cm below** the hymen |
| III | More than 1 cm below the hymen, but not fully everted |
| IV | Complete eversion (procidentia) |

**Risk factors:** vaginal parity (especially instrumental delivery, macrosomia, prolonged second stage), ageing and menopause, obesity, chronic cough, chronic constipation/straining, heavy lifting, connective tissue disorders (Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan), prior hysterectomy (vault prolapse).

**Management:**
1. **Observation** if asymptomatic — prolapse is treated for symptoms, not for stage.
2. Conservative: weight loss, treat constipation and cough, **pelvic floor muscle training** (best evidence in mild–moderate disease), vaginal oestrogen for atrophy.
3. **Vaginal pessary** — ring, ring-with-support, or Gellhorn for advanced/apical prolapse. Needs regular review (typically 4–6 monthly) for erosion; use with vaginal oestrogen. Appropriate at any age and the correct answer in a frail or pregnancy-planning patient.
4. **Surgery** — native tissue repair (anterior/posterior colporrhaphy), **sacrospinous fixation** or **uterosacral suspension** for apical support, **abdominal/laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy** for vault prolapse, or **colpocleisis** in frail women no longer sexually active.

⚑ **Mesh in Australia.** Following the TGA's 2017 review, **transvaginal mesh for pelvic organ prolapse was cancelled from the ARTG and is no longer available**. **Mid-urethral slings for stress incontinence and abdominal/laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy mesh remain available** under conditions (credentialled surgeons, informed consent requirements, device registry). Older textbooks describing transvaginal mesh kits for prolapse are obsolete in Australian practice.

# O&G Notes — Section 12: Contraception and Subfertility
*Anchored to RANZCOG/FSRH Contraception Clinical Guideline (C-Gyn 3, which formally adopts UKMEC), Australian Prescriber, Family Planning Australia, the 2023 International PCOS Guideline, WHO Laboratory Manual 6th edition (2021), and Australian consent law. UKMEC categories are used because RANZCOG endorses them — note this if a stem cites WHO MEC instead; the numbering is the same and the categories differ only in a handful of conditions.*

## 12.1 Methods and efficacy

**The short version**
- Rank by **typical use**, not perfect use — the gap is where real-world failure lives.
- **LARC** (implant, IUD/IUS, DMPA) has no user-dependent gap, which is why it is recommended first-line for anyone, including nulliparous women and adolescents.
- Sterilisation and LARC are comparable in efficacy; LARC is reversible.

| Method | Perfect use failure/yr | Typical use failure/yr | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Etonogestrel implant** (Implanon NXT) | **0.05%** | **0.05%** | **3 years** |
| **52 mg LNG-IUS** (Mirena) | 0.2% | 0.2% | ⚑ **8 years** for contraception (extended from 5) |
| 19.5 mg LNG-IUS (Kyleena) | 0.2% | 0.2% | 5 years |
| **Copper IUD (≥380 mm²)** | 0.6% | 0.8% | **10 years** |
| Vasectomy | 0.1% | 0.15% | Permanent |
| Tubal occlusion/salpingectomy | 0.5% | 0.5% | Permanent |
| **DMPA** (depot medroxyprogesterone) | 0.2% | ~6% | 12–14 weekly |
| **COCP** | 0.3% | ~7–9% | Daily |
| **POP** | 0.3% | ~7–9% | Daily |
| Vaginal ring / patch | 0.3% | ~7% | Monthly / weekly |
| Male condom | 2% | ~13% | Per act |
| Withdrawal | 4% | ~20% | Per act |
| Fertility awareness methods | 0.4–5% | ~2–23% | Cycle-based |

**Mechanisms worth knowing:** COCP — suppresses the LH surge, inhibits ovulation, thickens cervical mucus, thins endometrium. POP — cervical mucus is the primary effect for traditional POPs; **desogestrel and drospirenone POPs also inhibit ovulation** in most cycles (hence better efficacy and a wider missed-pill window). Implant/DMPA — ovulation inhibition. **LNG-IUS** — local endometrial suppression + mucus thickening; **ovulation usually continues** (so it does not treat vasomotor symptoms and does not cause hypo-oestrogenism). **Copper IUD** — copper ions are directly spermicidal and prevent fertilisation; **not an abortifacient**, and the only IUD that works as emergency contraception.

**Non-contraceptive benefits (exam favourites):** COCP reduces ovarian and endometrial cancer risk (persisting decades after cessation), treats acne/hirsutism, dysmenorrhoea and menorrhagia, regulates cycles in PCOS. **LNG-IUS is first-line medical therapy for heavy menstrual bleeding**, provides endometrial protection with MHT, and treats endometriosis-related pain and adenomyosis. **DMPA** causes amenorrhoea in ~50% at one year — useful where menstrual suppression is the goal — but ⚑ causes **reversible loss of bone mineral density**, a delayed return of fertility (median ~9 months after last injection), and weight gain.

**Missed-pill windows** — a clean discriminator:

| Pill | Late by |
|---|---|
| Traditional POP (levonorgestrel, norethisterone) | **3 hours** |
| Desogestrel POP | **12 hours** |
| ⚑ Drospirenone POP (Slinda) | **24 hours** |
| COCP | **24 hours** (a pill is "missed" once >48 h has elapsed since the last one) |

**COCP missed-pill rule:** one missed pill (<48 h late) → take it, no extra precautions. **Two or more missed (>48 h)** → take the most recent pill, continue the pack, **use condoms for 7 days**; consider **emergency contraception** if there was unprotected sex in the preceding 7 days and the misses were in **week 1**; if the misses were in **week 3**, **omit the hormone-free interval** and run into the next pack.

## 12.2 COCP contraindications by UKMEC category

**Category definitions:** **1** = no restriction. **2** = benefits generally outweigh risks — use it. **3** = risks generally outweigh benefits — **not usually recommended** unless other methods are unavailable/unacceptable, and only with specialist judgement. **4** = **unacceptable health risk — do not use.**

⚑ **The smoking distinction — memorise the age threshold, it is exactly what gets examined:**

| Smoking status | UKMEC for CHC |
|---|---|
| **Age <35**, smoking **any** amount (including ≥15/day) | **2** |
| **Age ≥35**, smoking **<15 cigarettes/day** | **3** |
| **Age ≥35**, smoking **≥15 cigarettes/day** | **4** |
| Age ≥35, stopped **<1 year** ago | 3 |
| Age ≥35, stopped **≥1 year** ago | 2 |

So a 32-year-old smoking 25 a day is **category 2** — the pill is not contraindicated, though cessation should be encouraged. The same woman three years later at 35 is **category 4**. The risk being avoided is myocardial infarction and ischaemic stroke, which rises steeply with the interaction of age and smoking. *(Verified concordant across UKMEC and the US MEC 2024.)*

| Condition | UKMEC for CHC |
|---|---|
| **Migraine with aura** — at any age, current or past | **4** |
| Migraine without aura, age <35 / ≥35 | 2 / 3 |
| BP **140–159 / 90–99** | 3 |
| BP **≥160 / ≥100** | **4** |
| Vascular disease | **4** |
| BMI 30–34 / **≥35** | 2 / **3** |
| Current or past **VTE**, or on anticoagulants | **4** |
| Known **thrombogenic mutation** (Factor V Leiden, prothrombin, protein C/S/antithrombin deficiency) | **4** |
| Major surgery with **prolonged immobilisation** | **4** |
| **Current breast cancer** / past breast cancer (>5 yrs, disease-free) | **4** / 3 |
| **Positive antiphospholipid antibodies** (incl. SLE) | **4** |
| Diabetes with nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy or other vascular disease | 3 or **4** |
| **Breastfeeding <6 weeks postpartum** | **4** |
| Breastfeeding 6 weeks – 6 months | 2 |
| Not breastfeeding, **<3 weeks postpartum** with VTE risk factors / without | **4** / 3 |
| Not breastfeeding, 3–6 weeks postpartum | 2–3 by risk |
| Severe (decompensated) cirrhosis, hepatocellular adenoma or carcinoma | **4** |
| Complicated valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathy with impaired function | **4** |
| Stroke or ischaemic heart disease | **4** |
| ⚑ **Lamotrigine monotherapy** | **3** |

⚑ **Two commonly-mis-stated points.** First, **migraine with aura is category 4 at any age** — it is not age-modified like migraine without aura, because of the ischaemic stroke risk. Second, **lamotrigine**: this is a *bidirectional* interaction, not enzyme induction of the contraceptive. Oestrogen induces lamotrigine glucuronidation and **lowers lamotrigine levels → loss of seizure control**, and levels then **rebound in the hormone-free interval → toxicity**. Hence UKMEC 3 for CHC with lamotrigine monotherapy; **progestogen-only methods are unaffected.**

## 12.3 Enzyme inducers and drug interactions

**Enzyme-inducing drugs (CYP3A4) reduce the efficacy of oestrogen- and progestogen-containing methods that rely on systemic levels:**

**Inducers:** **rifampicin, rifabutin** (the most potent — even a short course requires alternative cover), **carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone, primidone, oxcarbazepine, topiramate** (dose-dependent), **efavirenz, nevirapine, ritonavir-boosted protease inhibitors**, **St John's wort**, modafinil, bosentan.

**Affected:** COCP, patch, ring, POP, implant, and oral emergency contraception.
**Unaffected — the answer to "what should she use instead":** **copper IUD**, **LNG-IUS**, and **DMPA** (its depot levels are high enough to withstand induction).

⚑ **Broad-spectrum non-enzyme-inducing antibiotics (amoxicillin, doxycycline, trimethoprim, cephalosporins) do NOT reduce COCP efficacy.** The old advice about additional precautions during any antibiotic course was withdrawn — this remains a common wrong answer in stems.

Also note: after rifampicin, efficacy remains impaired for **28 days** after stopping. **Ulipristal's efficacy is reduced by enzyme inducers**, so a copper IUD is the better EC choice in that setting.

## 12.4 Emergency contraception

| Option | Window | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg** | Licensed to **72 h**, may be used up to **96 h** with reduced efficacy | Delays/prevents ovulation. **Ineffective once the LH surge has begun.** OTC (Pharmacist Only). Efficacy falls with increasing BMI — consider 3 mg if BMI >26/weight >70 kg or on enzyme inducers |
| **Ulipristal acetate 30 mg** | **120 h (5 days)** | Selective progesterone receptor modulator — **still works after the LH surge has started**, so more effective than LNG in the days closest to ovulation. Preferred if BMI >26 or weight >70 kg |
| **Copper IUD** | Insert within **5 days of intercourse**, or within 5 days of the **earliest estimated ovulation** | **>99% effective — the most effective EC by a wide margin**, unaffected by BMI or enzyme inducers, and provides 10 years of ongoing contraception. Should be offered to every woman presenting for EC |

**Quick-start rules after EC — the discriminator:**
- **After levonorgestrel:** start/restart hormonal contraception **immediately**; condoms for **7 days** (2 days for POP).
- ⚑ **After ulipristal:** **wait 5 days** before starting hormonal contraception — a progestogen given sooner competes at the progesterone receptor and blunts UPA's effect. Condoms until cover is established.
- Conversely, **do not give ulipristal to a woman who has taken a progestogen in the preceding 7 days** — use LNG or a copper IUD.

**Follow-up:** pregnancy test at **3 weeks** if the period is more than a week late, is unusually light, or symptoms suggest pregnancy. Offer STI screening and ongoing contraception at the same visit.

## 12.5 Sterilisation

**Vasectomy** — local anaesthetic, office-based, failure ~1 in 2000, complications minor (haematoma, infection, chronic scrotal pain ~1–2%). **Not effective immediately**: continue contraception until a **post-vasectomy semen analysis at ~12 weeks confirms azoospermia**.

**Female tubal occlusion** — laparoscopic clips, or ⚑ increasingly **bilateral salpingectomy**, which is now preferred where feasible because it further reduces the risk of high-grade serous carcinoma (fallopian tube origin — see 10.5) and has a lower failure rate. Failure ~1 in 200 lifetime; if failure occurs, **the risk that the pregnancy is ectopic is substantially raised**. ⚑ Hysteroscopic sterilisation (Essure) has been withdrawn worldwide.

**Counselling essentials:** regard as **permanent and irreversible**; reversal is not funded and success is limited; discuss LARC as an equally effective reversible alternative (implant and IUS are as effective as tubal occlusion); document that the patient understands failure rates; discuss regret risk (higher if young, recently postpartum, or in a new/unstable relationship). Sterilisation of a person unable to consent is **"special medical treatment"** requiring authorisation by a court or tribunal (see 12.6).

## 12.6 Contraception in minors: Gillick and the mature minor in Australian law

**The principle.** *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* (1986, House of Lords) established that a child under 16 may consent to their own treatment if they have **"sufficient understanding and intelligence to enable them fully to understand what is proposed."** Australia adopted this in ***Marion's Case*** (*Secretary, Department of Health and Community Services v JWB and SMB* (1992) 175 CLR 218, High Court): parental authority **diminishes as the child's capacity increases**, and terminates when the child achieves sufficient understanding.

**Capacity is decision-specific and sliding-scale**: the understanding needed to consent to the COCP is far less than that needed to consent to a hysterectomy. Assess understanding of the nature and purpose of the treatment, the risks and benefits, the alternatives, and the consequences of declining.

**Jurisdictional variation — the exam point.**

| Jurisdiction | Position |
|---|---|
| **Common law (Gillick/Marion) applies** | NSW, Qld, WA, Vic, Tas, ACT — no fixed statutory age; capacity assessed individually |
| **South Australia** ⚑ | *Consent to Medical Treatment and Palliative Care Act 1995* (SA): a person **aged 16 or over** may consent **as effectively as an adult**. **Under 16**, consent is valid if the treating practitioner believes the child is capable of understanding the nature, consequences and risks, **AND a second medical practitioner personally examines the child and corroborates that opinion in writing** — a uniquely prescriptive two-doctor requirement |
| **New South Wales** | *Minors (Property and Contracts) Act 1970* (NSW) **s 49**: treating a child **aged 14 or over** with that child's consent gives the practitioner a **defence to assault/battery**. It does not displace Gillick, and parental consent for a child under 16 also affords protection |
| **Northern Territory** ⚑ | Commonly cited as having a statutory position under the *Emergency Medical Operations Act 1973* (NT) permitting a person **aged 16 or over** to give valid consent, together with provisions allowing urgent treatment of a child without parental consent. **I was unable to confirm the exact section wording against the primary legislation in this pass — verify before quoting the section number in an exam answer; the age of 16 and the Act name are the safe elements to state** |

**Special medical treatment.** Procedures with permanent consequences and no therapeutic purpose for the child — most importantly **sterilisation** — fall outside parental authority entirely (*Marion's Case*) and require **court or tribunal authorisation** (e.g. NCAT in NSW for a child under 16).

**Practical rules for prescribing contraception to an adolescent:**
1. Assess and **document Gillick competence**.
2. **Encourage — but do not require — parental involvement.** Refusal to involve parents does not preclude treatment in a competent minor.
3. Confidentiality applies, with the standard limits: risk of serious harm to self or others, and **mandatory reporting** obligations.
4. Screen for **coercion, exploitation and abuse** — a substantially older partner, secrecy, or gift/drug exchange should trigger child protection consideration.
5. Note the **age of consent to sexual activity** varies: **16** in most Australian jurisdictions, **17 in South Australia and Tasmania** ⚑; mandatory reporting thresholds are set by state law, not by your judgement of the relationship.
6. Offer **LARC** — it is recommended, not contraindicated, in nulliparous adolescents, and IUDs may be used by people who have never been pregnant. Offer condoms concurrently for STI protection ("dual protection").

⚑ **Australian access changes worth knowing:** from **1 March 2025** Yaz and Yasmin (drospirenone/ethinyloestradiol) were **PBS-listed** — the first new oral contraceptives added in over 30 years — with the drospirenone-only pill Slinda also listed; several states (Qld, NSW, Vic and others) have introduced **community pharmacist resupply/prescribing of oral contraception**, and Medicare items and training funding have been expanded to increase LARC insertion in primary care.

## 12.7 Subfertility

**Definition:** failure to conceive after **12 months** of regular unprotected intercourse. **Investigate after 6 months** if the woman is **≥35**, or immediately where there is known pathology — amenorrhoea/oligomenorrhoea, prior pelvic infection or surgery, known endometriosis, undescended testes or prior chemotherapy. Roughly 1 in 6 Australian couples is affected.

**Causes (approximate):** male factor ~30%, ovulatory disorder ~25%, tubal/pelvic ~20%, unexplained ~25%, with uterine and cervical factors a small remainder. **Both partners must be investigated in parallel** — investigating the woman alone is a classic exam error.

**Ovulatory disorders (WHO groups):**
- **Group I — hypogonadotropic hypogonadism** (low FSH/LH, low oestradiol): hypothalamic amenorrhoea from low body weight, excessive exercise or stress; Kallmann syndrome. Treat by restoring energy balance; pulsatile GnRH or gonadotrophins.
- **Group II — normogonadotropic** (~85%): **PCOS** dominates.
- **Group III — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism** (high FSH): **POI/ovarian failure** — donor oocytes are usually the only route to pregnancy.
- Plus **hyperprolactinaemia** (treat with cabergoline) and **thyroid disease**.

**PCOS** ⚑ (2023 International Evidence-based Guideline, Australian-led): diagnosis in adults requires **two of three** — clinical/biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and **polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound (follicle number per ovary ≥20, or ovarian volume ≥10 mL) OR an elevated AMH** ⚑ (AMH is now an accepted alternative to ultrasound, but **not** as a stand-alone diagnostic test). **If irregular cycles and hyperandrogenism are both present, neither ultrasound nor AMH is required.** In **adolescents within 8 years of menarche, ultrasound and AMH must not be used** — both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are required. **⚑ Letrozole is first-line for ovulation induction** (superior live birth rates to clomiphene, and now preferred over it); clomiphene ± metformin, gonadotrophins or laparoscopic ovarian drilling are second-line. Metformin is for metabolic features, and is **not routinely recommended in pregnancy**. Weight optimisation remains the foundation.

### First-line investigations

| Investigation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Semen analysis** | The single highest-yield first test. Abstinence 2–7 days; **repeat abnormal results after ~3 months** (one spermatogenic cycle) before acting |
| **Mid-luteal serum progesterone** | Day 21 of a 28-day cycle — more precisely **7 days before the expected period**. **>30 nmol/L confirms ovulation** (some laboratories report 16–30 nmol/L as equivocal — check local reference range) |
| Day 2–5 FSH, LH, oestradiol | Ovarian reserve and WHO group |
| **AMH** | Ovarian reserve, principally to plan IVF stimulation — **does not predict natural fertility** and should not be used to counsel a woman that she cannot conceive |
| TSH, prolactin | Treatable endocrine causes |
| **Rubella serology** | Vaccinate before conception if non-immune (live vaccine — avoid pregnancy for 28 days) |
| **Chlamydia NAAT** | Before any uterine instrumentation |
| **Tubal patency** | **HyCoSy** or **HSG** if low risk of tubal disease; **laparoscopy and dye** if endometriosis, prior PID or pelvic surgery is suspected (also therapeutic) |
| Pre-conception care | Folic acid (0.5 mg, or 5 mg if diabetes, BMI ≥30, epilepsy on valproate, or prior NTD), iodine 150 µg, smoking/alcohol cessation, weight optimisation, cervical screening up to date |

⚑ **WHO 2021 (6th edition) semen reference limits — these replaced the 2010 figures:** volume **1.4 mL** (was 1.5), sperm concentration **16 million/mL** (was 15), total sperm number **39 million** per ejaculate, total motility **42%** (was 40), **progressive motility 30%** (was 32), vitality **54%** (was 58), **normal morphology 4%** (unchanged). Note these are **5th-centile reference limits from men whose partners conceived within 12 months** — they are **not** a threshold for fertile vs infertile, and a man below them can still conceive naturally.

⚑ **HSG with oil-based contrast (lipiodol)** has a demonstrated therapeutic "flushing" effect increasing subsequent conception rates (H2Oil trial) — relevant to Australian practice and a plausible best-answer in an unexplained-infertility stem.

## 12.8 Assisted reproduction in outline

**Stepwise escalation:** optimise modifiable factors (weight, smoking, alcohol, timing of intercourse — every 2–3 days throughout the cycle rather than ovulation-tracking) → treat the specific cause → **ovulation induction** (letrozole for PCOS) → **intrauterine insemination** (mild male factor, unexplained, donor sperm) → **IVF**.

**IVF sequence:**
1. **Controlled ovarian stimulation** with injectable FSH ± LH, with pituitary suppression by **GnRH antagonist** (now the common protocol; shorter and lower OHSS risk) or long GnRH agonist protocol.
2. Monitoring by serial TVUS follicle tracking ± oestradiol.
3. **Trigger** with hCG, or a **GnRH agonist trigger** where OHSS risk is high.
4. **Transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval** ~36 hours later.
5. **Insemination** — conventional IVF, or **ICSI** (single sperm injected into the oocyte) for male factor or prior fertilisation failure.
6. Culture to **blastocyst (day 5)**; ± preimplantation genetic testing.
7. **Elective single embryo transfer** ⚑ — Australian practice strongly favours SET to avoid multiple pregnancy, which is the dominant iatrogenic risk of IVF. Surplus embryos vitrified for frozen transfer.
8. **Luteal support** with vaginal progesterone.

**Prognosis:** live birth rate per initiated cycle falls steeply with maternal age — good in the early 30s, and low above 42 with autologous oocytes. **Female age is the strongest single predictor** and the point to make in counselling.

**Complications:** **OHSS** — vascular hyperpermeability from VEGF release; risk factors PCOS, high AMH/antral follicle count, young age, high oestradiol; presents with bloating, nausea, ascites, haemoconcentration, oliguria, and in severe cases pleural effusion, VTE and renal failure. Prevented by antagonist protocols, agonist trigger, and freeze-all. Also: multiple pregnancy, ectopic pregnancy (higher after IVF, and **heterotopic pregnancy** is a real risk), retrieval-related bleeding/infection, and the psychological and financial burden. ⚑ **Australia funds IVF through Medicare with no statutory cap on the number of cycles** — a substantive difference from the NHS, where funded cycles are limited and vary by region.
