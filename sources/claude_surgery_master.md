# Surgery Notes — Section 1: Perioperative Assessment and Preparation

*Sources: ANZCA PG07(A) and ANZCA/ADS/GESA/NACOS recommendations, Choosing Wisely Australia (ANZCA), ADS–ANZCA perioperative diabetes guidelines and ADS SGLT2i alerts, Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic, AMH, THANZ/IMJ DOAC guidance, RACS VTE guidance, NBA PBM Module 2, ACSQHC, 2024 ACC/AHA perioperative guideline, ERAS Society, Rogers v Whitaker (1992). Scope: pre-operative assessment, risk stratification, medication management, fasting, prophylaxis, consent and ERAS in the adult Australian surgical patient.*

## 1.1 Risk stratification

**The short version**
- ASA describes **systemic disease burden**, not operative risk — a crude descriptor predicting population mortality and nothing about the specific operation.
- ⚑ Routine pre-op bloods/ECG/CXR in a **healthy young patient having intermediate surgery are NOT indicated** — order in response to patient factors, symptoms, disease or the planned procedure (Choosing Wisely Australia/ANZCA).
- Inguinal hernia repair is **intermediate**, not minor — but surgical grade alone does not generate an investigation.
- **<4 METs** (can't climb a flight of stairs) = poor functional capacity, the trigger for further cardiac thought.
- **RCRI** = 6 equally-weighted variables; ≥2 points marks elevated risk.

### ASA physical status classification

| Class | Meaning (paraphrased) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Normal healthy patient; non-smoker, nil/minimal alcohol | Fit 25 y.o. |
| **II** | Mild systemic disease, **no substantive functional limitation** | Controlled HTN or T2DM, smoker, pregnancy, BMI 30–40 |
| **III** | Severe systemic disease with **substantive functional limitation** | Poorly controlled DM/HTN, COPD, BMI ≥40, dialysis, MI/CVA/stent **>3 months ago** |
| **IV** | Severe systemic disease that is a **constant threat to life** | MI/CVA/stent **<3 months ago**, ongoing ischaemia, severe valve disease, sepsis, DIC |
| **V** | Moribund; not expected to survive without the operation | Ruptured AAA, massive trauma, intracranial bleed with mass effect |
| **VI** | Brain-dead; organs being retrieved | — |
| **E** | **Modifier**, not a class (e.g. 2E) — added when delay would increase threat to life or limb; the *inability to safely delay* defines it | Incarcerated hernia |

**ASA does** correlate with mortality, length of stay and complications at population level — useful for audit and resourcing. **ASA does not** score the individual: it ignores the operation, age and airway, and has poor inter-rater reliability. An ASA 1 for emergency laparotomy is at far higher risk than an ASA 3 for cataract surgery.

### The exam scenario: ASA 1, elective inguinal hernia repair — which investigations?

**Answer: essentially none.** ⚑ A healthy young man on no medications, no comorbidity, having intermediate surgery needs **no routine FBC, U&E, coags, BSL, ECG or CXR**. The "pre-op screen" is obsolete. Choosing Wisely Australia/ANZCA is explicit: avoid routine pre-operative bloods, CXR or spirometry; order **in response to patient factors, symptoms and signs, disease, or the planned surgery**. Abnormalities in asymptomatic patients rarely change management, are often false positives, and generate a cascade of testing, delay and anxiety.

What actually triggers a test:

| Investigation | Indicated when |
|---|---|
| FBC | Major surgery/anticipated blood loss; suspected anaemia, menorrhagia, chronic GI loss; CKD, malignancy, chemotherapy, haematological disease |
| U&E | Diuretics, ACEI/ARB, digoxin, lithium, NSAIDs; CKD, diabetes, cardiac failure; major surgery or large fluid shifts |
| Coags | Anticoagulated; suspected bleeding disorder or liver disease. **Not** a screen when the bleeding history is negative |
| BSL/HbA1c | Known or suspected diabetes |
| ECG | Cardiac disease or symptoms (chest pain, syncope, palpitations), arrhythmia; major surgery with cardiac risk factors. Not by age alone in the asymptomatic fit patient |
| CXR | Acute respiratory symptoms, suspected malignancy. Not routine, not because a GA is planned |
| Group & hold | Anticipated transfusion |
| Pregnancy test | Anyone of childbearing potential in whom pregnancy is possible — offer, with consent |

### Surgical risk grading

| Grade | Examples | 30-day cardiac risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Minor** | Skin lesion excision, cataract, dental, breast biopsy, endoscopy | <1% |
| **Intermediate** | **Inguinal hernia repair**, lap chole, TURP, hip/knee arthroplasty, carotid endarterectomy, head & neck | 1–5% |
| **Major** | Open aortic/suprainguinal vascular, open colorectal/hepatic/oesophageal, pneumonectomy, Whipple | >5% |

### Functional capacity — METs

1 MET = resting O₂ consumption (~3.5 mL/kg/min). **4 METs** is the threshold: below it, reserve is poor and further evaluation may be warranted; above it, testing rarely changes management. 1 MET = dress, walk indoors; **4 METs = climb a flight of stairs, walk up a hill**; >10 METs = swimming, running. ⚑ Subjective estimation of METs performs poorly; 2024 ACC/AHA favours the **Duke Activity Status Index** (>34 = low risk).

### Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)

One point each: **high-risk surgery** (intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, **suprainguinal** vascular); **ischaemic heart disease** (prior MI, positive stress test, ischaemic chest pain, nitrates, pathological Q waves); **congestive cardiac failure** (pulmonary oedema, PND, bibasal creps, S3, CXR redistribution); **cerebrovascular disease** (stroke or TIA); **insulin-treated diabetes** (insulin, not diabetes per se); **creatinine >177 µmol/L**.

0 points ≈ very low risk; ≥2 ≈ elevated risk of major cardiac events. RCRI underestimates risk in vascular surgery. **NT-proBNP/BNP** adds discrimination in patients ≥45 with risk factors having major surgery.

## 1.2 Cardiac assessment before non-cardiac surgery

**The short version**
- Test only if the result **would change management** — same indications as the non-surgical setting.
- Active cardiac conditions mean **postpone and treat**.
- Elective surgery: delay **≥60 days after MI**; **≥30 days after bare-metal stent**; **≥6 months after drug-eluting stent** (⚑ 12 months if placed for ACS).
- **Never stop aspirin + P2Y12 within the mandatory DAPT window** — reschedule the surgery instead. Stent thrombosis carries ~20–45% mortality.

### When to investigate further

Investigate only when functional capacity is poor or unknown (**<4 METs**) **AND** clinical risk is elevated **AND** the result would alter management. **Resting TTE** — new/worsening dyspnoea, known or suspected moderate–severe valvular disease, uncharacterised LV dysfunction; not routine before major surgery. **Stress testing** — same indications as in the non-operative patient, not in low-risk patients or those with good exercise tolerance; ⚑ revascularisation purely to "get the patient through" does not improve outcome (CARP) and delays surgery. **Coronary angiography** — standard ACS indications only.

### Active cardiac conditions — delay surgery

Unstable/severe angina or recent ACS; decompensated heart failure (new, worsening, NYHA IV); significant arrhythmia (high-grade AV block, symptomatic bradycardia or VT, SVT with uncontrolled rate); severe symptomatic AS or MS. ⚑ Uncontrolled hypertension is *not* on this list — it predicts peri-operative events poorly, though ≥180/110 warrants reassessment rather than reflex cancellation.

### Timing rules

| Event | Minimum delay for elective surgery | Antiplatelet rule |
|---|---|---|
| Myocardial infarction | **60 days** (risk falls steeply over the first 2 months) | Continue aspirin |
| Balloon angioplasty, no stent | **14 days** | Continue aspirin |
| **Bare-metal stent** | **30 days** | DAPT mandatory 30 days — do not interrupt |
| **Drug-eluting stent, chronic coronary syndrome** | **6 months** (3 months acceptable if surgery cannot be delayed and risk of delay outweighs stent risk) | DAPT mandatory ≥3 months |
| **Drug-eluting stent, placed for ACS** | **12 months** ⚑ | DAPT 12 months |

**Rules that earn marks:** if surgery must proceed inside the window, operate in a centre with 24-hour PCI access, **continue aspirin throughout**, stop only the P2Y12 inhibitor and for the shortest possible time, restart it as soon as haemostasis allows (ideally <5 days). Bridging a P2Y12 inhibitor with a short-acting IV agent (tirofiban, cangrelor) is a specialist decision, not routine. Discuss with the interventional cardiologist and document who was consulted.

## 1.3 Respiratory disease, OSA and smoking

**The short version**
- Post-op pulmonary complications (PPC) rival cardiac complications in frequency and exceed them in attributable length of stay.
- The biggest predictors are **surgical**: upper abdominal/thoracic incision, open approach, operative duration >3 h, emergency surgery.
- **STOP-BANG ≥3** = intermediate risk of OSA, **≥5** = high risk.
- Stop smoking **≥4 weeks** before surgery for a measurable reduction in respiratory and wound complications; stopping at any point is still worth doing.
- Routine spirometry and CXR do not predict PPC and are not indicated in the absence of symptoms.

**Patient risk factors:** age >60, ASA ≥3, COPD (especially recent exacerbation), current smoking, obesity/OSA, heart failure, functional dependence, hypoalbuminaemia, active respiratory infection, pulmonary hypertension.

**Risk reduction that works:** lung expansion (incentive spirometry, CPAP), early mobilisation, analgesia adequate to allow coughing, regional/neuraxial where feasible, minimally invasive approach, quantitative neuromuscular monitoring with full reversal, and deferring elective surgery 4–6 weeks after a significant LRTI.

**STOP-BANG:** **S**noring loudly, **T**iredness, **O**bserved apnoea, **P**ressure (treated HTN), **B**MI >35, **A**ge >50, **N**eck >40 cm, **G**ender male. ≥3 intermediate, ≥5 high. High risk → peri-operative CPAP (own machine), minimise sedatives and long-acting opioids, extended recovery monitoring, avoid major day-case surgery.

**Smoking.** Nicotine and CO effects resolve in 12–24 h; ciliary function and sputum clearance take weeks; wound healing and immune benefits accrue over 4–8 weeks. ⚑ The old claim that stopping within 8 weeks *increases* complications is not supported by contemporary meta-analyses. Offer NRT — no need to stop it peri-operatively.

## 1.4 Medications: stop, continue, or switch

**The short version**
- Most cardiovascular drugs **continue**; most anti-diabetic drugs **stop** on the day. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1RAs have new, specific rules.
- **Aspirin continues** in secondary prevention (always with a stent); stop only in primary prevention or bleeding into a closed space.
- ⚑ **Warfarin bridging is now much more restricted** — BRIDGE showed bridging in AF increases major bleeding without reducing thromboembolism.
- ⚑ **DOACs are not bridged** — interrupt by drug, renal function and procedural bleeding risk.
- ⚑ **SGLT2 inhibitors: cease ≥3 days pre-procedure** because of euglycaemic DKA.

| Drug class | Action | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspirin** | Usually **continue** | Secondary prevention, all stents, vascular surgery. Withhold ~7 days only for intracranial, intramedullary spinal or posterior-chamber eye surgery, or primary prevention (POISE-2: no benefit, more bleeding) |
| **Clopidogrel / prasugrel / ticagrelor** | Stop **5–7 / 7 / 3–5 days** | Clopidogrel and prasugrel irreversible; ticagrelor reversible but has an active metabolite |
| **Warfarin** | Stop **5 days**; INR day before/of; target **<1.5** (⚑ **≤1.4 for neuraxial** — not 1.2) | Urgent reversal: vitamin K ± PCC. ⚑ **Bridge only for genuinely high thrombotic risk** — mechanical **mitral**/caged-ball valve, VTE <3 months, thromboembolism during a prior interruption, severe thrombophilia, recent stroke with very high CHA₂DS₂-VASc. **Do not bridge routine AF** |
| **DOACs** | See below — **no bridging** | By drug, CrCl and bleeding risk |
| **ACEI / ARB** | **Withhold morning dose** | Reduces refractory intra-op hypotension. Reasonable to continue in HFrEF |
| **Beta-blockers** | **Continue** — never stop abruptly | ⚑ Do **not** start de novo on the day (POISE: fewer MIs, excess stroke and death). If newly indicated, start ≥8 days pre-op |
| **CCBs / statins** | **Continue** | Statin rebound if stopped; consider starting in vascular surgery |
| **Diuretics** | Withhold morning dose | Check K⁺; continue in decompensated HF |
| **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapa-, empa-, ertugliflozin) | ⚑ **Cease ≥3 days** — 2 days before **plus** the day of surgery | **Euglycaemic DKA** — acidosis at a near-normal BSL. Check ketones if unwell/nauseated regardless of BSL. Day-stay: stopping on the day may suffice if fasting is brief. Emergency surgery on an SGLT2i → post-op ward able to manage DKA |
| **Metformin / sulfonylureas / DPP-4 inhibitors** | Withhold on the day | Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia while fasting; recheck renal function after major surgery/contrast |
| **GLP-1 / GLP-1–GIP RAs** (semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide) | ⚑ 2025 ADS/ANZCA/GESA/NACOS: **do not routinely cease**; **24 h clear fluid diet**, then standard fasting | Delayed gastric emptying → aspiration. ⚑ Diverges from earlier US (ASA 2023) advice to withhold 1 day (daily)/1 week (weekly). If unprepared: gastric POCUS, regional technique or RSI |
| **Insulin** | Basal continues (see 1.5); halve pre-mixed morning dose; omit short-acting while fasting | Never omit basal in T1DM |
| **Corticosteroids** | **Continue** + stress dose if prednisolone **≥5 mg/day for ≥1 month** or known adrenal insufficiency | Hydrocortisone **100 mg IV at induction**, then 200 mg/24 h infusion while NBM. Double usual oral dose: up to a week (major), 48 h (intermediate), 24 h (minor) |
| **COCP / HRT** | Individualise | Consider stopping COCP 4 weeks before major surgery; otherwise ensure VTE prophylaxis |
| **Lithium** | Withhold 24–72 h before major surgery | Narrow index; toxicity with fluid shifts, NSAIDs, ACEI, diuretics. Check level and U&E |
| **MAOIs** | Continue where possible, **flag to anaesthetics** | Avoid **pethidine** (serotonin syndrome) and indirect sympathomimetics (ephedrine, metaraminol) |
| **SSRIs/SNRIs** | Continue | Platelet dysfunction; hyponatraemia; serotonin syndrome with tramadol/fentanyl |
| **Methotrexate** | **Continue** | Safe at RA doses with stable renal function; withholding provokes flare |
| **Biologics** (TNF inhibitors, rituximab, JAK inhibitors) | Withhold — time surgery for the **end of a dosing interval** | Infection and wound healing; JAK inhibitors ~3–7 days |
| **Herbal supplements** | Stop ~**7 days** | The "G"s bleed — garlic, ginger, ginkgo, ginseng. St John's wort induces CYP450; kava/valerian sedate |

### DOAC interruption (no bridging)

| Drug | Renal function | Low bleeding-risk procedure | High bleeding-risk procedure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban | CrCl ≥30 | Omit **1 day** (last dose 2 days pre-op) | Omit **2 days** (last dose 3 days pre-op) |
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban | CrCl <30 | Extend by ≥1 day; seek haematology advice | Extend by ≥1–2 days |
| Dabigatran | CrCl >50 | Omit **1 day** | Omit **2 days** |
| Dabigatran | CrCl 30–50 | Omit **2 days** | Omit **4 days** |

Restart at **~24 h** after low-bleeding-risk and **48–72 h** after high-bleeding-risk surgery, once haemostasis is secure; prophylactic LMWH may cover the interval if VTE risk is high. Neuraxial blockade counts as high bleeding risk.

**⚑ Exam scenario — 71 y.o. on apixaban for non-valvular AF, elective right hemicolectomy.** Right hemicolectomy is major intra-abdominal surgery = **high bleeding risk**. Check CrCl; **omit apixaban for 2 clear days** (last dose 3 days pre-op, ~48–72 h of interruption); **do not bridge** — a DOAC's onset and offset are fast enough that bridging only adds bleeding; no routine coagulation testing (INR/APTT do not reflect DOAC level); post-op VTE prophylaxis per protocol; **restart apixaban 48–72 h post-op** once haemostasis is confirmed, with prophylactic LMWH in the interim.

## 1.5 Diabetes in the perioperative period

**The short version**
- Measure **HbA1c** within 3 months of elective surgery; **delay elective surgery if HbA1c ≥75 mmol/mol (9%)** and optimise.
- Put diabetic patients **first on the list** to minimise fasting.
- ⚑ **In type 1 diabetes, basal insulin is NEVER omitted** — omission causes DKA regardless of BSL and regardless of fasting.
- Target BSL **5–10 mmol/L** peri-operatively; monitor hourly.
- A VRIII is added *alongside* basal insulin, not instead of it.

**Pre-operative.** HbA1c, U&E, ECG if indicated. Look for complications with anaesthetic implications: autonomic neuropathy (gastroparesis → aspiration; silent ischaemia), nephropathy, stiff-joint syndrome (difficult intubation), CAD. Hypoglycaemia unawareness shifts the target upward (~10 ± 2.5 mmol/L).

**Day of surgery.** Withhold all non-insulin agents that morning (SGLT2i already ceased 3 days). Continue **basal insulin at usual dose and time** (−20% if recent nocturnal hypoglycaemia); halve pre-mixed morning dose; omit short-acting while fasting. Hourly BSL from admission.

**VRIII indications:** prolonged fasting (>1 missed meal) or major surgery; BSL rapidly rising and/or >12 mmol/L, **>12 for over an hour despite a correction bolus, or >15** (⚑ the ">10 twice" trigger is specific to cardiothoracic/ICU patients) or persistently >10; T1DM having more than a short day-case procedure; emergency surgery with poor control, DKA/HHS; ICU/cardiothoracic with BSL >10 twice.

**⚑ Absolute rule:** run subcutaneous long-acting basal **alongside** the VRIII. If basal is stopped, any interruption (tissued line, pump alarm, ward transfer) leaves a T1DM patient with **no circulating insulin** and DKA follows within hours. Give a substrate infusion (glucose with potassium) with the VRIII and overlap 30–60 min with subcutaneous insulin before ceasing.

**⚑ Exam scenario — 45 y.o. T1DM for bowel resection who has taken no insulin.** T1DM is **absolute** insulin deficiency. Omitting insulin while fasting does not protect against hypoglycaemia — it removes suppression of lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis, FFAs are converted to ketone bodies, and **DKA** develops, potentially at a BSL that looks acceptable (euglycaemic DKA); fasting, bowel prep and surgical stress accelerate it. Action: **BSL, VBG, ketones and electrolytes now**; do not proceed until ketoacidosis is excluded or treated; start a **VRIII with glucose and potassium** and restart basal; treat established DKA with fluid resuscitation and a fixed-rate infusion before elective surgery. If genuinely emergent, resuscitate and operate concurrently with senior input.

**Hyperglycaemia** >10 mmol/L: subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin by weight and diabetes type; recheck at 1 h; escalate to VRIII if persistent. **Hypoglycaemia**: <4.0 mmol/L must be avoided; treat from <5.0 mmol/L with IV glucose, recheck at 15 min, then longer-acting carbohydrate. Anaesthesia abolishes the adrenergic warning signs — hourly measurement is the only safeguard.

## 1.6 Fasting

**The short version**
- Adults: **6 h** solids, **2 h** clear fluids — ⚑ then further liberalised.
- ⚑ "**Sip til send**" — clear fluids up to **200 mL/h (adult) or 3 mL/kg/h (child) until called to theatre**.
- Children: solids 6 h, **breast milk 3 h**, **formula 4 h — ⚑ only in infants under 12 months; over 12 months formula and other non-human milk count as a solid and need 6 h**, clear fluids **1 h**.
- ⚑ "Nil by mouth from midnight" is **obsolete** — ~12 h average fasts, dehydration, hypotension on induction, hypoglycaemia and distress, with no aspiration benefit.
- GLP-1RA users: 24 h clear fluid diet then standard fasting (see 1.4).

| Intake | Adults | Children |
|---|---|---|
| Solid food / limited solid food (e.g. toast) | 6 h | 6 h |
| Infant formula | — | 4 h |
| Breast milk | — | 3 h |
| **Clear fluids** | 2 h (and **sip til send**, ≤200 mL/h) | 1 h (and sip til send, 3 mL/kg/h) |

**Clear fluids** = water, clear cordial/pulp-free apple juice, black tea or coffee, clear carbohydrate drinks. **Not** clear: anything with milk or particulate matter, alcohol.

Fasting rules assume normal gastric emptying. Consider RSI, gastric ultrasound or prokinetics with: pregnancy, obesity, diabetic gastroparesis, symptomatic GORD, bowel obstruction, raised intra-abdominal pressure, trauma/pain/opioids after the last meal, and GLP-1RA use.

## 1.7 VTE risk assessment and prophylaxis

**The short version**
- Assess every surgical inpatient on admission and reassess as things change — documented risk assessment is a national safety standard.
- **Enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily** is the usual Australian agent and dose (20 mg if lower risk; UFH 5000 U SC BD–TDS if CrCl <30).
- **Mechanical prophylaxis** (GCS ± IPC) whenever pharmacological prophylaxis is contraindicated or deferred.
- Extended prophylaxis: **28 days after major abdominal/pelvic cancer surgery**; **28–35 days after hip fracture and hip arthroplasty**.
- Neuraxial: **12 h** after prophylactic LMWH, **24 h** after a therapeutic dose; **4 h** after catheter removal before the next dose.

**Risk factors — patient:** previous VTE, active cancer, age >60, obesity, immobility, thrombophilia, pregnancy/puerperium, oestrogen therapy, heart failure, sepsis, IBD, dehydration, CVC. **Procedural:** major surgery (>30–45 min), pelvic/abdominal/orthopaedic surgery, arthroplasty, hip fracture, major trauma, spinal cord injury, prolonged anaesthesia, tourniquet.

**Agents.** LMWH (enoxaparin) first line, once daily, renally cleared — reduce or switch if CrCl <30. UFH 5000 U SC BD–TDS in severe renal impairment (higher HIT risk). Fondaparinux 2.5 mg daily from 6–8 h post-op in orthopaedics. Rivaroxaban/apixaban after elective arthroplasty. Aspirin is an arthroplasty option, though ⚑ **CRISTAL** found enoxaparin superior for symptomatic VTE. Mechanical = GCS and/or IPC (IPC has the better surgical evidence). First dose usually 6–12 h post-op, balancing the bleeding window against VTE risk and neuraxial timing.

**Duration.** Until discharge or full mobility (typically 5–10 days). Extend to **28 days** after major abdominal/pelvic **cancer** surgery and **28–35 days** after hip fracture or hip arthroplasty; ~10–14 days after knee arthroplasty.

**Contraindications — pharmacological:** active bleeding, severe coagulopathy, platelets <50, recent intracranial haemorrhage or neurosurgery, untreated inherited bleeding disorder, neuraxial procedure within the window, HIT. **Mechanical:** severe PAD, peripheral neuropathy, recent skin graft, cellulitis, severe leg deformity or oedema.

## 1.8 Surgical antibiotic prophylaxis and SSI prevention

**The short version**
- **A single pre-operative dose** — the aim is a therapeutic tissue concentration at knife-to-skin, not days of "cover".
- Give **within 60 min before incision** (usually at induction; vancomycin 2 h before, as it infuses slowly).
- **Cefazolin 2 g IV** first-line in adults (⚑ the 30 mg/kg up to 2 g formula is the **paediatric** dose — do not apply it to adults, it under-doses anyone under ~67 kg), **plus metronidazole 500 mg** (given within **120 min**, as for vancomycin) for colorectal and anaerobic-risk procedures.
- **Redose** beyond ~2 half-lives (cefazolin ~4 h) or blood loss >1.5 L.
- ⚑ **Prolonged post-operative prophylaxis is not indicated** — no SSI reduction, and it drives resistance and *C. difficile*. Do not continue "until drains out".

Prophylaxis is warranted where the SSI rate is meaningful or the consequence catastrophic: clean-contaminated and contaminated surgery, and clean surgery with prosthetic material (joint, graft, valve) — ⚑ **including hernia repair with or without mesh, which Therapeutic Guidelines lists as YES**. Clean surgery without prosthesis generally needs none. Dirty cases (established infection, perforated viscus) require **treatment**, not prophylaxis.

Severe/immediate penicillin hypersensitivity: substitute per Therapeutic Guidelines (vancomycin ± gentamicin ± metronidazole). ⚑ A non-severe penicillin rash is **not** a contraindication to cefazolin — cross-reactivity with first-generation cephalosporins is very low and reflex vancomycin substitution worsens SSI outcomes. Allergy delabelling is a genuine peri-operative intervention.

### SSI prevention bundle

| Element | Position and evidence |
|---|---|
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Single dose within 60 min of incision, redose for duration/blood loss. Post-op doses only in defined settings (some cardiac/vascular, lower-limb amputation) and ⚑ **never beyond 24 h**. **Strong** |
| **Normothermia** | Core **≥36 °C** — forced-air warming, warmed fluids. Hypothermia → vasoconstriction, low tissue O₂, impaired neutrophil function, coagulopathy, blood loss. **Strong** |
| **Glycaemic control** | **<10 mmol/L** in diabetics **and** non-diabetics with stress hyperglycaemia; avoid tight control. **Strong** |
| **Skin preparation** | **Alcohol-based chlorhexidine** (2% in 70% alcohol) > aqueous povidone-iodine. Must **dry fully** — diathermy fire. Avoid near eye, ear, mucosa. **Strong** |
| **Hair removal** | ⚑ **Don't remove unless it interferes.** If needed, **clippers immediately pre-op — never a razor** (microabrasions). **Strong** |
| **Supplemental oxygen** | Reasonable intra-op and immediately post-op in intubated patients. **Contested** |
| **Technique** | Haemostasis, gentle handling, no dead space, minimise operative time. **Moderate** |
| **Peri-operative bathing** | Soap or antiseptic wash night before/day of. **Weak but harmless** |
| **Bowel prep** | Mechanical alone: no benefit. ⚑ **Mechanical + oral antibiotics** reduces SSI in elective colorectal surgery. **Moderate** |
| **Dressings / drains** | Advanced dressings no better than standard; drains do **not** prevent SSI. **Moderate** |
| **Surgical Safety Checklist** | Sign-in/time-out/sign-out, antibiotic timing confirmed at time-out. **ACSQHC standard** |

## 1.9 Consent

**The short version**
- Australia applies the **material risk** test from **Rogers v Whitaker (1992)** — **not** Bolam, which the High Court expressly rejected for the duty to warn.
- Two limbs: **objective** — a reasonable person in the patient's position would attach significance to the risk; **subjective** — the doctor knows, or ought to know, that *this* patient would.
- Valid consent = **voluntary + informed + capacity + specific to the procedure**.
- It must be taken by someone able to perform the procedure or with adequate knowledge of it — not the most junior person available.
- In an emergency, treatment to save life or prevent serious harm may proceed without consent, limited to what is immediately necessary.

**Rogers v Whitaker.** Mrs Whitaker, near-blind in the right eye, had elective surgery on that eye and developed **sympathetic ophthalmia** in the good left eye — roughly a 1 in 14,000 risk — leaving her effectively blind. She had asked repeatedly about risk to the good eye; it was not disclosed. The High Court held a risk is material if **either** limb is met, and that the courts, not the profession, set the standard. Statistical rarity does not excuse non-disclosure where the consequence matters to that patient.

**Must be disclosed:** diagnosis; the procedure and what it involves; material risks, including rare risks with grave consequences; expected benefit and likelihood of success; **alternatives, including conservative management and doing nothing**; who will perform it (including trainee involvement); recovery, pain and time off; costs where relevant; and answers to the patient's actual questions — in language they understand, with a professional interpreter where needed.

**Capacity** is decision-specific and presumed in adults: **understand, retain, weigh, communicate**. An unwise decision is not evidence of incapacity.

**Who may consent:** the competent adult; a **mature minor** (Gillick-competent) for that decision, otherwise parent/guardian; for adults lacking capacity, a substitute decision-maker under state/territory guardianship law, respecting any valid **advance care directive**.

**Emergency doctrine:** where the patient lacks capacity and treatment is urgently needed to save life, prevent serious harm or relieve significant pain, proceed without consent — limited to what is immediately necessary, and not against a known valid refusal.

**Invalidating factors:** coercion; lack of capacity; consent taken after premedication/sedation; consent for a different procedure or wrong side/site; material risks undisclosed; questions unanswered; language barrier unaddressed; form signed with no discussion. Consent is a **process**, not a signature.

## 1.10 ERAS — enhanced recovery after surgery

**The short version**
- A bundled pathway from pre-admission to discharge; benefit comes from **compliance with the bundle**, not any single element.
- Reduces length of stay and complications **without** increasing readmission.
- Best pre-op evidence: counselling, avoiding prolonged fasting, **carbohydrate loading**, smoking/alcohol cessation, anaemia optimisation.
- Best intra/post-op evidence: opioid-sparing analgesia, goal-directed fluids, **no routine NG tubes or drains**, early feeding and mobilisation, early catheter removal, PONV prophylaxis.

**Pre-op:** structured counselling (patients who know the plan mobilise and eat sooner), prehabilitation, anaemia correction, smoking/alcohol cessation ~4 weeks out, no long-acting sedative premedication, and **carbohydrate loading** — a clear carbohydrate drink the evening before and 2–3 h pre-op, which attenuates the catabolic response and post-operative insulin resistance without increasing aspiration risk where emptying is normal.

**Intra-op:** minimally invasive approach, short-acting agents, normothermia, goal-directed rather than liberal fluids (overload and hypovolaemia both harm), regional/neuraxial and TAP blocks to spare opioids, multi-agent PONV prophylaxis.

**Post-op:** ⚑ **no routine NG tube** after abdominal surgery — it delays return of bowel function and increases pulmonary complications; ⚑ **no routine abdominal drains** — they neither prevent nor reliably detect anastomotic leak; remove urinary catheters early (<24 h unless epidural/pelvic surgery); **early oral intake** on day 0–1 rather than waiting for flatus; **out of bed on day 0**; multimodal analgesia with opioids as rescue only; defined discharge criteria.

## 1.11 Pre-operative anaemia and patient blood management

**The short version**
- **Three pillars:** optimise the patient's own red cell mass, minimise blood loss, optimise tolerance of anaemia.
- Anaemia is common, an **independent** predictor of morbidity, mortality and transfusion, and **modifiable** — screen ≥4 weeks before elective surgery.
- Iron deficiency: **ferritin <30 µg/L = absolute**; 30–100 µg/L with **TSAT <20%** or inflammation = functional.
- Transfusion: Hb **<70 g/L** usually indicated; **70–100 g/L** assess clinically; **>100 g/L** usually not indicated.
- ⚑ **Single unit, then reassess** — transfusion is a dose, not a number of bags.

**Workup:** FBC, film, ferritin, transferrin saturation, CRP, B12/folate, renal function, reticulocytes. Do not treat and stop there — **iron deficiency anaemia in an adult male or postmenopausal woman mandates investigation for a GI source**; the operation may itself be the treatment.

**Treatment.** Oral iron if there is time (≥6 weeks) and it is tolerated — ⚑ **alternate-day dosing improves absorption** by avoiding hepcidin induction. **IV iron** (ferric carboxymaltose) where surgery is sooner, oral iron is not tolerated or absorbed (IBD, coeliac, bariatric surgery, ongoing loss), or in functional deficiency with inflammation; watch for hypophosphataemia. Treat B12/folate deficiency. ESAs selectively, with concurrent iron.

⚑ **PREVENTT** found no reduction in transfusion or death from a single IV iron dose close to major abdominal surgery (readmissions fell). The lesson is timing, not futility — correct anaemia **early enough for erythropoiesis to occur**, not as a token infusion the week before.

**Minimising loss and optimising tolerance:** meticulous haemostasis, **tranexamic acid** where significant blood loss is expected (established in trauma, cardiac, orthopaedic and obstetric settings), cell salvage, point-of-care coagulation testing, limited phlebotomy, normothermia, cessation of non-essential antithrombotics. Restrictive transfusion is at least as safe as liberal in most stable surgical populations; active bleeding, ACS and symptomatic patients need individual judgement.

# Surgery Notes — Section 2: Postoperative Complications and Surgical Critical Care

*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines, AMH, ACSQHC Sepsis Clinical Care Standard (2022) and HAC list, ANZICS, ANZCA/FPM acute pain guidance and the AAGBI-derived LAST protocol, ERAS Society, RACS, NICE CG174/CG32, THANZ, Australian crystalloid PI. Scope: complications of major abdominal and general surgery in adults, plus fluids, nutrition, analgesia and sepsis.*

## 2.1 The post-operative fever ladder by day

**The short version**
- **Day 0–2**: almost always the **inflammatory (cytokine/IL-6) response to tissue injury** — proportional to operative magnitude, self-limiting. Examine; do not pan-culture reflexively.
- **Day 3–5**: **infection begins to dominate** — UTI (catheter-associated), then pneumonia, then IV cannula site.
- **Day 5–7**: **surgical site infection**, and after bowel surgery **anastomotic leak** — the fever you must not miss.
- **Beyond day 7**: **deep/organ-space collection or abscess**, **VTE**, prosthetic or line infection, *C. difficile*, drug fever.
- ⚑ **Atelectasis does not cause fever.** Repeated studies — and a 2024 review framed as debunking the teaching — find no association between radiological atelectasis and pyrexia. It causes **hypoxia**, not temperature.

### The 5 Ws (a hook, not a differential)

| W | Cause | Typical day |
|---|---|---|
| **Wind** | Pneumonia/aspiration (⚑ **not** atelectasis) | 3–5 |
| **Water** | UTI, catheter-associated | 3–5 |
| **Walking** | DVT/PE | 5+ (any time) |
| **Wound** | SSI, then leak/collection | 5–7 |
| **Wonder drugs** | Drug fever, transfusion reaction, TPN line sepsis | any |

⚑ **Fever <24 h** raises **necrotising soft-tissue infection**, **malignant hyperthermia** and **transfusion reaction** — the early fevers that kill. **Fever after day 7 in a patient who "isn't quite right"** is a collection until imaged.

### Worked stem: "Day 3 post laparotomy, temperature 38.6°C"

Day 3 sits at the pivot from inflammatory to infective. Reason in this order:

1. **Is she septic?** Vitals, perfusion, conscious state — tachycardia or hypotension out of proportion changes the tempo entirely (§2.14).
2. **Anastomotic leak** — if bowel was joined, exclude it even though day 3 is early: peritonism, failure to progress, unresolving ileus, new AF.
3. **Chest** — pneumonia/aspiration: hypoxia, focal signs, purulent sputum. **4. Urine** — catheter *in situ*? Dysuria after removal?
5. **Wound** — take the dressing down and *look*. **6. Lines** — every cannula site; phlebitis is under-diagnosed.
7. **Legs** — asymmetric calf swelling. **8. Drug chart** — new antibiotic, transfusion, heparin.

**First investigations**: FBC, CRP, U&E, LFT, **lactate**, **two sets of blood cultures** (before antibiotics if no delay results), urine MCS, CXR — and with peritonism, instability or a bowel anastomosis, **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast**. ⚑ After colorectal surgery a **falling CRP by days 3–5 has high negative predictive value for leak**; a rising or plateauing CRP with fever mandates CT.

## 2.2 Respiratory complications

**The short version**
- **Atelectasis**: near-universal after GA — hypoxia, basal creps, reduced air entry; resolves with expansion. ⚑ Not a cause of fever.
- **Pneumonia**: fever *plus* purulent sputum, new consolidation, neutrophilia/CRP rise, worsening gas exchange — usually day ≥3.
- **Aspiration**: emergency/unfasted surgery, impaired consciousness, NGT, post-extubation; dependent-segment infiltrate.
- Prevention is mechanical and behavioural: sitting up, **early mobilisation**, deep breathing/incentive spirometry, physiotherapy, **analgesia good enough to allow coughing**, smoking cessation.

| Feature | Atelectasis | Pneumonia | Aspiration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours–day 2 | Day ≥3 | Minutes–hours after the event |
| Fever | ⚑ Not attributable | Yes | Chemical pneumonitis may give early fever |
| CXR | Basal volume loss, raised hemidiaphragm | New consolidation, air bronchograms | Dependent-segment infiltrate |
| Bloods | Normal | Neutrophilia, CRP rise | Late rise only if infection supervenes |
| Treatment | Physio, mobilisation | Antibiotics | Suction and support; ⚑ antibiotics only if infection supervenes |

**Ventilator-associated pneumonia**: arises **>48 h after intubation** — new infiltrate, fever, purulent secretions, leucocytosis, worsening oxygenation. Bundle: head-up 30–45°, daily sedation interruption and extubation assessment, oral care, subglottic suction.

**Post-op hypoxia — causes**: opioid/anaesthetic **hypoventilation**, residual neuromuscular blockade, **atelectasis/shunt**, pneumonia, aspiration, **pulmonary oedema**, **PE**, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, splinting from pain, obesity/OSA — plus **anaemia and low cardiac output**, impairing oxygen delivery with a normal SpO₂.

## 2.3 Venous thromboembolism after surgery

**The short version**
- **DVT**: unilateral calf/thigh swelling, tenderness, warmth. **PE**: pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, tachycardia, unexplained hypoxia, syncope, new AF.
- ⚑ **D-dimer is useless post-operatively** — raised by surgery, inflammation and haematoma. Do not use it to exclude VTE; **image directly**.
- **CTPA** for suspected PE; **compression duplex ultrasound** for suspected DVT.
- **Surgery-provoked VTE is anticoagulated for 3 months**, then stopped.

**Wells (PE)**: DVT signs (3), PE most likely diagnosis (3), HR >100 (1.5), immobilisation ≥3 days **or surgery within 4 weeks** (1.5), previous VTE (1.5), haemoptysis (1), malignancy (1). Two-level: **>4 = likely** (image); **≤4 = unlikely** (D-dimer *in the general population*). ⚑ Post-operatively everyone scores for recent surgery and the D-dimer arm is invalid — **CTPA is the answer**. Wells (DVT) similarly weights cancer, immobilisation, **surgery within 12 weeks**, deep-vein tenderness and calf ≥3 cm larger.

| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| VTE, bleeding risk acceptable | Therapeutic **LMWH (enoxaparin)** or DOAC; LMWH preferred immediately post-op and in cancer |
| VTE, high bleeding risk / immediate post-op | **Unfractionated heparin infusion** — short half-life, titratable, reversible with protamine. This is the point of UFH |
| Anticoagulation absolutely contraindicated | **Retrievable IVC filter** |
| Massive PE with instability | ⚑ Recent major surgery contraindicates systemic thrombolysis — consider catheter-directed therapy or embolectomy |

**IVC filter** — only for **acute proximal DVT/PE in whom anticoagulation cannot be given**, or recurrence despite therapeutic anticoagulation. ⚑ It **treats nothing**: PREPIC2 showed no mortality benefit added to anticoagulation, and filters thrombose. Use a **retrievable** device and **remove it once anticoagulation can start**.

## 2.4 Wound complications

**The short version**
- **SSI** is **superficial incisional / deep incisional / organ-space**, diagnosed within **30 days** (**90 days** where an implant remains).
- Timing suggests organism: **<48 h** *Strep pyogenes* or *C. perfringens*; **days 3–7** *Staph aureus* and skin flora; **days 5–7+** enteric Gram-negatives and anaerobes after colorectal surgery.
- **Dehiscence** classically **day 7–10**; the herald sign is **pink serous ("salmon") discharge** from an intact-looking wound.
- **Evisceration**: cover with **saline-soaked sterile gauze**, resuscitate, NBM, analgesia, antibiotics, **theatre**.
- **Necrotising fasciitis is a clinical and surgical diagnosis** — **debridement, not antibiotics alone**.

| Level | Involves | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Superficial incisional** | Skin, subcutaneous tissue | Open and drain, swab, dress; antibiotics only for cellulitis/systemic features |
| **Deep incisional** | Fascia, muscle | Open, explore, debride; antibiotics; exclude fascial dehiscence |
| **Organ-space** | Any structure opened at surgery | **CT then drainage** ± antibiotics (§2.7) |

Principle: **drainage beats antibiotics** — a wound with pus is opened, not escalated antimicrobially; empirical therapy follows *Therapeutic Guidelines* by site and operated viscus.

**Dehiscence risk factors.** *Pre-op*: age, malnutrition/hypoalbuminaemia, obesity, diabetes, malignancy, jaundice, uraemia, steroids, smoking. *Intra-op*: emergency surgery, midline incision, inadequate fascial bites, contamination. *Post-op*: **raised intra-abdominal pressure** (coughing, vomiting, ileus, straining, ascites), infection, haematoma.

**Immediate management of a burst abdomen**: stay with the patient; NBM, IV access, fluids, analgesia, antibiotics; **cover exposed bowel with saline-soaked gauze** (do not push it back); NGT if distended; **urgent theatre for washout and mass closure**. Incisional hernia follows — warn the patient.

### Necrotising fasciitis vs cellulitis

| Cue | Cellulitis | Necrotising fasciitis |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Proportional | **Out of proportion**, then anaesthesia as nerves die |
| Progression | Hours–days | **Hours** — mark the margin, re-check |
| Systemic state | Usually well | **Toxic**: tachycardia, hypotension, confusion, organ dysfunction |
| Skin | Erythema, warmth | Dusky/violaceous, bullae, **crepitus**, necrosis (late) |
| Antibiotics | Improves | **Deteriorates despite them** |

**LRINEC**: CRP ≥150 (4); WCC 15–25 (1) or >25 (2); Hb 110–135 (1) or <110 (2); Na <135 (2); creatinine >141 µmol/L (2); glucose >10 mmol/L (1). ⚑ Supportive only — **≥6** raises suspicion, a low score **does not exclude** it, and neither score nor imaging may delay theatre. **Treatment**: resuscitate, **urgent radical debridement** (repeated at 24–48 h), plus **broad-spectrum antimicrobials** with an antitoxin agent (**clindamycin**, against streptococcal exotoxin). ⚑ **Antibiotics alone cannot work** — devitalised tissue is not perfused, so the drug never arrives.

## 2.5 Anastomotic leak

**The short version**
- Classic timing **post-operative day 5–7** (range ~3–10; later leaks occur).
- **Subtle before it is dramatic**: unexplained **tachycardia**, **new AF**, **failure to progress**, **unresolving ileus**, low-grade fever, oliguria, delirium.
- Highest risk: **low rectal/colorectal** and **oesophagojejunal** anastomoses. Investigation: **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV ± rectal contrast**.
- Management spans **antibiotics + percutaneous drainage** (contained) to **laparotomy, washout and defunctioning/takedown** (peritonitis or instability).

**Risk factors**: technical (tension, poor blood supply, low anastomosis); patient (malnutrition, hypoalbuminaemia, smoking, diabetes, obesity, steroids, CKD); disease (emergency surgery, contamination, obstruction, pelvic radiotherapy); peri-operative (hypotension/vasopressors, transfusion, ⚑ **NSAIDs**, linked to colorectal leak — many Australian units restrict them).

| Picture | Management |
|---|---|
| Radiological/subclinical, well | Antibiotics, bowel rest, observation, repeat imaging |
| Contained collection, stable | Antibiotics + **CT-guided percutaneous drainage** |
| Peritonitis, sepsis, instability | **Resuscitate → theatre**: washout, defunctioning stoma ± takedown (Hartmann's) |

### Worked stem: "6 days post anterior resection — abdominal pain, fever, tachycardia"

1. **Day 6 + colorectal anastomosis + fever + tachycardia = leak until proven otherwise** — day 6 is the modal day and this is the highest-risk anastomosis.
2. **Tachycardia is the earliest reliable sign.** Persistent unexplained tachycardia after bowel surgery is a leak signal — do not explain it away as pain or dehydration.
3. **Assess for peritonitis and shock**: guarding, rebound, rigidity; BP, urine output, lactate, conscious state.
4. **Resuscitate in parallel with diagnosis**: IV access, crystalloid, blood cultures, empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics, catheter, NBM, analgesia, **senior review**.
5. **Investigate**: FBC, U&E, **CRP** (⚑ failure to fall by day 3–5 is itself a red flag), coags, **lactate**, group and hold, and **CT with IV ± rectal contrast** for extraluminal gas, contrast extravasation, free fluid, perianastomotic collection.
6. **Branch**: peritonitic or unstable → **theatre**; localised collection and stable → **percutaneous drainage** plus antibiotics. ⚑ Do not wait for a better test — an unstable peritonitic patient goes to theatre without a CT.

## 2.6 Post-operative ileus vs mechanical obstruction

**The short version**
- **Ileus** = functional failure of propulsion; **prolonged POI** when it persists beyond ~3–4 days or forces NGT reinsertion.
- Ileus is **generalised and quiet**; early obstruction is **colicky and noisy**. The best bedside discriminator is **passage of flatus**.
- **CT with contrast** decides it: ileus = diffuse dilatation, **no transition point**; obstruction = **transition point** with collapsed distal bowel.
- Prevention is the **ERAS bundle**; a "stubborn ileus" is a **leak or collection** until excluded.

| Feature | Ileus | Early post-op mechanical obstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Continuous from surgery | Often after an **initial return of function**, then relapse (day 5–14) |
| Pain | Constant distension discomfort | **Colicky**, crescendo |
| Bowel sounds | Absent/hypoactive | **Hyperactive, tinkling** early; absent late |
| Flatus | Slowly returns | **Absent** |
| CT | Dilated small **and** large bowel, gas to rectum, no transition | **Transition point**, collapsed distal bowel |
| Cause | Handling, opioids, electrolytes, inflammation | Adhesions, internal or port-site hernia, anastomotic stricture, twisted stoma |

**Correctable contributors**: **opioids**, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia, uraemia, sepsis/collection, anticholinergics, immobility, **fluid overload** (bowel wall oedema).

**Prevention (ERAS)**: minimally invasive access, **no routine NGT or drains**, **opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia** and regional techniques, **goal-directed not liberal fluids**, early oral intake, **early mobilisation**, early catheter removal, **chewing gum**. ⚑ Alvimopan is not PBS-listed and is not routine Australian practice.

**Management**: NBM or clear fluids, NGT for symptom relief if vomiting, IV fluids with **K⁺ and Mg²⁺ replacement**, minimise opioids, mobilise — and **image if it fails to resolve or fever/tachycardia appear**. **Suspect early mechanical obstruction** when function returns then fails, or pain turns colicky; this favours earlier operation, because internal and port-site hernias strangulate.

## 2.7 Intra-abdominal collection and abscess

**The short version**
- Presents **day 5 onward**: swinging fever, tachycardia, anorexia, failure to progress, ileus, rising WCC/CRP.
- Site clues: **subphrenic** — shoulder-tip pain, hiccups, basal effusion; **pelvic** — diarrhoea, tenesmus, urinary frequency, boggy mass on PR; **paracolic** — local tenderness.
- **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast** is the investigation; ultrasound helps for pelvic/subphrenic collections.
- **Drainage is the treatment**, antibiotics adjunctive. Always ask **why** — a collection after bowel surgery implies a leak until excluded.

**Image-guided percutaneous drainage** is first line for an accessible unilocular collection, sparing a re-laparotomy; send fluid for **MCS** and narrow antibiotics. Operate if it is inaccessible or multiloculated, drainage fails, there is an ongoing source, or there is peritonitis or instability. Pelvic collections may be drained **transrectally**; re-image if fevers persist.

## 2.8 Post-operative acute kidney injury

**The short version**
- The dominant mechanism is **pre-renal**: hypovolaemia, blood loss, third-spacing, sepsis, intra-operative hypotension, low cardiac output.
- **Exclude post-renal first with a bladder scan** — retention and a blocked catheter are the quickest reversible causes.
- ⚑ Stop the nephrotoxins: **NSAIDs, ACEI/ARBs, aminoglycosides, diuretics**, avoidable contrast — dose-adjust metformin, morphine, enoxaparin.
- **KDIGO**: a creatinine rise, or urine output **<0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 h**. Treat the cause — **fluid treats hypovolaemia, not oliguria per se.**

| Category | Post-op examples | Bedside test |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-renal** | Hypovolaemia, haemorrhage, third-spacing, sepsis, cardiac failure | Tachycardia, low JVP, response to fluid challenge; concentrated urine |
| **Renal** | ATN from prolonged hypoperfusion, nephrotoxins, contrast, rhabdomyolysis | Muddy brown casts, CK, urinalysis |
| **Post-renal** | **Retention**, blocked IDC, ureteric injury (pelvic surgery), clot retention | **Bladder scan**; renal tract ultrasound |

**AKI vs urinary retention**: retention gives **suprapubic discomfort, an urge to void, a palpable bladder and a large scanned volume**, and catheterisation gives immediate high-volume drainage with resolution; oliguric AKI has an **empty bladder**. Hence the bladder scan precedes the fluid bolus. After relieving chronic retention watch for **post-obstructive diuresis**. Escalate for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload or uraemic complications.

## 2.9 Post-operative delirium

**The short version**
- Common, under-recognised, an **ACSQHC hospital-acquired complication**; high incidence after hip fracture, emergency and cardiac surgery, and in the frail elderly.
- **Predisposing**: age, **pre-existing cognitive impairment**, frailty, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, alcohol, prior delirium.
- **Precipitants**: pain, opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, **hypoxia**, sepsis, **retention and constipation**, electrolyte disturbance, dehydration, anaemia, sleep disruption, restraints.
- **Prevention is multicomponent and non-pharmacological**: orientation, sensory aids, sleep hygiene, early mobilisation, hydration, bowel and bladder care, deprescribing.
- **Management rule: delirium is treated by finding and fixing the cause.** Antipsychotics do not treat it — last resort only, lowest dose, shortest time, avoided first-line in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia.

⚑ In a surgical patient, new delirium is often the **presenting feature of a surgical complication** — hypoxia from pneumonia or PE, sepsis from a leak or collection, AKI, retention. Screen (4AT), examine, investigate — then consider drugs. **Cross-reference: the Geriatrics notes carry the screening tools, the delirium-vs-dementia-vs-depression table and the full pathway.**

## 2.10 Fluids and electrolytes in surgery

**The short version**
- Routine maintenance: **water 25–30 mL/kg/day**, **Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻ ~1 mmol/kg/day each**, plus **50–100 g/day glucose** to limit starvation ketosis.
- **Resuscitation is a different prescription**: **500 mL crystalloid over <15 min**, reassess, repeat.
- ⚑ Large-volume **0.9% saline causes hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis**; balanced crystalloids are preferred for most surgical resuscitation and replacement.
- ⚑ **The examinable exception**: in **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** from vomiting or gastric outlet obstruction, the correct fluid is **0.9% sodium chloride with added potassium — not Hartmann's**.
- **Over-resuscitation harms**: bowel wall oedema and ileus, anastomotic compromise, pulmonary oedema — ERAS targets **near-zero fluid balance**.

| Fluid | Na⁺ | K⁺ | Cl⁻ | Other | Glucose | Osmolarity (mOsm/L) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0.9% sodium chloride** | 154 | — | **154** | — | — | ~308 |
| **Hartmann's (compound Na lactate)** | 131 | 5 | 112 | lactate 29, Ca²⁺ 2 | — | ~278 (measured osmolality ~254) |
| **Plasma-Lyte 148** | 140 | 5 | 98 | acetate 27, gluconate 23, Mg²⁺ 1.5; ⚑ **no calcium** | — | ~295 |
| **5% dextrose** | — | — | — | — | 50 g/L | ~278 |
| **4% dextrose / 0.18% NaCl** | 30 | — | 30 | — | 40 g/L | ~283 |

**5% dextrose is free water** once metabolised — useless for resuscitation, a cause of iatrogenic hyponatraemia. **Hartmann's contains potassium**, so avoid as sole fluid in significant hyperkalaemia. ⚑ **Plasma-Lyte contains no calcium** — the balanced solution compatible with co-administered blood.

### Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis — the mechanism

Prolonged vomiting or gastric outlet obstruction loses **gastric HCl** — **H⁺ and Cl⁻**, with water, Na⁺ and K⁺. Then:
1. Loss of **H⁺** leaves the ECF **alkalotic** — bicarbonate rises.
2. Volume and **chloride** depletion drive **avid renal Na⁺ reabsorption** (hypovolaemia + aldosterone); with chloride unavailable as the accompanying anion, Na⁺ is reabsorbed **in exchange for K⁺ and H⁺**.
3. That exchange **wastes potassium** and **excretes H⁺ into the urine despite systemic alkalosis** — **paradoxical aciduria**.
4. Hypokalaemia itself promotes further H⁺ secretion, so the alkalosis is **self-perpetuating** until chloride, potassium and volume are restored.

⚑ The kidney cannot correct this until given **chloride** — hence **0.9% sodium chloride with potassium**, not Hartmann's, which is chloride-poor and whose lactate becomes **bicarbonate**, worsening the alkalosis. One of the few settings where saline is genuinely correct.

### Worked stem: "Vomiting two days post-op; Na 138, Cl 92, HCO₃ 34, K 3.0"

- **Interpretation**: metabolic alkalosis (HCO₃ 34) with **hypochloraemia** (92) and **hypokalaemia** (3.0), sodium apparently normal — classic gastric losses; the chloride deficit is the story.
- **Confirm**: VBG (raised pH, compensatory pCO₂ rise) and **urinary chloride** — **<20 mmol/L** confirms **saline-responsive (chloride-depletion) alkalosis**; the urine is **acidic**.
- **Ask why he is vomiting.** Two days post-op is not automatically gastric outlet obstruction — consider **ileus**, **mechanical obstruction** and **anastomotic leak**; examine, and image if plausible.
- **Treat**: NBM, **NGT** if obstructed or vomiting large volumes, and **IV 0.9% sodium chloride with potassium chloride** (peripherally, typically ≤10 mmol/h and ≤40 mmol/L; faster only centrally with cardiac monitoring — check local protocol and AMH). **Replace magnesium** — hypomagnesaemia makes hypokalaemia refractory. Chart losses, recheck U&E, treat the cause.

### Third-space loss, hyponatraemia, refeeding

**Third-space loss** — fluid sequestered into gut lumen, bowel wall, peritoneum and traumatised tissue; functionally lost from the circulation, greatest in the first 24–48 h after major surgery, peritonitis or pancreatitis, then **mobilised back around days 3–5** with a diuresis (and pulmonary oedema if over-filled). Replace with **balanced crystalloid** titrated to perfusion, urine output and lactate.

**Post-op hyponatraemia** is usually **non-osmotic ADH release** (pain, nausea, opioids, surgical stress) plus **hypotonic fluid**; prevent with **isotonic** maintenance fluid. ⚑ Correct chronic hyponatraemia slowly — ceiling **8–10 mmol/L in 24 h** to avoid osmotic demyelination; hypertonic saline only for severe symptoms under senior/ICU guidance.

**Refeeding syndrome** — in the malnourished (very low BMI, marked weight loss, prolonged minimal intake, alcohol misuse, low baseline K/PO₄/Mg), reintroduced carbohydrate drives insulin-mediated intracellular shift of **phosphate, potassium and magnesium** and consumes thiamine, causing arrhythmias, cardiac failure, seizures and Wernicke's. **Prevent**: **start low** (order of 10 kcal/kg/day), build over days, give **thiamine and B vitamins before and during** early feeding, replace **PO₄, K⁺, Mg²⁺** daily.

## 2.11 Nutrition in the surgical patient

**The short version**
- **If the gut works, use it** — enteral feeding preserves mucosal integrity and causes fewer infective complications than parenteral.
- ⚑ **Prolonged post-operative starvation is obsolete**; ERAS supports **early oral intake**, often within 24 h of colorectal surgery — bowel sounds are not a prerequisite.
- **TPN is for a gut that cannot be used or cannot absorb**, not one that is merely inconvenient.
- Assess **before** operating: weight loss, BMI, intake history, muscle bulk/handgrip, a validated tool (MST/MUST/SGA). ⚑ Albumin reflects inflammation more than nutrition — never use it alone.
- Malnutrition is itself an **ACSQHC hospital-acquired complication**, driving leak, SSI, dehiscence and death.

| Route | Use when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral ± supplements** | Functioning gut, safe swallow | Whether intake is actually adequate — chart it |
| **Enteral (NG/NJ, PEG/PEJ)** | Gut works, intake inadequate or unsafe | **Confirm tube position before feeding**, aspiration, diarrhoea, blockage, refeeding |
| **Parenteral (TPN, central)** | Intestinal failure: short bowel, high-output fistula, prolonged ileus, complete obstruction, severe malabsorption | **Line sepsis**, thrombosis, hyperglycaemia, electrolyte derangement, refeeding, hepatic steatosis/cholestasis |

**TPN discipline**: dedicated lumen, strict asepsis, dietitian-led prescription, daily U&E/BSL with regular LFT/PO₄/Mg — and **fever on TPN is line sepsis until proven otherwise**. Return to enteral feeding once the gut recovers.

## 2.12 Surgical drains and tubes of the abdomen

**The short version**
- Drains do not prevent complications — ⚑ ERAS discourages **routine prophylactic drainage** after colorectal surgery.
- **Open** (Penrose, corrugated) drain passively, higher infection risk; **closed suction** (Redivac, Blake) are the usual abdominal drains; **pigtail catheters** drain abscesses.
- Output **character** is diagnostic: serous → expected; **bilious/green** → bile leak; **feculent/turbid brown** → **anastomotic leak**; **frank blood at volume** → bleeding, escalate; **milky** → chyle; **urine** → ureteric/bladder injury.
- **Trend beats a single number** — a sudden rise *or* a sudden stop (possibly a blocked drain) needs assessment. Remove once output is low and the indication has passed.
- Send fluid for **MCS**, plus **amylase/lipase**, **bilirubin** or **creatinine** to confirm a pancreatic, biliary or urinary leak.
- ⚑ **A dry drain does not exclude a leak** — leaks track away from drains, and no drain substitutes for examining the patient or for CT. **Stomas are in Section 4.**

## 2.13 Post-operative analgesia

**The short version**
- **Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia** is the ANZCA/FPM standard — paracetamol, NSAIDs where safe, regional techniques and adjuncts, so opioid requirement falls.
- The WHO ladder is **inverted** for acute surgical pain: start strong, step **down**; **regular paracetamol is the foundation**, charted regularly, not PRN.
- **PCA** gives patient-triggered IV boluses with a lockout; ⚑ **no background infusion in the opioid-naive**, and **nobody else presses the button** — the patient's own sedation is the safeguard.
- Monitor **sedation score, not just respiratory rate** — sedation precedes respiratory depression.

| Step | Agents |
|---|---|
| **Foundation (always)** | **Paracetamol** regularly ± **NSAID** (caution: renal impairment, hypovolaemia, GI bleeding, elderly, ⚑ colorectal anastomosis) |
| **Mild–moderate** | Oral opioid (oxycodone; tramadol with care — serotonin toxicity, seizures) |
| **Severe / immediate post-op** | **IV opioid titration → PCA**, and/or **regional or neuraxial** |
| **Adjuncts** | Low-dose ketamine, lignocaine infusion, clonidine, TAP/rectus sheath blocks, wound infiltration |

⚑ **Discharge opioid stewardship**: **small quantities, immediate-release only, with a stop plan** — slow-release opioids are not recommended for acute post-operative pain.

| Complication | Recognise it by | Do |
|---|---|---|
| **Opioid respiratory depression** | **Rising sedation score** first, then slow shallow breathing, then falling SpO₂ (late, masked by oxygen), miosis | Stop opioid, oxygen, stimulate, support airway, **titrated IV naloxone** — short half-life, repeat or infuse |
| **Epidural haematoma / abscess** | ⚑ **Increasing or ascending motor block, block denser than expected, block not regressing after the infusion stops, new back pain, bladder/bowel dysfunction, fever** | **Stop the infusion, urgent MRI, urgent neurosurgical referral** — decompression within hours determines outcome; check coags and anticoagulant timing (Section 1) |

**LAST** — perioral tingling, metallic taste, tinnitus, agitation → seizures → bradycardia, arrhythmia, **arrest**. Management: **stop injecting**, call for help, **100% oxygen** and airway control, benzodiazepine for seizures (⚑ **propofol is not a substitute for lipid emulsion**), CPR if arrested — **prolonged resuscitation may be needed**. **20% lipid emulsion (Intralipid): 1.5 mL/kg bolus over 1 min, then 15 mL/kg/h**; repeat the bolus **up to twice at 5-minute intervals** and **double the infusion to 30 mL/kg/h** if the circulation is not restored; ⚑ **maximum 12 mL/kg**. Avoid **lignocaine** as an antiarrhythmic and vasopressin; give **adrenaline in small increments only** — large doses impair lipid rescue.

## 2.14 Sepsis in the surgical patient

**The short version**
- **Sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection.** Screen with track-and-trigger vital signs plus **lactate**; ⚑ **qSOFA is a poor screen** and is no longer recommended alone.
- ACSQHC framing: **"Could this be sepsis?"** — any clinician can escalate, and escalation must not wait for certainty.
- Australian timings (Sepsis Clinical Care Standard 2022): **blood cultures immediately** if this does not delay treatment; **antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 hour, where shock is present**; if shock is absent but concern persists, **no later than 3 hours from initial clinical review**; **antimicrobial review within 48 hours**.
- **Source control is the definitive treatment** — drainage, debridement, device removal, diversion or resection.
- ⚑ **Antibiotics without source control fail** — drug cannot reach undrained pus or dead tissue, and the patient deteriorates on "appropriate" therapy.

**Ward bundle**: oxygen if hypoxic; **two IV cannulae**; **two sets of blood cultures** plus site-specific specimens; **lactate** with FBC, U&E, LFT, coags, CRP, glucose, VBG; **empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobials per *Therapeutic Guidelines***, adjusted for source, local resistance, allergies and renal function; **balanced crystalloid in reassessed boluses**; **catheterise, measure urine output**; **escalate, consider MET/ICU** — and **control the source**.

**Source control by problem**: abscess → **drainage**; anastomotic leak → **washout ± defunctioning/resection**; necrotising soft-tissue infection → **radical debridement**; infected line → **remove it**; obstructed infected biliary tree or kidney → **ERCP or nephrostomy/stent**; ischaemic bowel → **resection**. ⚑ Achieve it **as soon as the patient is physiologically able** — sometimes the operation *is* the resuscitation.

Afterwards: **de-escalate** on cultures and chart a stop/review date (stewardship is an NSQHS requirement); plan follow-up — post-sepsis morbidity and cognitive effects are explicitly part of the standard.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Fever days 0–2 post-op in a well patient; evidence atelectasis causes fever | **Inflammatory/cytokine response to surgery**; ⚑ **none** — atelectasis causes hypoxia, not pyrexia |
| Commonest infective source of fever on days 3–5 | **UTI** (catheter-associated), then pneumonia, then cannula site |
| Fever on day 5–7 after a bowel anastomosis | **Anastomotic leak** until proven otherwise |
| Earliest sign of leak, and the investigation | **Persistent unexplained tachycardia** (± new AF, unresolving ileus); **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV ± rectal contrast** |
| Value of D-dimer in suspected post-op VTE; sole indication for an IVC filter | **None** — image directly (CTPA/duplex); filter only when **anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated** (provoked VTE treated **3 months**) |
| Classic day of abdominal wound dehiscence, and its herald sign | **Day 7–10**; **pink serous ("salmon") discharge** |
| Necrotising fasciitis vs cellulitis, and its treatment | **Pain out of proportion, toxicity, rapid progression, crepitus, deterioration on antibiotics** → **urgent radical debridement + broad-spectrum antibiotics incl. clindamycin**, never antibiotics alone |
| Ileus vs mechanical obstruction on CT | Ileus = **no transition point**; obstruction = **transition point, collapsed distal bowel** |
| Adult routine maintenance fluid prescription | **25–30 mL/kg/day water; ~1 mmol/kg/day Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻; 50–100 g/day glucose** |
| Na⁺/Cl⁻ in 0.9% saline vs Hartmann's | **154/154** vs **131/111** (Hartmann's also K⁺ 5, Ca²⁺ 2, lactate 29) |
| ABG picture of prolonged vomiting or gastric outlet obstruction | **Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria** |
| Correct fluid for that picture | ⚑ **0.9% sodium chloride with added potassium** — *not* Hartmann's |
| First test in the post-op patient not passing urine | **Bladder scan** — exclude retention/blocked catheter before the fluid bolus |
| Rule for managing post-operative delirium | **Find and treat the cause**; antipsychotics are last-resort, not treatment |
| Intralipid regimen for LAST | **1.5 mL/kg bolus over 1 min → 15 mL/kg/h**; ≤2 repeat boluses at 5 min; double to 30 mL/kg/h; **max 12 mL/kg** |

The **3-hour** limit applies only where there is genuine **diagnostic uncertainty** about infection** from initial clinical review |
| Why antibiotics fail in surgical sepsis | **No source control** — undrained pus and dead tissue are not perfused |

# Surgery Notes — Section 3: The Surgical Acute Abdomen and Bowel Obstruction

*Sources: WSES (adhesive SBO 2017, mesenteric ischaemia 2022), CODA, AVOD/DIABOLO and the van Dijk IPD meta-analysis, ACP 2022, NICE NG147, Cancer Council Australia Optimal Care Pathway and surveillance colonoscopy guidelines, National Bowel Cancer Screening Program, eviQ, Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic, RACS. Scope: surgical decision-making in the acute abdomen, appendicitis, perforation, diverticulitis, obstruction, colorectal cancer and intestinal ischaemia. Pancreatitis, biliary disease, PUD, GI bleeding, IBD and coeliac disease → see Gastroenterology.*

## 3.1 Approach to the acute abdomen

**The short version**
- **Visceral** pain is dull and midline; **parietal** pain is sharp and localised. **Migration** means inflammation has reached the parietal peritoneum — the most useful single item in the history.
- **Peritonism** is the sign, **peritonitis** the diagnosis; rigidity is involuntary and is the hard sign. ⚑ **Percussion tenderness has replaced deep rebound** — same information, better tolerated, more reliable.
- Exclude the **mimics** — MI, DKA, lower lobe pneumonia, ruptured ectopic, testicular torsion, ruptured AAA — and do a **βhCG in every woman of reproductive age**.

**Pain character.** *Visceral* (C fibres, bilateral autonomic afferents) — dull, colicky, patient writhes, felt **midline only**: foregut epigastric, midgut periumbilical, hindgut suprapubic. *Parietal* (A-δ, segmental somatic) — sharp, constant, patient lies still, over the inflamed peritoneum. *Referred* — diaphragm to the **shoulder tip (C3–5)**, ureteric loin-to-groin, biliary to the right scapula.

| Region | Surgical | Medical / other |
|---|---|---|
| **RUQ** | Cholecystitis, cholangitis (→ Gastro); duodenal perforation | Hepatitis; RLL pneumonia; Fitz-Hugh–Curtis |
| **Epigastric** | Perforated ulcer; pancreatitis (→ Gastro); **ruptured AAA** | **Inferior MI**; DKA; gastritis |
| **RIF** | **Appendicitis**; caecal Ca; mesenteric adenitis; Meckel's; ileal Crohn's | **Ruptured ectopic**, ovarian torsion, PID; **testicular torsion** |
| **Periumbilical** | **Early appendicitis**; SBO; **mesenteric ischaemia**; **AAA** | Gastroenteritis; DKA |
| **LIF** | **Diverticulitis**; sigmoid Ca; sigmoid volvulus | Ectopic, ovarian pathology; ureteric colic |
| **Diffuse** | **Perforation**; **mesenteric ischaemia**; advanced obstruction | DKA; hypercalcaemia |

**Signs.** Voluntary guarding is overcome by distraction; **involuntary guarding** is not, and means true peritoneal irritation. **Rigidity** is board-like and sustained — the hardest sign, suggesting free contamination. ⚑ **Percussion tenderness** gives the same information as rebound but is better tolerated and more reliable, rebound having poor inter-observer agreement and many false positives. RUQ pain and a positive βhCG both go to **ultrasound first**. **Never omit** hernial orifices, the scrotum, and PR.

**Mimics to exclude actively.** **Inferior MI** (epigastric pain, diaphoresis, often no chest pain — **ECG and troponin mandatory** over ~40); **DKA** (Kussmaul breathing, ketotic fetor); **lower lobe pneumonia** (commonest in children); **ruptured ectopic** (amenorrhoea, syncope, shoulder tip pain); **testicular torsion** (examine the scrotum, then theatre); **ruptured AAA**. Also hypercalcaemia, Addisonian crisis, porphyria.

**Investigations.** Bedside — obs, **βhCG**, urinalysis, BSL, **ECG**, VBG with lactate. Bloods — FBC, U&E, LFT, lipase, CRP, coags, group & hold (a normal WCC/CRP does not exclude early appendicitis). Imaging by presentation:

| Presentation | Imaging |
|---|---|
| ?Perforation | **Erect CXR** — sensitivity only ~**70–80%**, worse if poorly erect or small-volume, so **a normal film does not exclude perforation** → CT |
| RIF pain in a **child, young woman or pregnancy** | **Ultrasound** — assesses adnexa, no radiation. **A non-visualised appendix is non-diagnostic, not negative** |
| Adult male/older patient, equivocal US, or a sick undifferentiated adult | **CT abdo-pelvis with contrast** — the default; **CT angiography** if ischaemia suspected |

⚑ **AXR is not a screening test for abdominal pain** — its narrow uses are suspected obstruction, volvulus and following known pseudo-obstruction.

## 3.2 Appendicitis

**The short version**
- Luminal obstruction → distension → **visceral periumbilical pain** → mucosal ischaemia and bacterial invasion → transmural inflammation → **localised RIF parietal pain**. That sequence *is* the history.
- **Anorexia → pain → vomiting**; vomiting before pain should make you doubt the diagnosis.
- ⚑ **US first in children, young women and pregnancy**; CT where US is negative or equivocal.
- ⚑ **Appendicectomy remains standard in Australia**; antibiotics-first is an accepted option in *selected* uncomplicated cases, and **an appendicolith predicts antibiotic failure**.

**Eponymous signs.** **McBurney's** — maximal tenderness ⅓ along the ASIS–umbilicus line. **Rovsing's** — LIF palpation reproduces RIF pain. **Psoas** — pain on passive right hip extension (**retrocaecal**). **Obturator** — pain on internal rotation of the flexed right hip (**pelvic**).

**Alvarado score (MANTRELS).** Two points each for **RIF tenderness** and **leucocytosis**; one each for **M**igration, **A**norexia, **N**ausea/vomiting, **R**ebound, **E**levated temperature, **S**hift to the left. Total 10; ≤3–4 reasonably excludes, ≥7 supports, and the middle band is where imaging earns its place.

| Atypical group | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Retrocaecal** (commonest) | Caecum shields the parietal peritoneum → mild anterior tenderness, flank pain, **psoas sign**; mislabelled renal colic |
| **Pelvic** | Irritates rectum and bladder → diarrhoea, tenesmus, urinary frequency, suprapubic pain, soft abdomen; mistaken for gastroenteritis/UTI. **Obturator sign** |
| **Pregnancy** | Physiological leucocytosis and nausea are baseline; the uterus limits examination. ⚑ The classical (Baer) teaching of upward migration to the RUQ is **overstated — modern MRI shows ~1 cm displacement and McBurney's point remains the best guide**; say the pain is *often* higher and less localised, but don't quote a trimester map. **US → MRI**, and **operate on suspicion** — perforation harms the fetus far more than a negative appendicectomy |
| **Elderly** | Blunted pain and inflammatory response → much **higher perforation rate**; CT first; consider caecal cancer as the cause |
| **Very young (<5)** | Cannot localise, short omentum cannot wall off → vomiting, fever, **often already perforated** |

**Investigation (Australia).** βhCG, FBC, CRP, U&E, urinalysis (mild pyuria/haematuria from ureteric irritation does not exclude appendicitis). **US first** in children, young women and pregnancy — a non-compressible blind-ending tube >6 mm with periappendiceal fluid. **CT with IV contrast** if US is equivocal or an alternative diagnosis is plausible; **MRI** in pregnancy. **Diagnostic laparoscopy** is legitimate when suspicion is high and imaging equivocal.

**Antibiotics vs appendicectomy.** **CODA** (~1552 US adults, uncomplicated appendicitis including appendicoliths) found a 10-day antibiotic course **non-inferior for 30-day general health status**, but the practical numbers matter more: **~29% had appendicectomy by 90 days, ~40% by one year and ~50% by 3–4 years**, with more ED re-presentations; with an **appendicolith**, ~41% by 90 days and more serious complications. ⚑ **Australian position:** laparoscopic appendicectomy remains standard; antibiotics-first is reasonable in **selected** patients (CT-confirmed uncomplicated disease, no appendicolith, no perforation/abscess, not septic, immunosuppressed or pregnant) after consent that **about half eventually need the operation**.

**Complications.** *Perforation* → resuscitate, IV antibiotics, **urgent appendicectomy with lavage**. *Appendix mass/phlegmon* (~5+ day history, tender RIF mass, stable) → ⚑ **non-operative first**, with **colonic imaging afterwards if >40** to exclude caecal cancer or Crohn's; interval appendicectomy is selective. *Abscess* → antibiotics plus **percutaneous drainage** if ≳3–4 cm.

## 3.3 Perforated viscus

**The short version**
- Sudden, severe, **constant** pain with **rigidity** = perforation until disproved.
- Causes: **perforated peptic ulcer** (→ Gastro), **perforated diverticulitis**, malignancy, ischaemic or obstructed bowel, iatrogenic, trauma.
- **Erect CXR misses up to a third** → if suspicion persists, CT.
- The operation is **source control**, not elegance.

Abrupt onset (patients name the minute), worse on movement, patient lies rigidly still; board-like rigidity, tachycardia, then septic shock. **The elderly and immunosuppressed may perforate with few signs.** Take the erect CXR after ~10–15 minutes sitting up; **CT is definitive** and identifies the site and cause.

**Management.** Resuscitate — large-bore access, crystalloid, analgesia, NG tube, IDC, **broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Gram-negatives and anaerobes (Therapeutic Guidelines)**, HDU/ICU. **Source control** = washout plus repair: perforated duodenal ulcer → **omental (Graham) patch**; perforated colon → resect the diseased segment, with **a stoma if unstable or grossly contaminated** and anastomosis for the stable fit patient. Use **damage control** (resect, leave discontinuous, temporary closure, return after ICU resuscitation) in the physiologically deranged. Non-operative management only for the **sealed, contained** perforation.

## 3.4 Diverticular disease and diverticulitis

**The short version**
- **Diverticulosis** = pouches exist; **diverticular disease** = they cause symptoms; **diverticulitis** = they are acutely inflamed.
- ⚑ **Left-sided (sigmoid) in Western populations; right-sided in Asian populations** — easily misdiagnosed as appendicitis.
- ⚑ **Uncomplicated diverticulitis in a well, immunocompetent patient is increasingly managed WITHOUT antibiotics.**
- **CT-diagnosed diverticulitis warrants interval colonoscopy** — cancer imitates it exactly. Abscess → **percutaneous drainage**; peritonitis → theatre.

Pulsion diverticula herniate through the muscularis where the vasa recta penetrate, driven by raised intraluminal pressure and low fibre; inflammation follows micro-perforation of an obstructed diverticulum. **Presentation:** constant LIF pain over days, fever, altered bowel habit, localised tenderness ± mass, raised WCC/CRP. Diagnose on **CT with contrast**; **colonoscopy is contraindicated acutely.**

**Hinchey classification (in my own words)** — severity of *perforated* diverticular disease, graded by what has escaped the bowel and where it went:

| Stage | What has happened | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Inflammation confined next to the colon — small **pericolic** phlegmon/abscess | Antibiotics; drain if reachable |
| **II** | **Larger walled-off abscess further away** — pelvic, retroperitoneal | Antibiotics + **percutaneous drainage** |
| **III** | Abscess has burst giving **generalised purulent peritonitis**, defect sealed | **Surgery** — Hartmann's, or resection with anastomosis in the fit |
| **IV** | Unsealed perforation with **faecal peritonitis** | **Emergency Hartmann's**; high mortality |

⚑ **Antibiotics in uncomplicated disease.** **AVOD** and **DIABOLO** randomised CT-confirmed uncomplicated diverticulitis to observation vs antibiotics and found no difference in recovery, progression, recurrence or resection; the pooled individual-patient meta-analysis (~1100 patients) confirmed this at one year and found **no subgroup that benefited** — including higher pain scores, WCC >13.5 or prior episodes. NICE NG147 and Australian practice have followed. **"Uncomplicated" = all of:** no abscess, no free gas, no fistula, no obstruction, no peritonitis on CT. **Antibiotics and admission ARE needed if** systemically unwell; **complicated** on CT; **immunosuppressed**; comorbid, frail or pregnant; unable to tolerate oral intake; or no improvement at 48–72 h — covering Gram-negatives and anaerobes per **Therapeutic Guidelines** (amoxicillin + gentamicin + metronidazole IV, then amoxicillin–clavulanate).

**Complications.** *Abscess* — antibiotics plus **percutaneous drainage** if ≳3–4 cm. *Peritonitis* — laparotomy: **Hartmann's** if unstable, contaminated or comorbid, **primary anastomosis ± defunctioning ileostomy** increasingly preferred in the fit; ⚑ **laparoscopic lavage without resection is not recommended as routine practice**, though WSES allows an individualised role in selected Hinchey III. *Colovesical fistula* — **pneumaturia and faecaluria** ± polymicrobial UTIs; elective resection with bladder repair. *Stricture* — **distinguish from malignant stricture**. *Bleeding* — painless and brisk (→ Gastro).

⚑ **Interval colonoscopy.** Cancer produces a CT appearance indistinguishable from diverticulitis, so **book colonoscopy ~6–8 weeks after the episode settles** unless a good-quality one was very recent. *Source disagreement:* ACP restricts this to **complicated** diverticulitis (where cancer prevalence is severalfold higher); most surgical guidance and Australian practice scopes after any CT-proven episode.

**Past exam question — 58-year-old, LIF pain, fever, raised inflammatory markers, CT findings.** Structure: **diagnosis** = acute sigmoid diverticulitis → **classify** uncomplicated vs complicated on CT (abscess? free gas? fistula? obstruction?) → **management**: analgesia, oral intake as tolerated, and if well and uncomplicated, **observation without antibiotics** with safety-netting and 48–72 h review; antibiotics if the criteria above are met; drainage for an abscess; **and in every case interval colonoscopy at 6–8 weeks** — the mark candidates drop.

## 3.5 Bowel obstruction

**The short version**
- **SBO**: adhesions > hernia > malignancy. **LBO**: malignancy > volvulus > diverticular stricture. Vomiting **early** in SBO and **late** in LBO; absolute constipation **late** in SBO, **early** in LBO.
- **Valvulae conniventes** cross the full lumen; **haustra** do not. **3-6-9 rule**: small bowel >3 cm, colon >6 cm, caecum >9 cm.
- ⚑ **Colicky pain becoming CONSTANT with tachycardia, fever, localised tenderness and rising lactate = STRANGULATION → theatre now**; **closed-loop obstruction** will not resolve conservatively.

| Feature | **Small bowel** | **Large bowel** |
|---|---|---|
| Causes | • **Adhesions (~60–75%)** • **Hernia** • Malignancy • Crohn's stricture • Gallstone ileus, intussusception | • **Colorectal carcinoma** • **Volvulus** (sigmoid > caecal) • Diverticular stricture • Impaction, pseudo-obstruction |
| **Vomiting** | **Early**; proximal copious bilious, distal later and **faeculent** | **Late**, sometimes absent |
| **Distension** | Proportional to level | **Marked** |
| **Constipation** | **Late** | **Early — the presenting symptom** |
| **Plain film** | **Central** loops; **valvulae conniventes span the whole lumen**; short fluid levels | **Peripheral** loops; **haustra are incomplete and do not cross**; fewer, longer levels |
| **Diameter** | **>3 cm** | **>6 cm**; **caecum >9 cm** |

**Closed loop and strangulation.** A **closed loop** is obstruction at two points, so neither proximal decompression nor distal emptying relieves it — volvulus, a hernia trapping a loop, adhesive bands, and **LBO with a competent ileocaecal valve** (the caecum, having the largest diameter, perforates first by Laplace's law). Rising pressure exceeds venous then arterial pressure → **strangulation** → necrosis → perforation. Simple obstruction gives **colicky pain with pain-free intervals** and a soft abdomen. ⚑ **Strangulation is signalled by pain becoming constant and unremitting, with fever, tachycardia, localised tenderness with guarding and rising lactate**; CT shows wall thickening, **reduced or absent mural enhancement**, mesenteric oedema, a whirl sign, free fluid, pneumatosis or portal venous gas. **The trap:** lactate and peritonism are *late*.

**Management.** NBM, **NG tube on free drainage**, **IV resuscitation** with careful potassium replacement, IDC, analgesia, VTE prophylaxis, lactate, and **CT with contrast** to define level, cause and ischaemia. The conservative trial (**"drip and suck"**) suits **adhesive SBO** without peritonism, strangulation or a closed loop: WSES considers **up to ~72 hours** safe. A **water-soluble contrast (Gastrografin) challenge** is diagnostic and mildly therapeutic — ⚑ **if contrast has not reached the colon at 24 hours, non-operative management is very likely to fail** → theatre.

**Absolute indications for surgery:** peritonitis, strangulation or ischaemia; **closed-loop obstruction** on CT; **irreducible/strangulated hernia**; complete obstruction needing resection; perforation; failed conservative management or contrast challenge; ⚑ **first-presentation SBO with no previous abdominal surgery** — the cause is not adhesions, so do not "trial and see". In **LBO**, an obstructing left-sided cancer may be resected, defunctioned, or **stented as a bridge to elective one-stage surgery** or palliation; right-sided tumours go straight to right hemicolectomy.

**Volvulus.** *Sigmoid* — commoner; **elderly, institutionalised, constipated, on neuropsychiatric medication**; AXR shows the **"coffee bean"**, a huge loop from the pelvis pointing to the **RUQ** with loss of haustra (whirl sign on CT). ⚑ **Endoscopic decompression first** if there is no ischaemia or peritonitis, but **recurrence is ~50–60%, so fit patients get semi-elective sigmoid colectomy on the same admission**. *Caecal* — **younger** patients with a mobile caecum; the dilated caecum sits in the **LUQ** with an **SBO pattern behind it**. ⚑ **Straight to theatre** for **right hemicolectomy** — endoscopic decompression is unreliable.

**Other causes.** **Adhesions** — commonest SBO cause; risk rises with each laparotomy. **Gallstone ileus** — a stone erodes through a **cholecystoduodenal fistula** and impacts at the terminal ileum; **Rigler's triad = pneumobilia + SBO + an ectopic gallstone**; elderly women, treated by enterolithotomy. **Adult intussusception** — ⚑ unlike in children, **almost always has a pathological lead point** (tumour, polyp, lipoma, Meckel's), so **resect rather than simply reduce**. **Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie's)** — massive colonic dilatation **without mechanical obstruction** in the unwell, immobile or post-operative patient (orthopaedic and cardiac surgery, sepsis, electrolytes, opioids, anticholinergics). **CT first to exclude a mechanical cause**, then conservative measures (treat the precipitant, stop opioids/anticholinergics, correct K⁺/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺, NG, mobilise, flatus tube, serial caecal measurement); ⚑ **neostigmine 2 mg IV over ~3–5 minutes** if that fails at ~24–48 h, with **cardiac monitoring and atropine available**, contraindicated in mechanical obstruction, perforation, bradycardia, bronchospasm and renal impairment; then **colonoscopic decompression**, surgery last. ⚑ **Caecal diameter is the number: >9 cm defines it; >12 cm, or persistence beyond ~48–72 h, mandates escalation.**

## 3.6 Colorectal cancer — the surgical angle

**The short version**
- **Right-sided** → **iron-deficiency anaemia** and weight loss. **Left-sided** → altered bowel habit and obstruction. **Rectal** → fresh bleeding, **tenesmus**, urgency.
- ⚑ **IDA in any man or post-menopausal woman is colorectal cancer until proven otherwise.**
- ⚑ **NBCSP**: **iFOBT**, **2-yearly**, automatic for ages **50–74**; since **1 July 2024** those aged **45–49 may request** a first kit.
- **CEA is for monitoring, never diagnosis or screening**; **MRI rectum** drives neoadjuvant decisions, while colon cancer usually goes straight to resection.

**Presentation by site.** *Right colon/caecum* — wide lumen and liquid stool, so the tumour bleeds slowly rather than obstructing: **iron-deficiency anaemia**, weight loss, RIF mass, late presentation. *Left colon/sigmoid* — narrow lumen, formed stool, annular "apple-core" growth: **change in bowel habit**, **obstruction**, PR bleeding mixed with stool. *Rectum* — **fresh PR bleeding**, **tenesmus**, urgency, palpable on PR.

⚑ **Red flags mandating urgent referral (Australia).** Refer for **colonoscopy** (Optimal Care Pathway target: within about four weeks) for a **positive iFOBT** (never repeat it — scope it); **rectal bleeding**; **unexplained iron-deficiency anaemia**; **persistent change in bowel habit**; an **unexplained abdominal or rectal mass**; or **unexplained weight loss**.

**National Bowel Cancer Screening Program.** Free **iFOBT** home kit, **every 2 years**, mailed automatically to ages **50–74**. ⚑ From **1 July 2024** ages **45–49** are eligible but must **request** their first kit — they are not mailed one unsolicited — after which they are enrolled automatically. **A positive result means colonoscopy, not a repeat iFOBT.** It is not for symptomatic patients or those already on surveillance.

**Diagnosis and staging.** **Colonoscopy with biopsy** is the gold standard and clears the colon for synchronous tumours; if an obstructing tumour prevents completion, finish it **3–6 months post-operatively**. **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** for distant staging. **MRI rectum** is mandatory for rectal tumours — it defines **T stage, nodes, extramural venous invasion and the circumferential resection margin (CRM)**, and the CRM drives neoadjuvant decisions. ⚑ **CEA** — take a **pre-operative baseline** and use it for **surveillance**; it is neither sensitive nor specific, so never use it to diagnose or exclude. **MMR/MSI testing** is prognostic, guides immunotherapy and screens for Lynch.

**Operations by site.** Caecum/ascending → **right hemicolectomy**; hepatic flexure → **extended right hemicolectomy**; splenic flexure/descending → **left hemicolectomy**; sigmoid → **high anterior resection**; upper–mid rectum → **anterior resection with total mesorectal excision (TME)**; low rectum with sphincters spared → **low anterior resection + TME** ± defunctioning ileostomy; sphincters involved → **abdominoperineal resection with end colostomy**; obstructed left-sided → **Hartmann's** or a stent. Principles: adequate margins, **high vascular ligation with en-bloc lymphadenectomy (≥12 nodes)**, and **TME** — sharp dissection along the mesorectal fascial plane.

**Neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer.** The rectum sits in a bony pelvis with a tight CRM, so downstaging matters as it does not for colon cancer. **MRI-defined locally advanced disease (T3 with threatened CRM, T4, or node-positive)** receives **long-course chemoradiotherapy** (~5 weeks with a fluoropyrimidine radiosensitiser, surgery after ~8–12 weeks) or **short-course radiotherapy** (5 fractions). ⚑ **Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT)** is increasingly standard for high-risk disease, and complete clinical responders may be considered for **watch-and-wait**. **Colon cancer receives adjuvant, not neoadjuvant, chemotherapy.**

**Surveillance after curative resection.** Clinical review, **CEA**, imaging and colonoscopy for ~5 years. The Australian colonoscopy schedule is **1 year** post-resection (unless a complete perioperative colonoscopy was done), then **~6 years**, then **~11 years**.

**Hereditary syndromes.** **Lynch (HNPCC)** — autosomal dominant **MMR** mutation (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2); **few polyps but rapid adenoma–carcinoma progression**, **right-sided**, young onset, metachronous tumours, with extracolonic risk led by **endometrial** cancer, then ovarian, gastric and urothelial. eviQ: **colonoscopy 1–2 yearly from 25** (MLH1/MSH2) or **from 35** (MSH6/PMS2), **low-dose aspirin 100–300 mg daily actively considered**, and **risk-reducing hysterectomy ± BSO** after childbearing (~40; ~50 for PMS2). **FAP** — autosomal dominant **APC**; **hundreds to thousands** of adenomas from adolescence and **~100% cancer risk untreated**, plus duodenal cancer, desmoids and CHRPE. eviQ: **annual colonoscopy from age 12** (18–20 if attenuated) until colectomy, **upper GI surveillance from 25**, and **prophylactic proctocolectomy with IPAA or colectomy with IRA**, with lifelong surveillance of the retained rectum or pouch.

**Past exam questions.** *60-year-old, weeks of loose stools and intermittent PR bleeding* — change in bowel habit plus bleeding over 50 is a **red flag**: history, **rectal examination**, FBC, iron studies, **CEA baseline** and **urgent colonoscopy** — not an iFOBT, not laxatives. *65-year-old, change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss* — colorectal cancer until excluded; **IDA in a man or post-menopausal woman needs colonoscopy AND gastroscopy**, then staging CT, MRI if rectal, and MDT. The trap is treating the anaemia without scoping.

## 3.7 Mesenteric ischaemia

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Pain out of proportion to examination findings** — agonising pain with a soft abdomen in an arteriopath or a patient in AF.
- Four mechanisms: **embolic, thrombotic, non-occlusive (NOMI), venous**.
- **Lactate is a late marker** — a normal lactate does not exclude it.
- **Biphasic CT angiography** immediately: **each ~6 hours of delay roughly doubles mortality**, which is broadly 50%+ when delayed.

| Type | Share | Patient and key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Arterial embolic** | ~25% | **AF**, recent MI, valve disease. **Sudden**; lodges in the **SMA** just distal to the middle colic origin, sparing proximal jejunum |
| **Arterial thrombotic** | ~40% | Atherosclerosis, often preceded by **chronic mesenteric ischaemia**. Occludes at the **SMA origin** — large infarct territory |
| **NOMI** | ~25% | **Shock, vasopressors, sepsis, dialysis, cardiac failure** — vasoconstriction with patent vessels; insidious in the ICU patient. Treat the low-flow state |
| **Venous** | <10% | **Younger**; thrombophilia, malignancy, portal hypertension, OCP. Insidious over days, better prognosis, often **anticoagulation alone** |

Severe, poorly localised, constant central pain with early vomiting and diarrhoea, but an initially **soft, non-tender abdomen** because ischaemia is mucosal and has not reached the parietal peritoneum. **Peritonism, distension, shock and high lactate are LATE and mean transmural infarction.** Raised amylase can mislead toward pancreatitis. On **biphasic CTA**: arterial filling defects, wall thickening or a paper-thin wall, **absent mural enhancement**, mesenteric stranding, **pneumatosis intestinalis** and **portal venous gas**. ⚑ **Do not withhold contrast for renal impairment** here. Treat with resuscitation, antibiotics, heparin, correction of the low-flow state and **revascularisation** (endovascular where expertise exists, otherwise open embolectomy/bypass); resect necrotic bowel and use **damage control with a planned second look at 24–48 h** when viability is borderline.

**Chronic mesenteric ischaemia ("intestinal angina").** Atherosclerotic stenosis of ≥2 of coeliac/SMA/IMA causing **post-prandial epigastric pain 15–60 minutes after eating**, **food fear** and **weight loss** — often misattributed to malignancy and diagnosed only after a negative cancer workup. CTA/duplex confirms; treat with revascularisation (usually stenting) plus risk-factor modification. It is the substrate for acute thrombotic ischaemia.

## 3.8 Ischaemic colitis

**The short version**
- A **different disease** from acute mesenteric ischaemia: **colonic**, usually **non-occlusive**, far commoner, and usually settles conservatively.
- **Watershed zones**: **splenic flexure (Griffiths' point**, SMA/IMA junction) and **rectosigmoid (Sudeck's point)**; the rectum has dual supply and is usually spared.
- **Left-sided crampy pain then bloody diarrhoea within 24 hours**, patient comparatively well.

| | **Acute mesenteric ischaemia** | **Ischaemic colitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Vessel / bowel | SMA/coeliac ± venous, often **occlusive**; small bowel ± right colon | Colonic branches, usually **non-occlusive**; segmental colon at watershed zones |
| Pain / bleeding | **Severe, out of proportion**, central; bleeding late and ominous | Moderate, crampy, left-sided; **early bloody diarrhoea — relatively reassuring** |
| Systemic | Shocked, acidotic | Usually well |
| Management | **Emergency** revascularisation ± resection | **Conservative in ~80%** — bowel rest, IV fluids, optimise perfusion, stop vasoconstrictors |

CT shows segmental wall thickening in a vascular territory; **colonoscopy after the acute phase** confirms and excludes other colitides. Precipitants: hypotension, cardiac failure, **aortic surgery (IMA ligation)**, vasculitis, sickle cell disease, cocaine. **Surgery** (segmental colectomy with a stoma) only for gangrene, perforation, massive bleeding or failure to improve. Late complication: **ischaemic stricture**.

## 3.9 Ruptured/symptomatic AAA as a cause of abdominal pain

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Any patient over 50 with abdominal or back pain and hypotension has a ruptured AAA until excluded** — routinely mislabelled renal colic.
- Triad: **abdominal/back pain + hypotension + pulsatile expansile mass** (all three in a minority).
- **Unstable → theatre**, not the CT scanner; **stable → CT angiogram** to plan EVAR vs open repair. Bedside ultrasound confirms the aneurysm but will not reliably show the rupture.
- Use **permissive hypotension** (maintain cerebration and a radial pulse) until the aorta is clamped.

**→ See Section 7 (Vascular)** for screening, size thresholds, EVAR vs open repair and endoleak. What belongs here is the reflex: exclude it actively in any older patient with an acute abdomen, and image before accepting a first "renal colic" label over 50.

## 3.10 The acute abdomen in special groups

**The short version**
- **Pregnancy** — physiology masks everything; appendicitis is still the commonest surgical cause; US then MRI. **Elderly** — blunted signs, more dangerous causes, **low threshold for CT**.
- **Immunosuppressed/neutropenic** — minimal signs despite catastrophic pathology; think **typhlitis**.
- **Post-operative** — a new acute abdomen after surgery is an **anastomotic leak** until proven otherwise.

**Pregnancy.** Leucocytosis, tachycardia and nausea are normal, flattening every signal, and the gravid uterus lifts the omentum so contamination is poorly contained. **Appendicitis is the commonest non-obstetric surgical emergency**, then biliary disease. Involve obstetrics; consider ectopic, ovarian torsion, abruption and pre-eclampsia/HELLP (RUQ pain + hypertension + proteinuria). **US first, MRI second, CT only if essential.** ⚑ **Laparoscopy is safe in all trimesters**; necessary surgery should never be withheld on trimester grounds.

**Elderly.** Comorbidity, polypharmacy and low reserve obscure the picture; fever and leucocytosis are often absent, and **delirium or a fall may be the only presentation of an intra-abdominal catastrophe**. The differential shifts toward the dangerous — **mesenteric ischaemia, ruptured AAA, obstructing malignancy, perforated diverticulitis, volvulus, biliary sepsis**. Emergency laparotomy mortality in the frail is substantial; discuss goals of care early.

**Immunosuppressed and neutropenic.** Steroids, biologics, transplant immunosuppression, chemotherapy and advanced HIV suppress the response that produces physical signs — **peritonitis may present as nothing more than tachycardia and a rising CRP**, so treat the imaging, not the examination. **Neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis)**: RIF pain, fever and diarrhoea in a **neutropenic** patient ~7–14 days after chemotherapy, with **caecal and right colonic wall thickening** on CT. ⚑ **Management is medical** — bowel rest, IV fluids, **broad-spectrum antibiotics with anaerobic and Pseudomonas cover**, no antimotility agents; **surgery only for perforation, bleeding or deterioration**. Differential: appendicitis, *C. difficile*, CMV colitis.

**Post-operative patients.** **Anastomotic leak** — classically day 5–7: tachycardia, new AF, fever, prolonged ileus, low urine output or delirium precede peritonism. ⚑ **Unexplained persistent tachycardia after bowel surgery is a leak until disproven** — CT with contrast, not reassurance and review tomorrow. Distinguish **post-operative ileus** (diffuse, quiet, self-limiting) from **mechanical obstruction** (transition point on CT). Also consider collection, dehiscence, bleeding, *C. difficile*, Ogilvie's, MI and PE. Opioids and epidurals mask signs — trust trends in observations and lactate.

## 3.12 Abdominal compartment syndrome

**The short version**
- **Intra-abdominal hypertension = sustained IAP ≥12 mmHg**; **abdominal compartment syndrome = sustained IAP >20 mmHg WITH new organ dysfunction**.
- Measured via the bladder with the patient supine at end-expiration, zeroed at the mid-axillary line.
- Settings: massive fluid resuscitation, pancreatitis, ruptured AAA, major burns, damage-control laparotomy, bowel obstruction.
- Consequences: ↑ airway pressures, ↓ preload and cardiac output, oliguric AKI, ↓ splanchnic perfusion, ↑ ICP.
- Medical measures first — sedation and analgesia, neuromuscular blockade, NG and rectal decompression, drain ascites, avoid over-resuscitation, position <30°. **Decompressive laparotomy** if these fail.

# Surgery Notes — Section 4: Hernias, Stomas and the Abdominal Wall

*Sources: HerniaSurge International Guidelines for Groin Hernia Management (2018) and BJS Open 2023 update, European Hernia Society, RACS/Choosing Wisely Australia, Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic (Australian), WACHS Stoma Management Guideline and Australian stomal therapy nursing resources, standard surgical anatomy. Scope: groin and abdominal wall hernias, strangulation, stomas, enterocutaneous fistula, and the groin/abdominal wall lump.*

## 4.1 Inguinal canal anatomy

**The short version**
- ~4 cm oblique canal running **inferomedially** from **deep ring to superficial ring**, above the medial half of the inguinal ligament.
- **Deep ring** = defect in **transversalis fascia**, ~1.5 cm above the **mid-point of the inguinal ligament** (ASIS→pubic tubercle). **Superficial ring** = slit in **external oblique aponeurosis**, **superolateral to the pubic tubercle**.
- **Hesselbach's triangle**: inferior epigastric vessels laterally, lateral border of rectus medially, inguinal ligament inferiorly.
- **Inferior epigastric vessels define everything**: sac **lateral** = indirect, **medial** = direct.
- Contents: **spermatic cord** (♂) / **round ligament** (♀), plus the **ilioinguinal nerve** in both.

| Wall | Composition |
|---|---|
| **Anterior** | • External oblique aponeurosis throughout<br>• Internal oblique reinforcing laterally |
| **Posterior** | • Transversalis fascia throughout<br>• Conjoint tendon and arching fibres **reinforce it medially above**, leaving bare transversalis fascia in **Hesselbach's triangle (medial and inferior)** — ⚑ **that is where direct hernias emerge** |
| **Roof** | • Arching fibres of internal oblique and transversus ("shutter mechanism") |
| **Floor** | • Inguinal ligament (in-rolled external oblique)<br>• Lacunar ligament medially |

Obliquity matters: at rest the rings do not overlie one another, and on straining the roof descends onto the floor and shutters the deep ring — so raised pressure alone rarely herniates intact anatomy.

⚑ **Mid-point of the inguinal ligament** (ASIS→pubic *tubercle*) = deep ring, 1.5 cm above. **Mid-inguinal point** (ASIS→pubic *symphysis*) = femoral artery. Two different landmarks; a standing trap.

**Contents.** In the **male**, the **spermatic cord** — rule of threes: 3 arteries (testicular, cremasteric, artery to vas), 3 fascial coverings (external spermatic, cremasteric, internal spermatic), 3 others (vas deferens, pampiniform plexus, lymphatics), plus the genital branch of genitofemoral and sympathetics. In the **female**, the **round ligament of the uterus**. In **both**, the **ilioinguinal nerve** — it enters through the lateral wall, not the deep ring, exits the superficial ring, and supplies medial thigh and root of penis/anterior scrotum or mons/labium.

The **genital branch of genitofemoral** enters via the *deep* ring (cremaster + scrotal/labial skin); **iliohypogastric** runs above the canal. These three are the nerves at risk at open repair (§4.3).

**Hesselbach's triangle** — **lateral: inferior epigastric vessels; medial: lateral border of rectus/rectus sheath; inferior: inguinal ligament.** (The original description used the pectineal line inferiorly; either is acceptable if stated consistently.) Its floor is bare transversalis fascia without the arching-fibre reinforcement above — hence **direct** hernias here.

## 4.2 Hernia terminology

**The short version**
- **Reducible** → contents return. **Irreducible = incarcerated** → they don't, but perfusion is intact.
- **Obstructed** → lumen occluded (colic, vomiting, distension, absolute constipation); wall still perfused.
- **Strangulated** → **blood supply compromised**. Constant severe pain, exquisitely tender lump, systemic upset. Time-critical.
- **Richter's** = part of the bowel **circumference** only → ⚑ **strangulates without obstructing**; unwell patient still passing flatus with a soft abdomen.
- Incarceration is **mechanical**, strangulation **vascular**. All strangulated hernias are irreducible; most irreducible hernias are not strangulated.

| Term | Definition | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Reducible** | Contents return spontaneously or with pressure | Elective pathway |
| **Irreducible/incarcerated** | Cannot be reduced (adhesions, narrow neck, volume) | Perfusion intact. ⚑ **Only *chronic, painless* irreducibility is non-urgent — an ACUTELY irreducible hernia cannot be told from strangulation clinically and needs emergency assessment and usually urgent operation** |
| **Obstructed** | Lumen occluded within the sac | Colic, vomiting, distension, absolute constipation |
| **Strangulated** | Venous then arterial occlusion → necrosis, perforation, sepsis | **Emergency**; never reduce |
| **Richter's** | Antimesenteric part of circumference only | Narrow-necked defects: femoral, obturator, port sites |
| **Littre's** | Sac contains a **Meckel's diverticulum** | May strangulate or fistulate |
| **Sliding** | A **retroperitoneal viscus forms part of the sac wall** (sigmoid, caecum, bladder, ovary) | Never open the sac blindly |
| **Maydl's** | "W" loop — the **intra-abdominal connecting loop** strangulates | Inspect inside the abdomen or you miss dead bowel |
| **Pantaloon / Amyand's** | Direct *and* indirect sacs straddling the vessels / sac contains the **appendix** | Repair whole myopectineal orifice / ± appendicitis |
| **Reduction en masse** | Reduced *with* its constricting neck | Still strangulated, now unseen — why force is dangerous |

## 4.3 Inguinal hernia

**The short version**
- **Lateral to inferior epigastrics = indirect; medial = direct.**
- **Indirect** ~2/3, congenital (patent processus), any age, may reach the scrotum, narrow neck → higher strangulation risk.
- **Direct** = acquired transversalis weakness, older, wide neck, rarely scrotal, low strangulation risk.
- **Pubic tubercle**: inguinal neck **superomedial**, femoral neck **inferolateral**.
- ⚑ 2023 HerniaSurge **recommends laparo-endoscopic (TEP/TAPP) repair for primary unilateral hernia where expertise exists** — less acute and chronic pain than Lichtenstein; mesh for the majority.

| Feature | **Indirect** | **Direct** |
|---|---|---|
| Inferior epigastric vessels | **Lateral** to them | **Medial** to them |
| Route | Deep ring → canal → superficial ring | Straight through **Hesselbach's triangle** |
| Aetiology | **Congenital** — patent processus vaginalis | **Acquired** — fascial degeneration, raised intra-abdominal pressure |
| Age | Any; commonest in children/young adults | Older adults |
| Relation to cord | **Within** the cord | **Outside** the cord, pushes it aside |
| Into scrotum | **Yes** (inguinoscrotal) | Rarely — broad-based bulge |
| Neck | Narrow | Wide |
| Strangulation | **Higher** | Low |
| Deep ring occlusion test | Classically controlled by pressure | Classically still bulges medially |
| At operation | Sac from the deep ring, adherent to cord, dissected off vas/vessels | Diffuse attenuated transversalis bulge medial to the vessels, easily inverted |

⚑ **The deep ring occlusion test is unreliable** — accuracy against operative findings is little better than chance. Examination establishes *that* a hernia exists (sens ~0.75, spec ~0.96) but not reliably *which type* — and it doesn't matter, since a mesh repair covering the myopectineal orifice treats both. Know the test *and* the caveat.

### Past exam stem — "25-year-old, groin lump appearing on standing, reducing on lying flat"

- **Reducible** (gravity-dependent, disappears supine) → no incarceration or strangulation → **elective** problem.
- **Age 25** → strongly favours **indirect** from a patent processus vaginalis.
- **Examine standing**: find the **neck** and relate it to the **pubic tubercle** (inguinal superomedial, femoral inferolateral); cough impulse; can you get above it (no in inguinoscrotal hernia, yes in hydrocele); testis palpable separately?
- **Investigations**: none routinely — clinical diagnosis. If equivocal (occult hernia, obesity, athletic groin pain), **ultrasound** first, CT/MRI for genuine doubt.
- **Answer: reducible indirect inguinal hernia → elective repair.** Watchful waiting is legitimate only for **asymptomatic/minimally symptomatic men**, and even then ~**50% cross over to surgery by ~7 years, ~68% by 10 years**, mostly for pain. A 25-year-old has decades of hernia-years ahead — repair.

**Risk factors.** Male; age; family history; previous contralateral repair; chronic cough/COPD; straining (constipation, BPH); heavy lifting; smoking and collagen disorders; prior lower abdominal incision; ascites/peritoneal dialysis; AAA. Low BMI for inguinal; obesity for incisional/umbilical.

### Differential of a groin lump

| Diagnosis | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Femoral hernia** | Neck **inferolateral** to pubic tubercle; small, tense, often irreducible |
| **Lymph node** | Multiple, discrete, no cough impulse, not reducible; seek distal/systemic source |
| **Saphena varix** | Soft, bluish, **vanishes lying flat**, cough thrill, varicose veins; at the SFJ |
| **Femoral artery aneurysm** | **Expansile** (not merely transmitted) pulsation |
| **Psoas abscess** | Swelling below the inguinal ligament, spinal/hip pain, fever; TB or Crohn's |
| **Lipoma of the cord** | Soft, no cough impulse, irreducible; often an operative diagnosis |
| **Undescended/ectopic testis** | **Empty ipsilateral hemiscrotum** — always check |
| **Hydrocele of the cord** | Transilluminates, irreducible, moves with traction on the testis |

### Management

- **Watchful waiting** for asymptomatic/minimally symptomatic men; emergencies are uncommon. Counsel crossover and red flags. ⚑ **Never** for femoral hernias, symptomatic hernias, or women.
- **Tension-free mesh repair** is standard.

| Approach | Preferred when |
|---|---|
| **Open Lichtenstein** | Local/regional anaesthesia needed (frail, cardiorespiratory disease); large inguinoscrotal hernia; hostile pre-peritoneal space (prostatectomy, pelvic radiotherapy, prior mesh); limited laparoscopic expertise; emergency groin approach |
| **Laparo-endoscopic TEP/TAPP** | **Bilateral**; **recurrence after open repair**; ⚑ **women** (avoids missing a femoral hernia, less chronic pain); early return to manual work; primary unilateral with expertise |
| **Non-mesh (Shouldice)** | Only with expertise and shared decision-making — mesh declined, contaminated field |

⚑ **Therapeutic Guidelines lists hernia repair "with or without prosthetic material" as warranting prophylaxis — cefazolin 2 g IV**, with metronidazole added only if bowel lumen entry is expected (Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic; HerniaSurge) — reserve it for high-risk patients or contaminated fields.

**Complications.** Early: haematoma/seroma, urinary retention, wound infection, injury to vas/bowel/bladder, ischaemic orchitis (tender swollen testis days later, may end in atrophy). Recurrence low with mesh.

⚑ **Chronic post-herniorrhaphy groin pain (CPIP)** is the dominant modern outcome: **clinically significant in ~10–12%**, **debilitating in ~0.5–6%**. Neuropathic (nerve caught in suture, mesh or scar) or nociceptive (mesh inflammation, "meshoma", periosteal suture at the pubic tubercle). **Nerves at risk: ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, genital branch of genitofemoral.** HerniaSurge recommends **identifying all three** at open repair; routine prophylactic neurectomy is **not** recommended, pragmatic resection of a damaged nerve is. Strongest predictors: pre-operative pain, young age. Treat with multimodal/neuropathic analgesia, blocks and pain service; neurectomy or mesh removal only in specialist units.

## 4.4 Femoral hernia

**The short version**
- ~**3% of groin hernias**, ~**4× commoner in women** — but inguinal hernia is still the commonest groin hernia *in* women.
- ⚑ **Highest strangulation risk of any groin hernia — ~15–20%** — because the ring is narrow and rigid.
- Neck **inferolateral to the pubic tubercle**; often small, tense, no cough impulse — mistaken for a node.
- ⚑ **Never watchful waiting** — repair promptly at diagnosis; timely mesh repair, laparo-endoscopic where expertise exists.

The **femoral canal** is the medial compartment of the femoral sheath (~1.25–2 cm), containing fat and **Cloquet's node**, and exists to let the femoral vein distend. Its upper opening is the **femoral ring**:

| Border | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Anterior** | Inguinal ligament |
| **Posterior** | **Pectineal (Cooper's) ligament** |
| **Medial** | **Lacunar ligament** |
| **Lateral** | **Femoral vein** |

The sac descends through the ring, exits the **saphenous opening**, then turns **up and forwards over the inguinal ligament** — so a large one can appear to lie above it. **Judge by the neck, not the fundus.** Two ligaments and bone make an unyielding ring: hence the strangulation rate and the frequency of **Richter's** hernias here. ⚑ An aberrant obturator artery (**corona mortis**) — an obturator-to-external-iliac/inferior-epigastric anastomosis — crosses the **superior pubic ramus behind the pectineal (Cooper's) ligament**, and is at risk when the lacunar ligament is divided or tacks are placed on Cooper's ligament.

### Past exam stem — "62-year-old man, tender irreducible groin lump, vomiting, abdominal distension"

- Tender + irreducible + **vomiting and distension** = **complicated groin hernia causing small bowel obstruction**; tenderness raises **strangulation**.
- **Most likely a femoral hernia**, despite the patient being male — complicated, obstructing, first-presentation groin hernias are disproportionately femoral (narrow rigid ring). Confirm the **neck inferolateral to the pubic tubercle**. Main alternative: incarcerated indirect inguinal hernia.
- **Immediate management** (in parallel, not sequentially): **A–E**, IV access, FBC/U&E/CRP/**lactate**/VBG, group & hold; **aggressive resuscitation** (profoundly volume-deplete from vomiting and third-spacing), catheter and urine output; **NBM + NG tube** on free drainage, analgesia and antiemetics; ⚑ **do NOT attempt reduction** (risks returning dead bowel, or *en masse* reduction with the ring); **IV antibiotics** covering enteric Gram-negatives and anaerobes per **Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic**; correct electrolytes; VTE prophylaxis; **urgent surgical referral for emergency repair** (CT only if genuinely uncertain and it won't delay theatre); consent including **bowel resection and possible stoma**.
- **In theatre**: low (Lockwood) approach electively; **high/pre-peritoneal (McEvedy)** favoured in the emergency for bowel access. Reduce, assess viability, resect non-viable bowel, repair — mesh if clean, suture if contaminated.

## 4.5 Other abdominal wall hernias

**The short version**
- **Paediatric umbilical hernias: >90% close spontaneously, most by 2 years and the great majority by 5** — repair at **4–5 years** if persistent, or if large/complicated. Incarceration risk very low.
- **Adult para-umbilical** hernias never resolve, have a narrow fibrous neck → repair.
- **Incisional hernia** is the commonest iatrogenic hernia; **wound dehiscence** is its acute in-hospital counterpart.
- **Obturator hernia** — thin elderly multiparous woman with SBO and the **Howship–Romberg sign**; diagnosed on **CT**.
- **Divarication of recti is not a hernia** — stretched linea alba, no fascial defect, no neck, cannot strangulate.

| Hernia | Features |
|---|---|
| **Umbilical (paediatric)** | Through the umbilical ring; preterm, Afro-Caribbean, trisomy 21, congenital hypothyroidism. **>90% close by 5 y**; **<1 cm** almost always closes, **≥1.5 cm** often doesn't. ⚑ Don't refer routinely before 4–5 y; taping is ineffective |
| **Para-umbilical (adult)** | Defect *adjacent* to the ring; obesity, multiparity, ascites. Narrow neck → **incarceration risk** → repair, mesh if larger |
| **Epigastric** | Linea alba **above** the umbilicus; small, pre-peritoneal fat, painful out of proportion to size, may be multiple |
| **Incisional** | Through a previous wound. Risks: **wound infection**, emergency surgery, midline incision, obesity, smoking, diabetes, malnutrition, steroids, chronic cough, poor closure. Prevent with small-bites continuous slowly-absorbable closure (suture:wound ≥4:1). Repair usually mesh |
| **Wound dehiscence** | Acute closure failure classically **day 7–10** (§2.4), heralded by **serosanguinous "salmon-pink" discharge**; full-thickness = **burst abdomen** — saline-soaked gauze over bowel, resuscitate, theatre. Same risks as incisional hernia, different timescale |
| **Spigelian** | Through the **semilunar line** at the lateral rectus border, usually **below the arcuate line**. **Interparietal** — deep to intact external oblique, so often impalpable; high incarceration risk; **US/CT** diagnoses |
| **Obturator** | Through the obturator canal. **"Little old lady's hernia"** — unexplained **SBO**. **Howship–Romberg sign**: medial thigh/knee pain on hip **extension, adduction or internal rotation**. CT diagnostic; high morbidity from delay |
| **Lumbar** | **Superior (Grynfeltt)** or **inferior (Petit)** triangle; primary, or after flank incision/iliac crest harvest |
| **Parastomal** | Around a stoma trephine; very common (§4.7) |
| **Divarication of recti** | **Not a hernia** — widened linea alba, separated rectus bellies, midline ridge on sitting up. **No defect → no neck → cannot obstruct or strangulate.** Reassure + physiotherapy; surgery cosmetic only |

## 4.6 Strangulation — recognition and immediate management

**The short version**
- **Constant severe pain** (not colic) + **tense tender irreducible lump** + **loss of cough impulse** = strangulation until disproved.
- **Overlying skin change** — erythema → dusky discolouration → oedema — means ischaemia has reached the sac and wall: late and ominous.
- **Systemic signs** (tachycardia, fever, hypotension, raised lactate/WCC, peritonism) mark necrosis and sepsis, not an observable hernia.
- ⚑ **Never attempt to reduce a strangulated hernia.**
- **Resuscitate and operate**; investigations must not delay theatre.

The usual sequence is irreducible → obstructed → strangulated, but **Richter's** short-circuits it: pain, tenderness and systemic upset with flatus still passing. Expect leucocytosis and **rising lactate** (a normal lactate does not exclude early strangulation). Erect CXR if perforation suspected; contrast CT only if genuinely uncertain.

**Immediate management.** A–E; large-bore access; aggressive crystalloid; NBM + **NG decompression**; catheter and hourly urine output; analgesia; bloods, VBG/lactate, group & hold; **IV antibiotics per Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic**; correct electrolytes and coagulopathy; early anaesthetic and surgical review; consent covering **bowel resection and possible stoma**.

**Operative.** ⚑ Expose and open the sac, **grasp and hold the strangulated contents FIRST, and only then divide the constricting ring** — release it first and necrotic bowel reduces into the abdomen unseen (the same failure mode as reduction en masse). Assess viability by **colour, peristalsis, sheen and mesenteric arterial pulsation** after warming with saline-soaked packs. **Viable** → reduce and repair. **Doubtful** → re-warm, re-inspect, consider a planned **second look at 24–48 h**. **Non-viable** → **resect**; primary anastomosis if stable and clean, **stoma** or damage control if unstable or contaminated.

Mesh is acceptable in a clean field; ⚑ in a **contaminated** field prefer suture repair or biological/absorbable mesh over permanent synthetic mesh. Always inspect proximally for **Maydl's** before closing.

## 4.7 Stomas

**The short version**
- **Ileostomy: RIF, spouted, liquid enzyme-rich output, high volume.** **Colostomy: LIF, flush, formed faeces, low volume.** The spout delivers corrosive small bowel effluent clear of the skin.
- **Loop** = defunctioning/reversible; **end** = definitive; **double-barrel** = two ends brought out.
- **Loop ileostomy defunctions a low anterior resection; end colostomy follows Hartmann's; end ileostomy follows panproctocolectomy; ileal conduit = urostomy.**
- ⚑ **High output stoma ≈ >1500 mL/24 h on 2 consecutive days** → dehydration, Na⁺/Mg²⁺/K⁺ loss, **AKI**.
- Every stoma patient needs a **stomal therapy nurse**; pre-operative siting is a quality standard.

| Feature | **Ileostomy** | **Colostomy** |
|---|---|---|
| Site | **Right iliac fossa** | **Left iliac fossa** (transverse loop sits upper abdomen) |
| Profile | **Spouted, 2–3 cm** | **Flush** or minimally protruding |
| Why | Effluent is **enzyme-rich and alkaline** — must reach the appliance without touching skin | Formed, faeculent, non-enzymatic, far less corrosive |
| Output character | Liquid → porridge, green/khaki | Semi-formed → formed brown faeces |
| Volume | ~**500–1000 mL/24 h** adapted (initially up to 1500–2000) | ~200–600 mL/24 h; may not act daily |
| Contents | Small bowel chyme, **sodium**-rich | Colonic faeces |
| Metabolic risk | **Dehydration, Na⁺/Mg²⁺/K⁺ loss, AKI**, gallstones, renal stones | Low |
| Other issues | Food-bolus obstruction; rehydration education | Constipation, flatus, odour |

**Configuration.** **End** = single lumen, distal bowel closed off or removed (Hartmann's, APR, panproctocolectomy). **Loop** = a loop over a rod opened antimesenterically, giving functioning + mucous limbs — used to **defunction** a distal anastomosis, easily reversed. **Double-barrel** = two separate ends adjacent (Paul–Mikulicz; when anastomosis is unsafe). **Mucous fistula** = defunctioned distal limb brought out separately, draining mucus.

| Operation | Stoma |
|---|---|
| **Hartmann's** (sigmoid resection, stump closed) | **End colostomy**, LIF — reversible later |
| **Low anterior resection** | **Defunctioning loop ileostomy** — protects the anastomosis; reversed ~3–6 months |
| **Abdominoperineal resection** | **Permanent end colostomy** |
| **Panproctocolectomy** (UC, FAP) | **Permanent end ileostomy** |
| **Subtotal colectomy** (severe colitis) | **End ileostomy** ± mucous fistula; rectal stump retained |
| **Cystectomy** | **Ileal conduit (urostomy)** — spouted, RIF, urine with mucus |

**Temporary vs permanent.** Temporary stomas protect a distal anastomosis or divert while sepsis settles; reversal at **~3–6 months** once the patient has recovered, adjuvant therapy is settled and the anastomosis is shown intact (contrast enema/flexible sigmoidoscopy). Many are never reversed — say so at consent.

| Complication | Features and management |
|---|---|
| **High output** (>1500 mL/24 h × 2 days) | → **hyponatraemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia, AKI**, malnutrition, skin damage. **Exclude a cause first**: sepsis/collection, obstruction, *C. difficile*, drug withdrawal, short bowel, recurrent disease. Then **restrict hypotonic fluid (~500–1000 mL/day)**, **glucose–electrolyte ORS with Na⁺ ≥90 mmol/L**, **loperamide before meals** (high doses often needed), **PPI**, ± codeine; IV fluid and Mg²⁺/K⁺ replacement; dietitian + stomal therapy nurse; octreotide/parenteral support if refractory |
| **Retraction** | Sinks below skin (tension, obesity, ischaemia) → leakage; convex appliance, belt; refashion if severe |
| **Prolapse** | Telescoping bowel; commonest in **loop transverse colostomy**. Reduce gently (cold compress, topical sugar for oedema); surgery if irreducible or ischaemic |
| **Parastomal hernia** | Very common, especially end colostomy; time, obesity, oversized trephine. Support garment; repair for obstruction, pain or appliance failure |
| **Stenosis** | Post-ischaemic/septic narrowing at skin or fascia; ribbon output, colic; dilate or refashion |
| **Ischaemia/necrosis** | Dusky/black stoma early post-op. Test tube + light: mucosal only → observe; **below the fascia → theatre** |
| **Skin excoriation** | Commonest problem — leakage, poor siting or fit, absent spout. Stomal therapy review, barrier products |
| **Obstruction** | Food bolus (nuts, sweetcorn, dried fruit), adhesions, parastomal hernia, stenosis. Colic + watery output, then nil; NBM, IV fluids, NG |
| **Psychosocial** | Body image, sexual function, odour/noise anxiety, withdrawal, depression, work — drives quality of life more than the technical result |

⚑ **Pre-operative siting by a stomal therapy nurse** — marked sitting, standing and lying, clear of belt line, scars, creases, bony prominences and umbilicus, on a flat 5–7 cm area in the patient's sight — is the most effective preventer of appliance failure. Australian patients get ongoing supplies via the **Stoma Appliance Scheme** through a registered stoma association.

## 4.8 Fistulae

**The short version**
- **Fistula** = abnormal communication between **two epithelialised (or endothelialised) surfaces**; a **sinus** is a blind-ending tract to a surface.
- **Enterocutaneous fistula (ECF)** is most often **post-operative** (anastomotic leak, enterotomy); otherwise Crohn's, malignancy, radiation, diverticulitis, trauma.
- **Output**: low **<200**, moderate 200–500, **high >500 mL/24 h**.
- **FRIEND** = the factors that prevent spontaneous closure.
- **Sepsis first, nutrition and skin next, surgery last** — definitive repair deferred **≥3 months** (often 6–12).

| Letter | Factor |
|---|---|
| **F** | **Foreign body** in the tract (mesh, suture, drain) |
| **R** | **Radiation** — irradiated bowel will not heal |
| **I** | **Inflammation/IBD (Crohn's)** or ongoing **infection** |
| **E** | **Epithelialisation** of the tract |
| **N** | **Neoplasm** at the fistula site |
| **D** | **Distal obstruction** — the fistula stays the path of least resistance |
| *(HIS…)* **H** | **High output** |
| *(HIS…)* **I** | **Intestinal disruption** — >50% of bowel circumference involved |
| *(HIS…)* **S** | **Short tract (<2 cm)**, and short segment of diseased bowel |

**Management ("SNAP").**
1. **Sepsis control first** — nothing heals around undrained sepsis. CT for collections, percutaneous or operative drainage, culture-directed antibiotics per **Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic**. This is what changes mortality.
2. **Nutrition, fluid and electrolytes** — **enteral where feasible** (including fistuloclysis), **parenteral** if proximal or high output; monitor albumin and trace elements.
3. **Anatomy** — once stable: **fistulogram, contrast CT or MRI** for origin, tract length, distal obstruction, residual bowel length. Planning, not urgent.
4. **Skin and effluent control** — stomal therapy input, appliances/wound managers, negative-pressure therapy, barrier films.
5. **Reduce output** — PPI, loperamide/codeine, ± octreotide (modest).
6. **Definitive surgery late** — most fistulae that will close do so within weeks of sepsis control and nutrition. Operate only after **≥3 months**, in a specialist unit.

## 4.9 Abdominal wall and groin lumps — a framework

**The short version**
- Three questions: **where is it, does it reduce or have a cough impulse, and is it in the skin, in the wall, or coming through it?**
- Reducible with an expansile cough impulse = **hernia** until disproved.
- A groin lump in a woman is a **femoral hernia** until disproved — and it is not watched.
- Tender + irreducible + systemically unwell = **strangulation**: stop examining and resuscitate.

| Layer | Diagnoses |
|---|---|
| **Skin/subcutaneous** | Sebaceous cyst, lipoma, abscess, skin malignancy |
| **Within the wall** | Rectus sheath haematoma (anticoagulated; **Fothergill's sign** — mass persists on tensing, does not cross the midline), desmoid, endometrioma (cyclical pain in a caesarean scar), sarcoma, divarication of recti |
| **Through the wall** | Inguinal, femoral, umbilical/para-umbilical, epigastric, incisional, Spigelian, lumbar, parastomal, obturator |
| **Intra-abdominal** | Organomegaly, tumour, aneurysm, faecal loading — moves with respiration, not reducible |
| **Groin-specific** | See §4.3 |

**Carnett's test** separates wall from viscus: mark the tender point, then have the patient tense the wall (lift head/legs). **Worse or unchanged = abdominal wall**; **better = intra-abdominal**.

# Surgery Notes — Section 5: Trauma and Burns
*Sources: EMST/ATLS 10th ed (RACS), ANZBA/EMSB and NSW ACI burns guidance, National Blood Authority PBM Guidelines Module 1, CRASH-2 and CRASH-3, Canadian CT Head Rule, Canadian C-Spine Rule, NEXUS, ANZCOR. Scope: written-finals depth on resuscitation of the injured patient — primary and secondary survey, thoracic and haemorrhagic emergencies, head and spine injury, abdominopelvic trauma, burns, compartment syndrome, crush injury.*

## 5.1 The primary survey

**The short version**
- **<C>ABCDE**: catastrophic external haemorrhage first (tourniquet/packing), then Airway with C-spine control, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure.
- **Treat what you find before moving to the next letter** — a tension pneumothorax found at "B" is decompressed there, not after "C".
- **Noisy breathing is obstructed breathing.** Gurgling = liquid → suction. Snoring = tongue → jaw thrust and adjunct. Stridor = laryngeal → definitive airway early.
- Reassess from the top whenever the patient deteriorates.
- Secondary survey (head-to-toe plus **AMPLE**) begins only once the primary survey is complete and the patient is not deteriorating.

**<C> — catastrophic haemorrhage.** Exsanguinating compressible limb bleeding kills faster than an obstructed airway: direct pressure, wound packing, proximal tourniquet tightened until bleeding stops (note the time). ⚑ Tourniquets are standard Australian prehospital equipment; the teaching that they inevitably cost the limb is obsolete.

**A — Airway with C-spine protection.** Ask a question — a clear answer in a normal voice means patent airway, ventilation and cerebral perfusion. Look for blood, vomit, teeth, foreign body. Escalate: suction → jaw thrust (not head tilt) → oro/nasopharyngeal airway → supraglottic device → **definitive airway** by RSI with manual in-line stabilisation; cricothyroidotomy is the failed-airway escape. Indications: apnoea, failure to maintain patency, **GCS ≤8**, aspiration risk, expanding neck haematoma, facial burns/inhalation injury, predicted deterioration in transfer.

> **Exam point.** Gurgling is an **airway** problem, not a breathing problem. The answer is *suction the airway*, then reassess and secure it — before oxygen titration, IV access or imaging. Gurgling means fluid the patient cannot clear: the immediate precursor to aspiration.

**B — Breathing.** Expose the chest: inspect (asymmetry, wounds, flail segment, tracheal shift), palpate (surgical emphysema), percuss (hyper-resonant vs stony dull), auscultate. High-flow O₂, SpO₂, capnography.

**C — Circulation.** Control bleeding; find where it has gone — **"on the floor and four more": chest, abdomen, pelvis/retroperitoneum, long bones**. Two large-bore (14–16G) cannulae, IO if access fails; crossmatch, VBG/lactate, coags, β-hCG. Activate MTP early.

**D — Disability.** GCS, pupils, lateralising signs, **BSL** (hypoglycaemia mimics head injury). **E — Exposure.** Undress fully, log-roll, then **actively rewarm** — hypothermia is one third of the lethal triad and usually iatrogenic.

**Adjuncts:** ECG/monitoring, oximetry, capnography, ABG/VBG, urinary catheter (contraindicated with suspected urethral injury — blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal haematoma), gastric tube (orogastric if base-of-skull fracture suspected), **chest and pelvis radiographs**, **eFAST**.

**Secondary survey.** Head-to-toe: scalp, tympanic membranes, face, neck, chest, abdomen, perineum, PR/PV where indicated, four limbs, log-roll with spinal palpation, full neurological exam. Plus **AMPLE** — **A**llergies, **M**edications (anticoagulants especially), **P**ast history/pregnancy, **L**ast meal, **E**vents. Mechanism predicts pattern: speed, restraint, ejection, fall height, intrusion.

## 5.2 Immediately life-threatening chest injuries

**The short version**
- Six found at "B": **tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, flail chest with pulmonary contusion, cardiac tamponade, tracheobronchial disruption**.
- **Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis — decompress before imaging.** Requesting a CXR here is a wrong answer.
- ⚑ Adult needle decompression site is now the **5th ICS, just anterior to the mid-axillary line**, not 2nd ICS mid-clavicular.
- Massive haemothorax: **>1500 mL immediately** on ICC, or **>200 mL/hr for 2–4 hrs** → thoracotomy.
- Tamponade: hypotension + distended neck veins + muffled heart sounds; FAST confirms, surgery treats.

| Injury | Clinical signature | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| Tension pneumothorax | • Distress, hypoxia<br>• **Absent** breath sounds + **hyper-resonant**<br>• Distended neck veins, hypotension<br>• Tracheal deviation away (late) | Needle decompression → **ICC** |
| Open ("sucking") wound | Audible air movement through defect | Dressing taped on **three sides**, then ICC at a *separate* site |
| Massive haemothorax | • Absent breath sounds + **stony dull**<br>• Shock, **flat** neck veins | **Blood** concurrently with ICC; thoracotomy per volume criteria |
| Flail chest / contusion | ≥2 ribs in ≥2 places, paradoxical segment; hypoxia from the contusion | Analgesia (regional blocks), O₂, careful fluid, ventilatory support |
| Cardiac tamponade | **Beck's triad**; pulsus paradoxus; PEA arrest | FAST subxiphoid → **surgical decompression**; pericardiocentesis only a bridge |

**Tension pneumothorax — the site change.** ⚑ EMST/ATLS 10th ed moved adult needle decompression from 2nd ICS mid-clavicular to the **5th ICS, just anterior to the mid-axillary line** (the safe-triangle position; some sources say 4th–5th, but 5th is the examinable number). The reason is mechanical: a standard 4.5 cm cannula fails to reach pleura anteriorly in a substantial minority of adults, whereas the lateral wall is thinner and is where the ICC is going anyway. Use a **long (≥5 cm, ideally 8 cm) large-bore cannula**. Decompression is temporising and often fails — **definitive treatment is an ICC**; peri-arrest, finger thoracostomy is faster and more reliable. The anterior route remains acceptable if the lateral chest is inaccessible. Paediatrics: 2nd ICS mid-clavicular still standard.

> **Exam point — the talking patient with reduced breath sounds.** That the patient is *talking* is data: air is moving and the brain is perfused, so this is not peri-arrest tension. Discriminate on percussion and haemodynamics:
> - **Hyper-resonant + normal BP + veins not distended** → simple pneumothorax → ICC, no needle.
> - **Hyper-resonant + hypotension + raised JVP + tracheal shift + escalating distress** → tension → decompress now, no CXR.
> - **Stony dull + hypotension + flat neck veins** → haemothorax.
> Between the two shocked pictures the discriminator is **percussion note plus neck veins**: tension obstructs venous return (veins up), haemothorax empties the tank (veins down). A tension patient who is also hypovolaemic may have flat veins — percussion then decides.

## 5.3 Haemorrhagic shock

**The short version**
- ⚑ **BP is a late sign — it does not fall until class III (~30–40% loss).** Tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure and anxiety come first.
- **Pulse pressure narrows in class II** — the earliest reliable marker.
- Young, fit patients and pregnant women compensate hard then crash; a normal BP in a 20-year-old does not exclude litres of loss.
- ⚑ Australian practice: **permissive hypotension, minimal crystalloid, early balanced blood products (MTP), TXA within 3 hrs.**
- Fix the **lethal triad**: hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy.

| | Class I | Class II | Class III | Class IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood loss | <750 mL (<15%) | 750–1500 mL (15–30%) | 1500–2000 mL (30–40%) | >2000 mL (>40%) |
| Heart rate | <100 | 100–120 | 120–140 | >140 or bradycardia pre-arrest |
| Blood pressure | Normal | **Normal** | **Decreased** | Decreased/unrecordable |
| Pulse pressure | Normal | **Narrowed** | Narrowed | Narrowed |
| Resp rate | 14–20 | 20–30 | 30–40 | >35 |
| Urine output | >30 mL/hr | 20–30 mL/hr | 5–15 mL/hr | Negligible |
| Mental state | Slightly anxious | Mildly anxious | Anxious, **confused** | Confused, **lethargic** |

⚑ The 10th edition cautions that this is a teaching framework, not a diagnostic instrument — beta-blockade, pacemakers, age, hypothermia, alcohol and analgesia decouple pulse and pressure from volume state. Treat the trend, lactate and base deficit.

**Compensation masks loss.** ⚑ A fit 20-year-old holds BP by vasoconstriction until sudden decompensation; in **pregnancy** plasma volume rises ~40–50%, so 1.5 L can be lost before maternal BP moves — foetal distress is often the first sign.

**Damage control resuscitation — three pillars.** (1) **Permissive hypotension**: accept a lower BP (palpable radial pulse, systolic ~80–90 mmHg) until surgical haemostasis, so clot is not dislodged. ⚑ **Exception: traumatic brain injury**, where cerebral perfusion must be protected. (2) **Minimal crystalloid** — ⚑ the old "2 L for everyone" is gone; it dilutes clotting factors, worsens acidosis and cools the patient. (3) **Balanced blood products** — red cells, FFP and platelets in roughly equal ratio via the local **MTP**, fibrinogen/cryoprecipitate guided by level or ROTEM/TEG, plus **ionised calcium** (citrate chelation impairs clotting and contractility).

**Tranexamic acid.** ⚑ Standard of care in Australian major trauma with significant haemorrhage. **CRASH-2**: **1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 hours**, started **within 3 hours of injury** — beyond 3 hours it increased death from bleeding, so the window is examinable both ways. **CRASH-3** enrolled isolated TBI with **GCS ≤12 or intracranial bleeding on CT**, within 3 hours; ⚑ benefit was found in the **GCS 9–15 subgroup**, which is the basis of the practice recommendation — do not quote 9–15 as the trial entry criterion. Some retrieval services use a simplified 2 g bolus — quote CRASH-2 unless told otherwise.

**Lethal triad:** hypothermia → acidosis → coagulopathy, each worsening the others. Countermeasures: fluid/blood warmers, active warming, minimal exposure, correct calcium, fast haemostasis (damage-control laparotomy — pack, control, temporary closure, return once corrected).

**Pelvic binder.** For suspected pelvic fracture with instability, at the level of the **greater trochanters** (not the iliac crests — a common error). Do not spring the pelvis repeatedly; it disrupts clot. Definitive control: angioembolisation or preperitoneal packing ± external fixation.

## 5.4 FAST and imaging choices

**The short version**
- FAST answers one question: **is there free fluid?** Four views — hepatorenal (Morison's), splenorenal, pelvic, subxiphoid pericardial.
- **Positive** FAST in an **unstable** patient = theatre. A **negative** FAST **excludes nothing**.
- FAST misses retroperitoneum, bowel, diaphragm, and solid-organ injury without free fluid.
- **eFAST** adds anterior thoracic views — pneumothorax (loss of lung sliding) and pleural fluid.
- **Unstable = theatre. Stable = CT.** "Too unstable for CT" is a real, examinable category.

The rule carrying most marks: **unstable + positive abdominal FAST → laparotomy**. Unstable with a **negative** FAST means excluding the other sites (chest and pelvic films, external, long bones) ± diagnostic peritoneal aspiration. **Haemodynamically normal, or a transient responder who stabilises** → whole-body **CT trauma series**: more sensitive, grades solid-organ injury for non-operative management, images the retroperitoneum, shows contrast blush for embolisation. A CT scanner is a dangerous place for a bleeding patient.

## 5.5 Head injury

**The short version**
- **Primary** injury happens at impact and cannot be undone; **secondary** injury (hypoxia, hypotension, hypercapnia, hyperthermia, seizure, raised ICP) is what you treat.
- Avoid the two great killers: **SpO₂ <90%** and **systolic <90 mmHg** — one hypotensive episode roughly doubles mortality.
- **Monro–Kellie**: fixed box; compensation by CSF and venous displacement is finite, then ICP rises steeply.
- **Cushing's reflex** (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiration) is **pre-herniation**, not an early warning.
- **Lucid interval → extradural haematoma → middle meningeal artery → urgent CT and neurosurgical referral.**
- ⚑ **Anticoagulated head injury:** warfarin → vitamin K + **Beriplex (4-factor PCC) 50 IU/kg, no FFP**. The "3-factor Prothrombinex-VF **plus** FFP" answer is pre-**June 2024** and is now wrong; andexanet alfa left the ARTG **20 May 2026**, so Xa-inhibitor reversal is PCC too.

**GCS (score 3–15).**

| Component | High → low |
|---|---|
| **Eye** (4) | 4 spontaneous · 3 to voice · 2 to pain · 1 none |
| **Verbal** (5) | 5 orientated · 4 confused · 3 words · 2 sounds · 1 none |
| **Motor** (6) | 6 obeys · 5 localises · 4 withdraws · 3 abnormal flexion (decorticate) · 2 extension (decerebrate) · 1 none |

Severity: **mild 13–15, moderate 9–12, severe ≤8**. Record components separately; the **motor score** carries most prognostic weight. Score the **best** response.

**Raised ICP and herniation.** Worsening headache, vomiting, falling GCS, pupillary change (papilloedema late or absent acutely). **Uncal herniation** compresses CN III → **ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil**, **contralateral** hemiparesis (Kernohan's notch gives the false-localising variant). Tonsillar herniation → brainstem compression, apnoea, death.

**The three haemorrhages.**

| | Extradural | Acute subdural | Traumatic SAH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vessel | **Middle meningeal artery** | **Bridging cortical veins** | Small cortical surface vessels |
| Mechanism | Temporal blow ± skull fracture | Acceleration–deceleration; trivial fall in the frail | Significant blunt injury |
| Patient | Young adult | **Elderly, alcohol, anticoagulated, atrophic brain** | Any age |
| Time course | **Lucid interval, then decline over minutes–hours** | Hours (acute); weeks with fluctuating cognition (chronic) | Immediate |
| CT shape | **Biconvex/lentiform, hyperdense** | **Crescentic** over the convexity | Blood in sulci/fissures |
| Boundaries | **Does not cross sutures**; can cross dural reflections | **Crosses sutures**; not falx/tentorium | Neither applies |
| Outcome | Excellent if evacuated early | Worse — reflects brain injury | Depends on associated injury |

> **Exam point — head strike, then headache and falling GCS at 40 minutes.** Textbook **extradural**: initially lucid, arterial bleed from the **middle meningeal artery** stripping dura off the inner table, deterioration accelerating as compensation is exhausted. Chain: <C>ABCDE, prevent hypoxia/hypotension → **urgent non-contrast CT head** → **immediate neurosurgical referral for craniotomy and evacuation**. Reaching theatre before pupillary change usually means a good outcome.

**Canadian CT Head Rule.** Blunt minor head injury, age ≥16, GCS 13–15 with witnessed LOC, amnesia or disorientation. Does **not** apply if anticoagulated, obvious open skull fracture, post-injury seizure, or GCS <13. CT if any of:
- *High risk (neurosurgical intervention):* GCS still <15 **at two hours**; suspected open or depressed skull fracture; any base-of-skull sign (periorbital or mastoid bruising, haemotympanum, CSF otorrhoea/rhinorrhoea); **≥2 vomits**; **age ≥65**.
- *Medium risk:* retrograde amnesia **≥30 min** before impact; dangerous mechanism (pedestrian struck, ejection, fall >1 m or >5 stairs).

**The anticoagulated head injury.** ⚑ The rules above *exclude* anticoagulated patients because their risk is not captured — Australian practice is a **much lower threshold: effectively CT anyone on an anticoagulant with any head strike**, plus observation and consideration of repeat imaging, as these bleeds evolve late.

| Agent | Reversal in traumatic ICH |
|---|---|
| **Dabigatran** | **Idarucizumab** 5 g IV (2 × 2.5 g) — specific antibody fragment; haemodialysis as fallback |
| **Apixaban / rivaroxaban** | **Beriplex (4-factor PCC) 50 IU/kg** — ⚑ **andexanet alfa is no longer available in Australia**: its provisional ARTG registration lapsed and it was removed from the ARTG on **20 May 2026** (thrombotic harm without demonstrated outcome benefit). Australian Xa-inhibitor reversal is therefore now **PCC-based**. Older texts and most revision material still list andexanet first — that is the **old** answer |
| **Warfarin** | **Vitamin K1 5–10 mg IV + Beriplex (4-factor PCC) 50 IU/kg** — ⚑ **no FFP**. **What changed:** until **June 2024** Australia's PCC was **Prothrombinex-VF**, a **3-factor** product (II, IX, X), so FFP 150–300 mL had to be given *with* it to supply factor VII. Prothrombinex-VF was **discontinued and replaced by Beriplex, a 4-factor PCC (II, VII, IX, X)**, and **co-administration with FFP is no longer required** (THANZ / MJA position statement 2025, Lifeblood). Most textbooks still say "3-factor + FFP" — recognise it as the superseded answer. Dose capped at **100 kg**; 25–50 IU/kg for clinically significant non-life-threatening bleeding |
| **Antiplatelets** | Platelet transfusion **not** routine (PATCH harm signal) |

> **Exam point — 70-year-old on dabigatran who hit his head.** Even with GCS 15 and a normal examination: **urgent CT**, coagulation assessment, early haematology/neurosurgical involvement, and **idarucizumab** if intracranial haemorrhage is confirmed. Reversal is not deferred pending the neurosurgical opinion.

**Raised ICP management.** Head up 30°, neutral neck, relieve collar obstruction to venous drainage; sedation and analgesia; normoxia and **normocapnia (PaCO₂ ~35–40 mmHg)** — hyperventilation is a short-term rescue for impending herniation only, since vasoconstriction causes ischaemia; hyperosmolar therapy (**hypertonic saline** or mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg); seizure prophylaxis; treat fever; **CPP = MAP − ICP** ~60–70 mmHg; then surgery — evacuation, EVD, decompressive craniectomy. ⚑ Steroids are **contraindicated** in TBI (CRASH showed increased mortality).

## 5.6 Cervical spine

**The short version**
- Assume injury until cleared in any blunt trauma with a suggestive mechanism.
- ⚑ Immobilisation has shifted from hard collar plus blocks toward **minimal handling and self-restriction of movement**, especially in penetrating trauma and agitated patients where collars harm.
- **NEXUS** and the **Canadian C-Spine Rule** clear the spine clinically in alert, stable patients — the Canadian rule is more specific but has stricter entry criteria.
- CT is the imaging of choice in adults; plain films have no role in significant trauma.
- **Neurogenic shock = hypotension with bradycardia and warm, well-perfused peripheries.**

**NEXUS** — no imaging if **all five** hold: no midline posterior cervical tenderness; no focal neurological deficit; normal alertness; no intoxication; no distracting painful injury.

**Canadian C-Spine Rule** — alert (GCS 15), stable adults. (1) Any **high-risk factor** — **age ≥65**, dangerous mechanism (fall ≥1 m or five stairs, axial load such as diving, high-speed/rollover/ejection crash, motorised recreational vehicle, bicycle collision), or **limb paraesthesiae** → **image**. (2) If none, look for a **low-risk factor** permitting assessment of movement — simple rear-end collision, sitting up in ED, ambulatory since injury, delayed neck pain, no midline tenderness; if none → image. (3) With a low-risk factor, ask for **active 45° rotation each way**; if achieved, no imaging.

**Cord syndromes.**

| Syndrome | Mechanism | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Central cord** | Hyperextension, older spondylotic patient (commonest incomplete syndrome) | Weakness **upper > lower limbs**, distal > proximal, sacral sparing, bladder dysfunction |
| **Brown-Séquard** | Hemisection — penetrating injury, lateral mass fracture | **Ipsilateral** motor and dorsal-column loss; **contralateral** pain/temperature loss |
| **Anterior cord** | Anterior spinal artery infarct, flexion injury | Motor and pain/temperature loss below lesion, **proprioception preserved**; worst prognosis |

**Shock discrimination (high-yield).**

| | Neurogenic | Spinal | Haemorrhagic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Haemodynamic** — lost sympathetic outflow above T6 | **Neurological** — transient flaccid areflexia below the lesion | Volume loss |
| Heart rate | **Bradycardia** | N/A | **Tachycardia** |
| Peripheries | **Warm, vasodilated** | N/A | **Cool, clammy, pale** |
| Treatment | Cautious fluid then **noradrenaline**; atropine | Time; return of bulbocavernosus reflex | **Blood** and haemostasis |

⚑ Assume the hypotensive spinal-injured patient is bleeding until proven otherwise — neurogenic shock is a diagnosis of exclusion. ⚑ High-dose methylprednisolone for acute cord injury is **no longer recommended**.

## 5.7 Abdominal and pelvic trauma

**The short version**
- Blunt trauma injures **spleen and liver**; stab wounds **liver and small bowel**; gunshot **small bowel and colon**.
- **Abdominal examination is unreliable** in the intoxicated, head-injured or distracted patient — image or observe.
- Immediate laparotomy: instability with positive FAST, peritonitis, evisceration, free air, diaphragmatic rupture, gunshot traversing peritoneum, blood on gastric tube/PR.
- **Seat-belt sign** predicts hollow-viscus, mesenteric and lumbar (Chance) fracture injury — normal early CT does not exclude it, so admit and serially examine.
- Pelvic fracture bleeding is mostly **venous plexus and cancellous bone**; binder first, then angioembolisation or packing.

Stable blunt trauma is increasingly **non-operative**: CT-graded splenic and hepatic injuries are observed with serial haematocrit and examination, with angioembolisation for contrast blush. Splenectomy commits the patient to **post-splenectomy sepsis prophylaxis** — pneumococcal, meningococcal and Hib vaccination, antibiotics, medical alert. Stable anterior stab wounds without peritonitis may be managed by wound exploration, CT and serial examination. Retroperitoneal (duodenal, pancreatic) injuries are easily missed — rising lipase and retroperitoneal air are the clues.

## 5.8 Burns

**The short version**
- Stop the burning; **cool running water 20 minutes, effective up to 3 hours after injury**; never ice. Keep the patient warm while cooling the burn.
- Depth is judged on **appearance, blistering, capillary refill, sensation**.
- **Rule of Nines** for adults; **palmar surface ≈1%** for scattered burns; **Lund–Browder** for children.
- ⚑ ANZBA/EMSB fluid formula is **3 mL × kg × %TBSA of Hartmann's over 24 hours from the time of injury**, half in the first 8 hours — not the classic Parkland 4 mL.
- ⚑ **Burns do not cause early hypotension. A shocked burns patient has another injury** until proven otherwise.

**Depth.**

| Depth | Appearance | Blisters | Cap refill | Sensation | Healing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial (epidermal) | Red, dry | **No** | Brisk | **Painful** | 5–7 days, no scar; **excluded from TBSA** |
| Superficial partial | Pale pink, moist | **Yes** | Brisk | **Very painful**, hypersensitive | ~2 weeks, minimal scar |
| Deep partial | Blotchy red/white, moist | Yes, may deroof | **Sluggish/absent** | **Reduced** (blunted pinprick) | >3 weeks, scars — usually grafted |
| Full thickness | White, waxy, leathery, charred; thrombosed vessels | **No** | **Absent** | **Insensate** | Will not heal — excision and grafting |

**TBSA — Rule of Nines (adult):** head and neck **9**; each arm **9**; anterior trunk **18**; posterior trunk **18**; each leg **18**; perineum **1**. ⚑ **Superficial (erythema-only) burns are not counted.** **Palmar surface:** the whole palmar hand **including fingers ≈ 1% TBSA** — the ANZBA/EMSB teaching figure to quote. ⚑ Anatomical meta-analyses put the true value nearer **0.8%** (palm alone ~0.5%); quote 1%, noting the discrepancy if precision is invited. **Children:** head proportionally larger, legs smaller — infants approach **18% head, 14% per leg**; use an age-corrected **Lund–Browder** chart.

> **Worked Rule of Nines — entire anterior torso plus the whole of one arm.**
> Anterior chest 9 + anterior abdomen 9 = **18%**; whole arm (anterior and posterior) = **9%**. **Total = 27% TBSA.**

**Fluid resuscitation.** Start formal resuscitation at **>20% TBSA in adults**, **>10% in children**. ⚑ **ANZBA/EMSB (current ANZ practice): 3 mL × weight (kg) × %TBSA of Hartmann's in the first 24 hours.** Classic **Parkland uses 4 mL** and persists in older texts — quote **3 mL** as current for an Australian paper, saying so if you show both. Rules: **the clock starts at the TIME OF INJURY, not arrival**; **half in the first 8 hours from injury**, the rest over the next 16; children need **maintenance fluid** on top; the formula sets only a starting rate — **titrate to urine output: 0.5 mL/kg/hr adults, 1 mL/kg/hr children**, in 10–20% increments. More is needed with inhalation injury, electrical burns, delayed presentation or dark urine.

> **Worked calculation — 80 kg, 30% TBSA, presenting 1 hour after injury.**
> Total 24-hour volume: **3 × 80 × 30 = 7200 mL** Hartmann's.
> First half: **3600 mL** due within **8 hours of injury**. One hour has already elapsed → deliver over the remaining **7 hours** = **3600 ÷ 7 ≈ 514 mL/hr**.
> Second half: **3600 mL over the next 16 hours ≈ 225 mL/hr**.
> Target urine output: 0.5 × 80 = **40 mL/hr**.
> *(By the older Parkland 4 mL formula this would be 9600 mL total, 4800 mL in the first 8 hours, ≈686 mL/hr over the remaining 7 — quote the ANZBA figure.)*

> **Second worked example — 70 kg, 25% TBSA, arriving 2 hours after injury.**
> **3 × 70 × 25 = 5250 mL.** First half **2625 mL** over the **6 remaining** hours = **≈437 mL/hr**; second half 2625 mL over 16 hours ≈ **164 mL/hr**. Urine target **35 mL/hr**.

**Burns unit referral (ANZBA):** >10% TBSA (>5% children); full-thickness >5%; face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, major joints; **circumferential** limb or chest burns; **inhalation injury**; **electrical** or **chemical** burns; significant comorbidity, extremes of age, pregnancy, associated major trauma; suspected **non-accidental injury**.

**Inhalation injury.** Suspect with enclosed-space fire, facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in sputum, hoarseness or stridor. ⚑ **Intubate early** — laryngeal oedema progresses over hours and later becomes impossible. **Carbon monoxide**: SpO₂ falsely normal (the oximeter cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin) — measure **carboxyhaemoglobin by co-oximetry**, treat with **100% oxygen**. **Cyanide** (burning plastics/wool): profound lactic acidosis with apparently adequate oxygenation and cardiovascular collapse → **hydroxocobalamin**.

**Other management.** **Escharotomy** for circumferential full-thickness burns causing distal ischaemia, or chest burns restricting ventilation — bedside incisions along mid-axial lines; distinct from **fasciotomy** (deep electrical injury). **Analgesia** IV and titrated (IM absorption unreliable). Check **tetanus**; **no prophylactic antibiotics**. Remove rings early. Cling film is acceptable for transfer.

## 5.9 Compartment syndrome

**The short version**
- Interstitial pressure in a closed osseofascial compartment exceeds capillary perfusion pressure → **microvascular ischaemia while the major artery stays open**.
- **Pain out of proportion** and **pain on passive stretch** are the earliest and most reliable signs.
- **Pulses and capillary refill stay normal until very late** — waiting for pulselessness means waiting for a dead limb.
- **Diagnosis is clinical.** Absolute pressure **>30 mmHg**, or **ΔP (diastolic BP − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg**, supports it — measurement must never delay treatment.
- Treatment is **immediate open fasciotomy** of all compartments.

Commonest setting is a **tibial shaft fracture** (also forearm fracture, crush injury, reperfusion after vascular repair, tight casts, burns). Measure pressure only when the patient *cannot* report pain — intubated, obtunded, regional-blocked, very young.

**Six Ps in order:** **Pain** (severe, escalating, opioid-resistant) → **Pain on passive stretch** → **Paraesthesia** (deep peroneal first — first web space) → **Paresis** → **Pallor** → **Pulselessness** (terminal). Pulses persist because arterial systolic pressure far exceeds the compartment pressure that has already collapsed the capillaries.

**Management.** Remove all circumferential casts, splints and dressings to skin. ⚑ **Keep the limb at heart level — do NOT elevate**; elevation reduces inflow and narrows ΔP further. Analgesia, oxygen, normotension, correct hypovolaemia. **Urgent fasciotomy** — the leg needs **all four compartments** released, usually two-incision; wounds left open for delayed closure or grafting. Muscle tolerates ~4–6 hours of ischaemia.

> **Exam point — tibial fracture with severe pain 6 hours later.** Escalating pain despite adequate opioid is compartment syndrome until disproved. Answer: **split the cast to skin, limb at heart level, immediate fasciotomy referral** — not elevate, not more analgesia, not repeat films, and not "measure the pressure first" when the picture is clear.

## 5.10 Rhabdomyolysis and crush injury

**The short version**
- Myocyte necrosis releases myoglobin, potassium, phosphate and CK; myoglobin causes tubular obstruction and direct nephrotoxicity → **AKI**.
- **Tea-coloured urine, dipstick positive for blood with no red cells on microscopy** — the classic discriminator.
- **CK is the diagnostic test** (>5× upper limit; AKI risk rises sharply above ~5000 U/L).
- **Hyperkalaemia is the immediate killer** — ECG and treat before creatinine returns.
- ⚑ **Crush/reperfusion syndrome:** start IV fluid **before** the crushing force is released where possible.

Management: high-volume isotonic crystalloid titrated to **urine output 1–2 mL/kg/hr**; cardiac monitoring; treat hyperkalaemia (calcium gluconate, insulin–dextrose, salbutamol, then definitive removal); watch for hypocalcaemia (calcium deposits in damaged muscle — do not over-correct, it is released later), hyperphosphataemia, and compartment syndrome as muscle swells. ⚑ **Bicarbonate alkalinisation and mannitol are no longer routine** — weak evidence over volume alone. RRT for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, overload or severe AKI.

# Surgery Notes — Section 6: Breast, Endocrine and Skin Surgery

*Sources: Cancer Australia and BreastScreen Australia; Cancer Council Australia / Melanoma Institute Australia melanoma and keratinocyte cancer guidelines; RACGP; Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic; AMH; Bethesda System (2023); ATA; Fifth International Workshop on Primary Hyperparathyroidism (2022); ESE/ENSAT; RACS/ANZES. Scope: breast triple assessment and disease, thyroid, parathyroid and adrenal surgery and its complications, MEN, and skin malignancy.*

## 6.1 Triple assessment

**The short version**
- **Clinical, imaging, pathology**, each graded independently — together detecting **>99.6%** of breast cancers; a uniformly negative triple test leaves residual risk **<1%**.
- ⚑ **Age drives imaging**: **<35 y → ultrasound first**; **≥35 y → mammography AND ultrasound**. Pregnancy/lactation → US first regardless.
- **Core biopsy > FNA** — FNA gives cells not architecture, so it cannot separate in-situ from invasive or grade; it survives for **cyst aspiration** and **axillary node sampling**.
- **The most suspicious leg governs management.** A benign biopsy never overrules a suspicious lump or mammogram.

**The three legs.** *Clinical* (P1–P5) — duration, change, cyclicity, discharge, HRT/OCP, family history; both breasts, axillae and supraclavicular fossae. *Imaging* (U/M1–5) — US ± mammography by age; MRI for high-risk screening or occult primary. *Pathology* (B1–B5 core, C1–C5 cytology).

**Why the age rule.** Dense young breast is radio-opaque, so mammography under 35 has poor sensitivity; from 35 the fatty-replaced breast makes it informative and shows **microcalcification** (the DCIS signature) that US misses. ⚑ **The threshold is 35 — Cancer Australia has no 35–40 band in which mammography is added only if the ultrasound is indeterminate.**

**Discordance.** Upsize the biopsy, add the missing modality, refer. Traps: hard fixed lump with a benign core → **excise** (sampling error); pleomorphic microcalcification with a benign core → **vacuum biopsy**; cyst with **blood-stained fluid, residual mass or rapid refill** → image and biopsy.

## 6.2 Benign breast disease

**The short version**
- **Fibroadenoma** — 15–35 y, firm, smooth, **highly mobile ("breast mouse")**, painless, well-circumscribed. Triple-assess then **observe** if concordant.
- **Phyllodes** is the mimic that matters: **older, larger, rapidly growing** → **wide excision, ≥1 cm margins**, never axillary surgery.
- **Gynaecomastia** = concentric tender subareolar disc (vs diffuse fat of lipomastia). Always interrogate the **drug chart**.

### Fibroadenoma

A benign oestrogen-responsive proliferation of the terminal duct lobular unit; 1–3 cm, rubbery, discrete, non-tender, freely mobile.

**Exam stem — 30 y.o., firm, mobile, painless, well-circumscribed 2 cm lump.** Answer: **triple assessment** — clinical + **ultrasound** (she is <35) + **core biopsy**; concordantly benign → **reassure, observe, safety-net**.

**Excise if:** patient preference or symptoms; **>3 cm** or documented growth; **discordant triple test**; a core lesion inseparable from phyllodes; increasing age with uncertainty (after ~35–40).

| Feature | Fibroadenoma | Phyllodes |
|---|---|---|
| Age / growth / size | 15–35; static or slow; <3 cm | **40–55; rapid over weeks–months; often >4 cm**, may ulcerate skin |
| Histology / core | Uniform low-cellularity stroma; core diagnostic | **Hypercellular stroma**, leaf-like clefts; core may under-call as a "cellular fibroepithelial lesion" |
| Spread | None | **Haematogenous (lung)**; **nodes rarely involved → no axillary surgery** |
| Management | Observe or excise | **Wide local excision, ≥1 cm margins**; mastectomy if huge or recurrent |

### The rest of the spectrum

| Condition | Key point |
|---|---|
| **Fibrocystic change** | Bilateral nodularity, **cyclical** pain. Physiological — reassure; triple-assess a **dominant** lump |
| **Simple cyst** | Smooth, tense; US anechoic with posterior enhancement. ⚑ **Cytology unnecessary if fluid is clear and the lump vanishes** |
| **Fat necrosis** | Post-trauma/surgery/RT; **tethers skin, retracts the nipple — mimics carcinoma**; mammogram shows spiculation or a rim-calcified oil cyst. Core still needed |
| **Duct ectasia** | Perimenopausal **smokers**; **thick green/creamy multi-duct discharge**, **slit-like nipple retraction** |
| **Intraductal papilloma** | Commonest cause of **single-duct blood-stained discharge** → **microdochectomy** |

### Gynaecomastia

True glandular proliferation from **relative oestrogen excess over androgen effect** — a **concentric rubbery tender subareolar disc**. ⚑ A **hard, eccentric, fixed lump, skin change or discharge in a man is male breast cancer until disproven**.

- **Peaks:** neonatal, pubertal, elderly. **Pathological:** hypogonadism (**Klinefelter's**), **hCG-secreting testicular tumours**, hyperthyroidism, **chronic liver disease**.
- **Drugs:** **spironolactone, digoxin, cimetidine, ketoconazole, metronidazole; bicalutamide, goserelin, finasteride; oestrogens; anabolic steroids; cannabis, alcohol, opioids; risperidone; calcium-channel blockers; PPIs; HAART.**
- **Work-up:** drug chart, then LFT, TFT, LH/FSH, testosterone, oestradiol, **hCG and AFP**, prolactin.

## 6.3 Breast infection

**The short version**
- **Lactational mastitis** = ***Staph aureus***, weeks 2–6 postpartum, cracked nipple + milk stasis. **Continue breastfeeding/expressing — this is treatment, not a risk to the baby.**
- Australian first line: **di/flucloxacillin 500 mg orally 6-hourly** — ⚑ **5 days only if symptoms resolve rapidly, otherwise 10 days**; non-immediate penicillin allergy → **cephalexin 500 mg q6h**; immediate/severe → **clindamycin**.
- Fluctuance or failure to improve at ~48 h → **ultrasound**. ⚑ **US-guided needle aspiration under LA is first-line, not incision and drainage.**
- **Any "mastitis" not resolving after an adequate antibiotic course needs imaging and biopsy** — the inflammatory-breast-cancer rule.

### Lactational mastitis and abscess

**Exam stem — 18 y.o. breastfeeding a 5-week-old, 5 days of left breast pain and fever.** Expect a **wedge-shaped, tender, erythematous, indurated segment** with fever and flu-like malaise, and poor attachment or a nipple crack in the history.

1. **Effective milk removal** — the single most important step: feed the affected side first, correct attachment, express after feeds.
2. **Analgesia** — paracetamol + ibuprofen.
3. **Antibiotics** if systemically unwell, cracked nipple, or no improvement after 12–24 h: **di/flucloxacillin 500 mg PO q6h — 5 days if rapid resolution, otherwise 10 days**.
4. **Reassess at 48 h** — no improvement → **ultrasound**. ⚑ **A woman who has already had 5 days of untreated symptoms has failed that window — image at presentation, do not observe a further 48 h.**

**Abscess:** ⚑ **US-guided needle aspiration ± catheter under LA**, repeated until dry, plus antibiotics — less pain, no scar, no milk fistula, breastfeeding preserved. **I&D** only for very large or multiloculated abscesses, necrotic skin, or failed aspiration. Send pus for **MC&S**.

**Non-lactational (periductal) mastitis:** **smoking-associated**; periareolar pain, abscess, **mammary duct fistula**, nipple retraction. Organisms are **mixed including anaerobes** → **amoxicillin–clavulanate** (or metronidazole + di/flucloxacillin), not flucloxacillin alone. **Smoking cessation is the definitive treatment.**

| | Lactational mastitis | Inflammatory breast cancer |
|---|---|---|
| Context | Young, lactating, early postpartum; onset over days | Weeks, progressive. ⚑ **Not lactating / older is a weak discriminator** — IBC has a *younger* median age than other breast cancers and occurs in pregnancy and lactation. Rely on the features below |
| Systemic | **Fever, rigors, flu-like** | **Afebrile, systemically well** |
| Breast | **Localised, wedge-shaped** erythema; tender nodes | **Diffuse — ≥1/3 of the breast**, **peau d'orange**, thickening, nipple inversion, **matted nodes** |
| Antibiotics | Improves in 48 h | **No response** |

**The rule:** not resolving on an adequate course — or non-lactating, diffuse, or peau d'orange — → **urgent mammography + US, core biopsy of the parenchyma AND a full-thickness skin punch** (dermal lymphatic invasion), plus axillary sampling. **Stage III at minimum**: **neoadjuvant chemotherapy → mastectomy with axillary clearance → radiotherapy**, never conservation.

## 6.4 Breast cancer

**The short version**
- Commonest cancer in Australian women (~1 in 7 lifetime), and mostly **sporadic** — a negative family history never excuses not investigating a lump.
- **ER/PR/HER2** determine systemic therapy; **triple-negative** has no target and depends on chemotherapy.
- **BCS + radiotherapy = mastectomy for survival.** Radiotherapy is not optional after conservation.
- **Axilla: clinically/US-negative → sentinel node biopsy; biopsy-proven node-positive → axillary clearance** (or axillary radiotherapy).
- **BreastScreen Australia: free 2-yearly mammography from 40; active invitations 50–74; ≥75 free but uninvited.**

**Risk factors.** **Age** (dominant), previous breast cancer, atypical hyperplasia/LCIS, dense breasts, prior chest-wall radiotherapy. *Hormonal* — early menarche, late menopause, **nulliparity or first pregnancy >30**, never breastfed, **combined HRT**. *Lifestyle* — **alcohol**, post-menopausal obesity. *Genetic (~5–10%)* — ***BRCA1*** (triple-negative, young; ovarian), ***BRCA2*** (ER+; **male breast cancer**), *TP53*, *PTEN*, *CDH1*.

**Red flags.** Painless hard lump; **skin dimpling/tethering**; **new nipple retraction**; **unilateral single-duct blood-stained discharge**; **peau d'orange**; ulceration; **eczematous nipple change**; a new firm axillary node. **Any discrete lump over 40 is cancer until triple assessment says otherwise.**

| Receptor | Frequency | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **ER (± PR)** | ~70–75% | **Endocrine therapy ≥5 y**; better long-term prognosis but a **late-relapse tail** |
| **HER2** | ~15–20% | **Trastuzumab ± pertuzumab** with chemotherapy; **cardiotoxic — serial LVEF** |
| **Triple-negative** | ~15% | No target; **chemotherapy backbone** ± immunotherapy ± PARP inhibitor if *BRCA*. Early recurrence, worse prognosis |

**In-situ disease.** **DCIS** is a **true precursor** confined by the basement membrane, **screen-detected as microcalcification**, progressing to invasive cancer **at the same site** — **excision (BCS + RT, or mastectomy if extensive) ± endocrine therapy**, with **no axillary surgery unless a mastectomy is done** (SLNB then, as the sentinel cannot be mapped later). **LCIS** is a **risk marker** — incidental, imaging-occult, raising risk in **both breasts** — so **surveillance**, excising **pleomorphic LCIS**.

**Staging (outline).** T1 ≤2 cm, T2 2–5 cm, T3 >5 cm, T4 chest wall/skin (**T4d = inflammatory**); N by nodes; M0/M1. Mets: **bone, liver, lung, brain**.

**Surgery — breast.** **BCS + whole-breast radiotherapy** for unifocal disease with a favourable tumour:breast ratio — **survival equals mastectomy**, local recurrence modestly higher. **Mastectomy** for multifocal disease, a large tumour in a small breast, inflammatory cancer, prior chest RT, persistently involved margins, *BRCA* carriers or preference; add **post-mastectomy RT** if large or node-positive. **Neoadjuvant chemotherapy** downstages to permit conservation and treats locally advanced or inflammatory disease.

**Surgery — axilla.** **Clinically and US-negative → sentinel lymph node biopsy** (blue dye ± radioisotope); negative means nothing further, and it is also done at mastectomy for DCIS. **Biopsy-proven node-positive or a heavy nodal burden → axillary dissection (levels I–II)** or axillary radiotherapy. ⚑ With **1–2 positive sentinel nodes** plus BCS and whole-breast RT, **completion clearance is often omitted** (Z0011-era).

**Complications of axillary surgery:** **lymphoedema** (much higher after clearance, worse with axillary RT and obesity), intercostobrachial nerve injury, seroma, **long thoracic** (winged scapula). **Arm care:** lymphoedema-therapist referral, skin care with prompt cellulitis treatment, **compression garments**, exercise and weight control. ⚑ The blanket ban on venepuncture and BP cuffs rests on weak evidence, but **avoiding the arm where possible remains standard Australian practice**.

**Adjuvant therapy (outline).** **Tamoxifen** for **pre-menopausal** ER+ disease (and men) — endometrial carcinoma, **VTE**, hot flushes. **Aromatase inhibitors** for **post-menopausal** ER+ disease, or pre-menopausal **with ovarian suppression** — ⚑ **ineffective alone premenopausally**; cause **arthralgia and osteoporosis**. **Chemotherapy** for node-positive, large, high-grade, HER2+ and triple-negative disease — discuss **fertility preservation first**. **Trastuzumab** 12 months in HER2+ disease.

**BreastScreen Australia.** ⚑ **Free 2-yearly screening mammography from 40**, with **active invitations to the 50–74 target group**. **40–49** are eligible and free but **not invited** — screen after discussing benefits and harms (lower incidence, denser breasts, more false positives). **≥75** stays free but uninvited. ⚑ **BreastScreen is for asymptomatic people only** — a symptomatic patient goes down the **diagnostic** pathway, and a recent normal screen does **not** exclude cancer in a symptomatic breast. Very-high-risk individuals get **annual MRI + mammography** from ~30, outside the program.

**Genetic referral.** Multiple close relatives on the **same side**; breast cancer **under 40–50**; **bilateral** disease; **male breast cancer**; breast **and** ovarian cancer in one family; **Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry**; a known familial variant. Carrier options include **bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy** and **salpingo-oophorectomy**.

## 6.5 Nipple discharge and nipple change

**The short version**
- **Physiological**: bilateral, multi-duct, expressed only. **Pathological**: **unilateral, single-duct, spontaneous, blood-stained or serous**, ± mass — commonest cause **intraductal papilloma**, the one to exclude **DCIS/invasive carcinoma**.
- **Bilateral milky discharge, non-lactating = galactorrhoea** → prolactin, TFTs, drug chart, pregnancy test — think prolactinoma, not surgery.
- **Paget's starts on the nipple and spreads outward; eczema starts on the areola and spares it** — any persistent unilateral nipple rash needs a **punch biopsy**.

Pathological discharge needs **triple assessment** ± ductography, then **microdochectomy** or total duct excision; discharge cytology has poor sensitivity, so **imaging plus tissue settles it**.

| | **Paget's disease of the nipple** | **Nipple eczema** |
|---|---|---|
| Starts / side | **The nipple**, spreading outward to the areola; **unilateral** | **The areola**, often **sparing the nipple**; usually bilateral |
| Look | Scaly, moist/crusted, sharply demarcated; may ulcerate and destroy the nipple | Vesicular, weeping, itchy, ill-defined edge |
| Underlying / steroids | **Underlying DCIS or invasive cancer in the great majority**; **no sustained steroid response** | None; responds to steroid |
| Management | **Punch biopsy** + mammography/US; treat the underlying cancer (BCS with nipple–areolar excision + RT, or mastectomy) | Topical steroid, emollients |

## 6.6 Thyroid nodule and goitre

**The short version**
- **TSH first.** **Suppressed TSH** → **scintigraphy**; a **hot** nodule is almost never malignant and is **not** FNA'd.
- **Ultrasound in every patient with a nodule, whatever the TSH**; a suppressed TSH *adds* scintigraphy rather than replacing US. Then **pattern + size** decide who gets FNA, reported by the **Bethesda system**.
- Alarm features: **age extremes (<20 or >60–70), male sex, rapid growth, a hard/fixed nodule, hoarseness, cervical lymphadenopathy, previous head/neck irradiation, family history of thyroid cancer or MEN2**.

**Pathway.** History and examination (compressive symptoms, voice change, **prior irradiation**, family history of thyroid cancer or MEN; nodule fixation, **cervical nodes**) → **TSH** → **ultrasound** → **US-guided FNA** → CT/MRI only for retrosternal or invasive disease → **laryngoscopy** before surgery.

**Suspicious sonographic features:** solid and **hypoechoic**, **taller-than-wide**, **irregular margins**, **microcalcifications**, extrathyroidal extension, abnormal nodes. ⚑ **Size alone does not determine risk**; **thyroglobulin has no diagnostic role**; **calcitonin** is the medullary marker.

| Bethesda | Name | Malignancy risk | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Non-diagnostic | ~10–13% | **Repeat US-guided FNA**; surgery if repeatedly non-diagnostic |
| **II** | Benign | <3% | **Clinical/US follow-up** |
| **III** | Atypia of undetermined significance | ~13–30% | Repeat FNA, **molecular testing**, or lobectomy |
| **IV** | Follicular neoplasm | ~23–34% | **Diagnostic lobectomy** — cytology cannot show capsular invasion |
| **V** | Suspicious for malignancy | ~65–75% | **Surgery** |
| **VI** | Malignant | ~95–100% | **Surgery**, extent per size, nodes and risk |

## 6.7 Thyroid cancer

**The short version**
- **Papillary (~80%) — lymphatic spread, excellent prognosis, FNA diagnostic.**
- **Follicular — haematogenous (bone, lung); FNA cannot diagnose it.**
- **Medullary — parafollicular C cells, calcitonin, MEN2**; no iodine uptake, so **radioiodine and TSH suppression only work for differentiated disease**.
- **Anaplastic — elderly, rapidly enlarging hard mass, airway compromise, palliative.**

| | **Papillary** | **Follicular** | **Medullary** | **Anaplastic** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency / age | ~80%; 30–50 | ~10%; 40–60 | ~5%; any | ~1–2%; **>65** |
| Spread | **Lymphatic — cervical nodes** | **Haematogenous — bone, lung** | Lymphatic **and** haematogenous, early | Aggressive local + distant |
| Diagnosis | **FNA diagnostic** — **Orphan Annie nuclei, grooves, psammoma bodies** | ⚑ **FNA cannot diagnose** — needs **lobectomy** to show capsular/vascular invasion | FNA + **calcitonin** (serum and washout), **CEA**; germline ***RET*** | FNA/core; giant/spindle cells |
| Associations / prognosis | Prior **radiation**, *BRAF* V600E; **excellent (>95% 10-y)** | Iodine deficiency; good (~85%) | **MEN2A/2B**, familial MTC — screen relatives; intermediate | Long-standing goitre; **months** |
| Treatment | Lobectomy (small, low-risk) or **total thyroidectomy** ± central nodes; **radioiodine** if higher risk; **TSH suppression** | Lobectomy → completion thyroidectomy if invasive; **radioiodine**; **TSH suppression** | **Total thyroidectomy + central nodes**. ⚑ **No radioiodine, no TSH suppression. Exclude phaeochromocytoma first** | Airway protection, chemoradiotherapy, palliation; ⚑ targeted therapy if *BRAF* V600E |

**Follow-up:** levothyroxine for **replacement and TSH suppression**, serial **thyroglobulin** (only interpretable after total thyroidectomy ± ablation) and neck US; **medullary** → **calcitonin and CEA**.

## 6.8 Thyroidectomy and its complications

**The short version**
- **Hypocalcaemia** is commonest — onset **12–48 h**, from parathyroid bruising, devascularisation or inadvertent removal.
- **Perioral tingling, distal paraesthesiae, cramps, carpopedal spasm, Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs**; **prolonged QT**; seizures/laryngospasm if severe.
- **Unilateral RLN injury = hoarse voice, weak bovine cough; bilateral = stridor and airway obstruction — reintubate immediately; external branch of the superior laryngeal → loss of high pitch.**
- **Neck haematoma is an airway emergency: open the wound at the bedside first.**

### Hypocalcaemia

**Exam stem — "the day after thyroidectomy, cramps in the hands and perioral tingling."** That is **acute hypocalcaemia from parathyroid injury**: check **corrected calcium, magnesium, phosphate and PTH**, monitor cardiac rhythm, treat.

- **Mechanism / timing:** all four parathyroids bruised, devascularised or removed — transient in up to a fifth of total thyroidectomies, **permanent ~1–3%**; onset **12–48 h**.
- **Signs:** **Chvostek's** (tap the facial nerve anterior to the tragus → facial twitch; less specific) and **Trousseau's** (BP cuff above systolic for 3 min → **carpopedal spasm**; more specific). **ECG: ⚑ long QTc**, risk of torsades.
- **Monitoring:** serial corrected calcium; ⚑ a low or undetectable **early post-op PTH** predicts significant hypocalcaemia. **Always check magnesium** — hypomagnesaemia makes it refractory.

| Severity | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Mild/asymptomatic** | **Oral elemental calcium 1–3 g daily in divided doses** ± **calcitriol 0.25–0.5 µg once–twice daily**; wean as PTH recovers |
| **Severe/symptomatic** (tetany, seizures, long QT, corrected Ca roughly <1.9 mmol/L) | **IV calcium gluconate 10%, 10–20 mL (1–2 ampoules, ≈2.2–4.4 mmol elemental Ca) diluted in 50–100 mL glucose 5% over 10–20 min with continuous cardiac monitoring**, repeated as needed, then a **continuous infusion** (~0.5–2 mg/kg/h elemental calcium in 5% glucose) titrated to symptoms and levels. Start **oral calcium + calcitriol** at the same time and **correct magnesium** |

⚑ **Use calcium gluconate peripherally** — calcium chloride is far more sclerosant. **Permanent hypoparathyroidism** = unable to wean by 6–12 months: lifelong calcium and active vitamin D, monitoring calcium, phosphate, magnesium and **urinary calcium** (over-treatment causes nephrocalcinosis), targeting a **low-normal, asymptomatic** calcium.

### Nerve injuries

- **RLN, unilateral** — **hoarse breathy voice, weak "bovine" cough**, aspiration on thin fluids. Most recover in 6–12 months; persistent palsy → injection medialisation or thyroplasty.
- **RLN, bilateral** — ⚑ **stridor and distress on extubation with a near-normal voice. AIRWAY EMERGENCY: reintubate immediately**, surgical airway if that fails.
- **External branch of the superior laryngeal** — cricothyroid palsy: **loss of high pitch and projection, voice fatigue**; protected by ligating the superior thyroid vessels **close to the gland**.

⚑ With **intra-operative nerve monitoring**, **loss of signal on the first side of a planned total thyroidectomy means staging the operation** rather than risking bilateral palsy.

### Post-operative neck haematoma

**Stem: neck swelling, tight dressing, distress and stridor hours after thyroidectomy.** Bleeding into a closed compartment obstructs venous and lymphatic drainage → **laryngeal and pharyngeal oedema**, which is why the airway does not recover instantly on evacuation. ⚑ **Immediate action: open the wound at the bedside** — remove clips/sutures, open skin, platysma and strap muscles and evacuate the clot **before** attempting a difficult intubation or moving to theatre; then oxygen, sit up, call anaesthetics, theatre for washout.

### Others

**Thyroid storm** — from operating on an unprepared thyrotoxic patient: fever, tachyarrhythmia, agitation, cardiac failure → **propranolol, propylthiouracil, iodine ≥1 h after the thionamide, corticosteroid, cooling**; prevented by rendering euthyroid pre-op. Also **permanent hypothyroidism**, bleeding, tracheomalacia after a large goitre, **inadvertent parathyroidectomy** (autotransplant into sternocleidomastoid).

## 6.9 Hyperparathyroidism

**The short version**
- **Primary**: **high calcium with a high or inappropriately normal PTH**; ~85% a **single adenoma**; low phosphate.
- ⚑ A **normal PTH with hypercalcaemia is abnormal** — it should be suppressed. **Secondary**: low/normal calcium, high PTH. **Tertiary**: **high calcium AND high PTH**.
- **Stones, bones, abdominal groans and psychic moans.** **Parathyroidectomy is the only cure**; the asymptomatic criteria are numerical and examinable.

| | **Primary** | **Secondary** | **Tertiary** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium | **↑** | **↓ or normal** | **↑** |
| PTH | ↑ or inappropriately normal | **↑** | **↑↑** |
| Phosphate | ↓ | ↑ (CKD) or ↓ (vit D deficiency) | ↑ |
| Cause | **Single adenoma ~85%**, 4-gland hyperplasia ~10–15% (MEN1/2A), carcinoma <1% | **CKD**, vitamin D deficiency | Long-standing CKD, often post-transplant |
| Treatment | **Parathyroidectomy** | Vitamin D, phosphate binders, calcimimetics | **Subtotal/total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation** |

⚑ Exclude **familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia** first — high calcium, normal/mildly raised PTH, but **low urinary calcium** (**Ca:Cr clearance ratio <0.01**). Review **thiazides and lithium**.

**Clinical.** *Stones* — nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, polyuria, CKD. *Bones* — osteoporosis at **cortical sites (distal radius, hip)**, fracture. *Groans* — constipation, peptic ulcer, pancreatitis. *Moans* — fatigue, depression, confusion. Plus hypertension and a **short QT**.

**Localisation.** **Sestamibi SPECT/CT + neck ultrasound**; **4D-CT** if discordant or reoperative. ⚑ **A roadmap, not a diagnostic test** — a negative scan neither excludes primary HPT nor contraindicates surgery. Concordant imaging permits **focused parathyroidectomy** with **intra-operative PTH** (>50% fall at 10 min predicts cure); otherwise **bilateral exploration**.

**Indications for parathyroidectomy in *asymptomatic* primary HPT** (Fifth International Workshop 2022 — any one):

| Domain | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Calcium** | **>0.25 mmol/L (1 mg/dL) above the upper limit of normal** |
| **Age** | **<50 years** — sufficient on its own |
| **Skeletal** | **T-score ≤ −2.5 at any site** and/or a **vertebral fracture** on imaging |
| **Renal** | **eGFR/CrCl <60 mL/min**; **nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis**; **hypercalciuria >250 mg/day (women), >300 mg/day (men)** with increased stone risk |

Symptomatic disease is an indication in its own right; others are monitored (annual calcium and eGFR, BMD 1–2-yearly), off thiazides and lithium. **Cinacalcet** lowers calcium without improving BMD; **bisphosphonates/denosumab** do the converse.

## 6.10 Adrenal surgery

**The short version**
- Every incidentaloma raises **two questions: is it functioning, and is it malignant?**
- Minimum screen: **1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression**, **plasma free metanephrines**, **ARR if hypertensive or hypokalaemic**. **Unenhanced CT ≤10 HU with rapid washout = benign lipid-rich adenoma.**
- Resect if **functioning**, **radiologically suspicious**, **growing**, or **large (>4 cm conventionally)**.
- **Phaeochromocytoma: alpha blockade first, beta blockade only afterwards — never the reverse.**

**Incidentaloma.** Found on ~5% of abdominal CTs. ⚑ **Do not biopsy an adrenal mass until phaeochromocytoma is biochemically excluded.** Favouring **adrenocortical carcinoma**: >4 cm, heterogeneity, necrosis, irregular margins, high attenuation, invasion, growth. **Operate** for any phaeochromocytoma, a lateralising **Conn's** adenoma, overt **Cushing's**, suspected carcinoma, or **>4 cm/growth** — ⚑ the **2023 ESE/ENSAT guideline emphasises radiological phenotype and growth over a rigid 4 cm rule**. **Laparoscopic** unless carcinoma is suspected (**open**).

**Phaeochromocytoma.** Catecholamine-secreting chromaffin tumour (extra-adrenal = **paraganglioma**). ⚑ The "rule of 10s" is obsolete — **up to ~40% are germline-mutation-associated** (*VHL*, *RET*/MEN2, *NF1*, *SDHx*), so **offer genetic testing to all**.
- **Presentation:** paroxysmal **headache, sweating and palpitations** on **hypertension**, with pallor, tremor, weight loss, hyperglycaemia; provoked by exertion, induction, palpation and ⚑ **beta-blockers, metoclopramide, tricyclics**. **Diagnosis: plasma free metanephrines** (supine) or 24 h urinary metanephrines → **CT/MRI** → **MIBG/⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET** if extra-adrenal or metastatic.
- **Preparation:** ① **alpha blockade first** — **phenoxybenzamine** (or prazosin/doxazosin) from **10–14 days** pre-op; ② **liberal salt and fluid** to re-expand the contracted plasma volume; ③ **beta blockade only afterwards**, and only for tachyarrhythmia. ⚑ **Beta blockade first leaves alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed → hypertensive crisis, pulmonary oedema, cardiac failure.** Intra-op: hypertension on handling, then **hypotension after venous ligation**.

**Conn's and Cushing's — see the Endocrinology notes.** **Conn's** — screen with **ARR**, confirm with a suppression test, then ⚑ **adrenal vein sampling** to separate a unilateral adenoma (**laparoscopic adrenalectomy**) from bilateral hyperplasia (**spironolactone/eplerenone**). **Cushing's** — confirm cortisol excess, then ACTH-dependence; ⚑ after removing a cortisol-secreting tumour the contralateral gland is suppressed, so **give tapering perioperative glucocorticoid replacement or the patient will have an adrenal crisis**.

## 6.11 MEN syndromes

**The short version**
- All **autosomal dominant**. **MEN1 = *MEN1* (menin) tumour suppressor; MEN2A/2B = *RET* proto-oncogene.**
- **MEN1 = 3 Ps. MEN2A = MTC + phaeo + parathyroid. MEN2B = MTC + phaeo + mucosal neuromas/marfanoid, NO parathyroid disease.**
- **Medullary thyroid carcinoma is the lethal component of MEN2** — *RET* testing drives **prophylactic thyroidectomy**.
- **Always exclude phaeochromocytoma before any other operation** in MEN2.

| | **MEN1** | **MEN2A** | **MEN2B** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gene | ***MEN1*** (menin), **tumour suppressor** | ***RET***, **proto-oncogene** | ***RET***, usually **M918T** |
| Components | **Parathyroid hyperplasia (~95%, usually first)**; **pancreatic/duodenal NETs** (gastrinoma → Zollinger–Ellison, insulinoma — leading cause of death); **pituitary adenoma** (prolactinoma) | **MTC (~100%)**; **phaeochromocytoma (~50%, often bilateral)**; **primary hyperparathyroidism (~20–30%)** | **MTC (~100%, earliest onset)**; **phaeochromocytoma (~50%)**; **mucosal neuromas**; **marfanoid habitus** |
| Surgery | **Subtotal (3½-gland) or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation** — single-gland surgery recurs | **Prophylactic total thyroidectomy** per *RET* codon risk (early childhood); **phaeo first** | **Prophylactic total thyroidectomy in the first year of life**; **phaeo first** |

## 6.12 Skin lesion surgery

**The short version**
- Australia has among the world's highest melanoma and keratinocyte cancer incidence — **any changing pigmented lesion is melanoma until proven otherwise**.
- ⚑ **Diagnostic biopsy of suspected melanoma = complete excisional biopsy with a 2 mm clinical margin into upper subcutis. Never shave, never punch** — it destroys the Breslow thickness that drives everything.
- **Definitive margins by Breslow:** in situ **5–10 mm**; **≤1 mm → 10 mm**; **1.01–4 mm → 10–20 mm**; **>4 mm → 20 mm**.
- **SLNB at Breslow >1 mm, or >0.8 mm with ulceration or mitoses** — **staging**, not therapeutic. **BCC never really metastasises; SCC can spread to regional nodes.**

### Melanoma

**Recognition — ABCDE + ugly duckling:** **A**symmetry, **B**order irregularity, **C**olour variegation, **D**iameter >6 mm, **E**volution. ⚑ **Change, and the "ugly duckling" lesion unlike its neighbours, outperform the static criteria**; **nodular and amelanotic melanoma break ABCDE** — use **EFG: Elevated, Firm, Growing**. Subtypes: superficial spreading (commonest), **nodular** (worst), lentigo maligna, acral lentiginous (**Hutchinson's sign**).

**Diagnostic biopsy.** ⚑ **Excisional biopsy with a 2 mm peripheral clinical margin, into the upper subcutaneous fat.** Shave and punch biopsies risk transecting the lesion and under-staging Breslow; partial biopsy is only for very large facial/acral or lentigo maligna lesions. Report: **Breslow thickness** (strongest prognostic factor), **ulceration**, mitotic rate, margins, lymphovascular and perineural invasion.

| Breslow thickness | Definitive margin (measured clinically from the lesion edge) |
|---|---|
| **Melanoma in situ** | **5–10 mm** (10 mm preferred; lentigo maligna may need wider or mapped excision) |
| **≤1.0 mm (pT1)** | **10 mm** |
| **1.01–2.0 mm (pT2)** | **10–20 mm** |
| **2.01–4.0 mm (pT3)** | **10–20 mm** (favour 20 mm with adverse features) |
| **>4.0 mm (pT4)** | **20 mm** |

⚑ Anatomy (face, digits) may force a narrower margin — permitted, but **histological clearance must be complete**; margins beyond 20 mm confer no survival benefit.

**SLNB** — offered at **Breslow >1.0 mm**, or **>0.8 mm with ulceration or a raised mitotic rate**, done **with the wide local excision** (mapping is unreliable afterwards); it is **prognostic/staging** and gains eligibility for adjuvant therapy. ⚑ **Completion lymph node dissection for a positive sentinel node is no longer routine** — nodal ultrasound surveillance plus adjuvant therapy is standard. Not indicated for in situ or thin melanoma.

**Adjuvant therapy** for resected stage III: **anti-PD-1 immunotherapy**, or **BRAF/MEK** if *BRAF* V600-mutant. **Follow-up** is life-long — skin examination and node palpation, 6-monthly for 5 years then annually.

# Surgery Notes — Section 7: Vascular Surgery and the Ischaemic Limb

*Sources: ANZSVS; Global Vascular Guidelines on CLTI (2019); ESVS (2024); SVS AAA (2018) and claudication (2025); ESO carotid guideline (2021); Stroke Foundation ANZ Living Stroke Guidelines; Therapeutic Guidelines and AMH; Wounds Australia/AWMA venous leg ulcer guideline; Australian diabetes-related foot disease guidelines (2021). Scope: chronic and acute limb ischaemia, aortic aneurysm and dissection, carotid, venous and diabetic-foot disease, leg ulcers and vascular access.*

## 7.1 Peripheral arterial disease and intermittent claudication

**The short version**
- Claudication is **reproducible, exertional, relieved within minutes by standing still**; spinal claudication needs flexion or sitting.
- ⚑ **Leriche syndrome triad — buttock/thigh claudication + erectile dysfunction + absent femoral pulses = occlusive atherosclerosis at the aortic bifurcation (aorto-iliac disease).**
- **ABI ≤0.90 diagnoses PAD; ABI >1.40 means incompressible calcified vessels** (diabetes, CKD) and is uninterpretable — use the **toe–brachial index**.
- **Smoking cessation + supervised exercise + antiplatelet + statin come BEFORE revascularisation.** Most claudicants never lose the limb — they die of coronary events.

**Risk factors:** ⚑ **smoking (3–4×) and diabetes weigh disproportionately** here; also age >65, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, CKD, male sex, family history. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians have far higher rates of diabetes-related PAD and amputation. Over 5 years about three-quarters are stable or improved on medical therapy and **only ~1–2% per year progress to CLTI** — but PAD is a coronary risk equivalent.

**Localising the level.** **Buttock/hip/thigh claudication = aorto-iliac disease**, with **erectile dysfunction** and **weak or absent femoral pulses bilaterally** — the **Leriche** picture. **Calf claudication = femoropopliteal disease** (classically SFA at the adductor hiatus): femoral pulse present, popliteal and distal absent. **Foot/arch pain = tibial/pedal disease**, typical of **diabetes** and CKD, often presenting as tissue loss because neuropathy masks pain. A **shortening claudication distance** is a red flag. **Examination:** pulses side-to-side, bruits, hair loss and shiny atrophic skin, **Buerger's test**; palpate for **AAA**, check for **AF**.

### Ankle–brachial index

**How:** supine, rested 10 min, **hand-held Doppler** (palpation over-reads). Record brachial systolic in **both arms** and, at each ankle, **dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial**; **ABI = highest ankle systolic in that leg ÷ highest brachial systolic**.

| ABI | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **>1.40** | ⚑ **Incompressible calcified vessels — uninterpretable.** Do **TBI**. Diabetes, CKD, elderly |
| 1.00–1.40 | Normal (0.91–0.99 borderline — if symptomatic, **exercise ABI**) |
| **≤0.90** | **Diagnostic of PAD**; 0.40–0.90 is the usual claudication range, **<0.40** severe (rest pain, tissue loss) |

**Toe–brachial index:** digital arteries escape medial calcification, so toe pressures stay valid. **TBI <0.70 = PAD**, and **toe pressure <30 mmHg** or **ankle pressure <50 mmHg** marks a wound unlikely to heal without revascularisation. Image (duplex → CTA/MRA) only once revascularisation is contemplated.

### Management — the order matters

**1. Lifestyle.** **Smoking cessation** is the highest-yield single intervention. **Supervised exercise therapy** is first-line ahead of revascularisation: **~3 sessions/week for ≥12 weeks, 30–60 min, walking to near-maximal claudication pain, rest, repeat**; where none is available (a real Australian access problem), a **structured home-based** programme is the fallback.

**2. Best medical therapy.**
- **Single antiplatelet: clopidogrel 75 mg daily is generally preferred** over aspirin in symptomatic PAD (CAPRIE); **aspirin 100 mg daily** is an accepted alternative and common Australian practice. **No routine DAPT** in stable PAD.
- **High-intensity statin for all** regardless of baseline lipids (atorvastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 20–40 mg), plus ezetimibe ± PCSK9 inhibitor if targets are missed — statins reduce events **and** improve walking distance. Control BP; ACE inhibitors are not contraindicated.
- ⚑ **Rivaroxaban 2.5 mg BD plus aspirin 100 mg daily** (the "vascular dose") reduced MACE and **major adverse limb events including amputation** in COMPASS and VOYAGER PAD, at the cost of major bleeding. ⚑ It is **not** full-dose anticoagulation, and **PBS listing here has been restrictive**.
- **Cilostazol** helps walking distance but is **contraindicated in heart failure**.

**3. Revascularisation** only when symptoms are **lifestyle- or vocation-limiting despite ≥3 months of exercise and best medical therapy**. **Endovascular** (angioplasty ± stent) suits short lesions, **aorto-iliac** disease and comorbid patients — less morbidity, **lower long-term patency**. **Open** (endarterectomy; **fem-pop bypass with autologous great saphenous vein rather than prosthetic**, especially below knee) suits long occlusions and failed endovascular therapy. ⚑ Endovascular-first is the default, but **preserve the vein conduit**.

## 7.2 Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia

**The short version**
- ⚑ **"Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia" has replaced "critical limb ischaemia"** (Global Vascular Guidelines 2019) — outcome depends on **wound, ischaemia and infection together** (**WIfI** staging), not one haemodynamic cut-off.
- Definition: **PAD plus ischaemic rest pain, or ulceration/gangrene present ≥2 weeks**, with corroborating haemodynamics.
- **Rest pain: burning forefoot/toe pain, worse lying flat at night, relieved by hanging the leg over the side of the bed** — gravity does the perfusing.
- **Limb- AND life-threatening** — high amputation and death rates within a year. **Urgent vascular referral, not a clinic letter.**

Patients often present with a **dependent oedematous leg** from sleeping upright. ⚑ **Buerger's test:** on elevation, pallor at a low angle (**Buerger's angle <20–30° = severe ischaemia**); on dependency, slow return of colour to a dusky "sunset" red — **dependent rubor**. **Elevation pallor plus dependent rubor is near-pathognomonic.** Also cool shiny hairless skin, venous guttering, ulceration at toes and pressure points, gangrene (**wet gangrene is an emergency**). Ankle pressure <50–70 mmHg, toe pressure <30–50 mmHg or ABI <0.40 supports it — but ⚑ **never exclude CLTI on a "normal" ABI in a diabetic**.

**Management:** **urgent vascular referral**; **opioid analgesia**; best medical therapy as in 7.1; wound care, offloading and infection treatment; **revascularisation** aiming for **direct in-line flow to the foot**; **amputation** where revascularisation is impossible or the foot unsalvageable. **Levels:** digital/ray; transmetatarsal (retains a weight-bearing foot); ⚑ **below-knee (transtibial) preserves the knee and therefore prosthetic ambulation — the level to aim for**; above-knee heals more reliably in poor perfusion but rarely permits walking. **Phantom limb pain** is common.

## 7.3 Acute limb ischaemia

**The short version**
- **The six Ps in order of appearance: Pain → Pallor → Pulselessness → Perishing cold → Paraesthesia → Paralysis.**
- ⚑ **Paraesthesia and paralysis are the two that matter** — a **threatened limb**. **Fixed non-blanching mottling + rigid tender muscles + anaesthesia and paralysis = irreversible.**
- Muscle tolerates roughly **4–6 hours** of complete ischaemia. **Immediate: IV unfractionated heparin, analgesia, bloods (K⁺, CK, lactate, group & hold), ECG, urgent vascular referral.**
- ⚑ **In a threatened (Rutherford IIb) limb, imaging must not delay revascularisation.**

| Feature | **Embolic** | **Thrombotic (in situ)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | **Sudden**; patient names the hour; more severe | **Gradual**, or a step-change on background claudication |
| **Source** | • **AF** (commonest)<br>• Recent **MI** with mural thrombus<br>• Prosthetic valve, endocarditis<br>• Proximal aneurysm (AAA, **popliteal**) | • **Atherosclerotic plaque rupture**<br>• Low flow, dehydration, thrombophilia<br>• **Graft or stent thrombosis** |
| **Prior claudication** | **Absent** — the limb was normal last week | **Present** — known PAD |
| **Contralateral pulses** | ⚑ **Normal** | ⚑ **Also absent/abnormal** — bilateral disease |
| **Collaterals** | **Few** → more profound ischaemia | **Well developed** → limb survives longer |
| **Angiography** | **Sharp cut-off**, meniscus sign, little atheroma elsewhere | **Tapered, irregular** occlusion, diffuse disease, many collaterals |
| **Management** | ⚑ **Fogarty embolectomy** + lifelong anticoagulation, treat the source | ⚑ **Angiography-guided**: thrombolysis, angioplasty or **bypass** — a Fogarty alone fails and may injure a diseased vessel |

| Rutherford | Sensory / motor | Doppler (arterial/venous) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IIa — marginal** | Toes or none / intact | Arterial often inaudible / venous audible | **Salvageable if promptly treated**; time for imaging |
| **IIb — immediate** | More than toes / **weakness** | Arterial usually inaudible / venous audible | ⚑ **Immediate revascularisation — theatre now** |
| **III — irreversible** | **Anaesthetic / paralysis, rigor** | **Both inaudible** | **Primary amputation** |

⚑ Loss of the **venous** Doppler signal is the bedside discriminator between IIb and III; revascularising a dead limb can kill the patient.

**Immediate management:** oxygen, IV access, **opioid analgesia**; **IV unfractionated heparin** bolus then infusion (UFH not LMWH — titratable and reversible before surgery) to limit propagation and protect the runoff; bloods, ECG, **urgent vascular referral**; limb dependent, NBM.

**Revascularisation.** **Embolectomy** — Fogarty catheter via femoral cut-down, feasible under LA in the unfit. **Catheter-directed thrombolysis** — better for thrombotic occlusion and graft thrombosis, but takes **hours**, so **not** for a IIb limb with motor loss. **Bypass** where the lesion needs anatomical correction; **primary amputation** for Rutherford III. Afterwards treat the cause — anticoagulate a cardiac source, echo/Holter, screen for aneurysms.

### ⚑ Reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome

Restoring flow washes out the products of anaerobic metabolism and cell death:
- **Hyperkalaemia** → peaked T waves, arrhythmia, arrest. Monitor K⁺ and ECG; calcium gluconate, insulin–dextrose, salbutamol, dialysis.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** → myoglobinuria ("Coca-Cola" urine), **AKI**, ↑↑CK; aggressive IV fluids. Also acidosis, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia.
- **Compartment syndrome** — reperfused muscle swells inside rigid fascia: **pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment.** ⚑ **Pulses and capillary refill may be preserved — do not wait for them to disappear.** The **calf** (four compartments, anterior most often) is the site; **urgent four-compartment fasciotomy**, with many units performing **prophylactic fasciotomy** after prolonged ischaemia.

## 7.4 Abdominal aortic aneurysm

**The short version**
- **AAA = infrarenal aortic diameter ≥3.0 cm** (or ≥1.5× the adjacent segment); >90% infrarenal.
- **Elective repair: ≥5.5 cm in men, ≥5.0 cm in women, growth >1.0 cm/year, or symptomatic.** So **4.2 cm asymptomatic = surveillance, NOT surgery**; **5.8 cm in a man = elective repair**.
- ⚑ **Australia has NO national AAA screening programme** — screening is targeted/opportunistic.
- **Ruptured AAA triad: sudden severe abdominal/back pain + hypotension + pulsatile expansile mass.** ⚑ Frequently **misdiagnosed as renal colic**. **Unstable → straight to theatre, no CT.**

**Aetiology.** Degenerative — medial elastin and collagen loss, transmural inflammation, matrix metalloproteinase activity — not simply "atherosclerosis." ⚑ **Smoking dominates**; also age >65, **male sex** (6× commoner, but ⚑ **women rupture at smaller diameters with worse outcomes**), **family history** (roughly doubles risk), hypertension, COPD. ⚑ **Diabetes is negatively associated** — a genuine exception. Also connective tissue disease, mycotic and inflammatory aneurysms. Most are **asymptomatic**; symptomatic ones give back/flank pain or distal atheroembolism (**"trash foot"**). **A tender AAA is impending rupture until proven otherwise.**

| Maximum diameter | ANZSVS | ESVS (recommended by RACGP) | SVS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.5–2.9 cm (ectatic) | Repeat US ~5 years | — | — |
| **3.0–3.9 cm** | **Every 2–5 years** | **24 months** | **3 years** |
| **4.0–4.4 cm** | **Annually** | **12 months** | **12 months** |
| **4.5–4.9 cm** | **6-monthly** | **6 months** | **12 months** |
| **5.0–5.4 cm** | **3-monthly** | **3 months** | **6-monthly** |
| **≥5.5 cm (men) / ≥5.0 cm (women)** | **Vascular surgical referral for repair** | | |

⚑ **ANZSVS has published no formal surveillance guideline** — RACGP therefore recommends the **ESVS** schedule, and the three columns above sit within one step of each other. If asked for one number, quote ESVS. Rupture below 4 cm is vanishingly rare, which is why the small-aneurysm intervals are long. The **repair thresholds** are consistent: **men ≥5.5 cm; women ≥5.0 cm; growth >1.0 cm/year; symptomatic or embolising at any diameter**; also saccular, mycotic, or iliac aneurysm ≥3.5 cm. Below threshold: surveillance plus **absolute smoking cessation** (the only intervention shown to slow expansion), BP control and statin. ⚑ **No drug reliably reduces AAA growth — doxycycline, beta-blockers and macrolides have all failed.**

**The two exam stems.** *Incidental 4.2 cm AAA, 68-year-old smoker* → **do not refer**; **12-monthly ultrasound** (4.0–4.4 cm is annual on all three schedules) plus hard risk-factor treatment and safety-netting — rupture risk <1%/year, below elective operative mortality. *5.8 cm AAA, 72-year-old man* → **refer for elective repair**, with CTA for EVAR planning and cardiorespiratory risk assessment.

**Screening.** ⚑ **No national Australian programme**, unlike the UK and Sweden (single ultrasound for men at 65); instead **opportunistic targeted** ultrasound (⚑ no dedicated MBS screening item exists) in **men >65 with a smoking history**, anyone **>65 with a family history**, **Marfan or Loeys–Dietz syndrome**, and anyone with a known **popliteal or thoracic aneurysm** (RACGP).

### Ruptured AAA

**Triad: sudden severe abdominal or back pain + hypotension/collapse + pulsatile expansile mass.** ⚑ **Misdiagnosis is the exam point** — it is mistaken for **renal colic** (identical loin-to-groin pain), diverticulitis, perforated viscus and musculoskeletal back pain. **A first episode of "renal colic" over 50–60, or back pain with haemodynamic disturbance, needs the aorta assessed.** Retroperitoneal rupture may tamponade and present sub-acutely.

**Immediate vascular surgery, anaesthetics and theatre activation**; two large-bore IVs, **urgent cross-match / massive transfusion protocol**, bloods, ECG. ⚑ **Permissive hypotension** — resuscitate to a **palpable radial pulse and preserved cerebration / systolic ~70–90 mmHg — ⚑ **lower than the ~80–90 quoted for blunt trauma (§5.3)**, because the aim is to avoid dislodging clot at the rupture site with maintained consciousness**, since aggressive crystalloid dislodges clot and worsens bleeding; prefer **blood products** and correct coagulopathy. ⚑ **Unstable with a convincing history → straight to theatre, no CT**; **stable** patients get rapid **CTA** for EVAR suitability, then **open repair or emergency EVAR**. Consider **ceiling of care** — operative mortality remains ~40–50%.

**EVAR vs open.** EVAR needs an adequate **infrarenal neck** and iliac access; open repair suits any anatomy and is required for juxta/suprarenal disease. ⚑ EVAR has **lower 30-day mortality** and shorter stay, but ⚑ **the survival advantage is lost by ~2–3 years**, with more reinterventions, persisting late rupture risk and mandatory ⚑ **lifelong imaging surveillance**. Open repair has higher perioperative mortality (MI, AKI, **colonic ischaemia**, spinal cord ischaemia, graft infection) but is **more durable**.

**Endoleak = persistent flow into the sac.** **I** (inadequate proximal/distal seal) and **III** (fabric defect/component separation) → **treat**; **II** (**retrograde lumbar/IMA flow, commonest**) → observe, treat if the sac expands; **IV** (porosity) and **V** (endotension) rarely need action.

### Thoracic aneurysm and aortic dissection — brief

**TAA** is usually asymptomatic; consider connective tissue disease or bicuspid aortic valve in the young. Thresholds are **around 5.5 cm for degenerative ascending disease, lower (~4.5–5.0 cm) in connective tissue disease or bicuspid valve**. **TEVAR** for descending disease.

**Aortic dissection** — intimal tear, blood tracking into the media to create a false lumen. Abrupt **tearing pain radiating to the interscapular back**, maximal at onset; **>20 mmHg inter-arm BP difference**; pulse deficit; new **aortic regurgitation**; malperfusion (stroke, **inferior MI** from right coronary involvement, paraplegia, mesenteric/renal or limb ischaemia). **CTA** diagnoses.
- ⚑ **Stanford: Type A involves the ascending aorta** (wherever the tear); **Type B does not**. Type A is ~two-thirds and a **surgical emergency** — graft replacement of the ascending aorta ± root/valve; untreated mortality rises ~1–2% per hour early on.
- ⚑ **Uncomplicated Type B = medical "impulse control"**: **IV beta-blocker first** (esmolol/labetalol) to **HR ~60**, then a vasodilator to **systolic ~100–120 mmHg** — reduce **dP/dt**, not just BP. ⚑ **Never give the vasodilator first** (reflex tachycardia extends the dissection). **TEVAR** for complicated type B.

## 7.5 Carotid disease and stroke prevention

**The short version**
- **Symptomatic** = ipsilateral TIA, amaurosis fugax or non-disabling stroke — ⚑ **the Australian guideline specifies a recent event, <3 months** (6 months is the NASCET/ESVS trial window). **70–99% → carotid endarterectomy, strong benefit; 50–69% → smaller benefit, individualise** (greater in men, older patients, hemispheric events); **<50% → no benefit.**
- ⚑ **Timing: operate once stable, ideally within 2 weeks (14 days) of the index event** — recurrent stroke risk is front-loaded and the NNT rises steeply with delay.
- **CEA is preferred to stenting** in most symptomatic patients, especially over 70.
- **Asymptomatic:** ⚑ **the Australian Stroke Foundation guideline says carotid endarterectomy or stenting should NOT be performed** for asymptomatic stenosis — best medical therapy only. The "intervene in selected higher-risk patients at centres with complication rates <3%" position is US/SVS framing; do not quote it in an Australian paper.

Duplex first, confirmed by **CTA or MRA**; stenosis is graded by **NASCET** (against the distal normal ICA) — ⚑ the older **ECST** method gives higher percentages for the same lesion. A **bruit is neither sensitive nor specific**.

**CEA vs stenting.** ⚑ CEA has **lower periprocedural stroke and death**, particularly over 70; stenting causes less MI and no cranial nerve injury, and suits a **hostile neck** (surgery/radiotherapy), high bifurcation, restenosis after CEA, or severe cardiac comorbidity. Stenting needs **DAPT**; aspirin continues through CEA. All patients get antiplatelet therapy, **high-intensity statin**, BP and diabetes control and smoking cessation.

**Complications of CEA.** **Stroke** (~2–3%) and death. **Cranial nerve injury** — **hypoglossal (XII)**: tongue deviates to the operated side; **vagus/recurrent laryngeal (X)**: hoarseness; **marginal mandibular VII**: drooping mouth corner. ⚑ **An expanding neck haematoma compromises the airway and needs immediate wound opening at the bedside.** Also carotid-sinus haemodynamic instability, MI, restenosis, and ⚑ **cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome** days later (ipsilateral headache, seizures, focal deficit, intracerebral haemorrhage) — **treat with strict BP control**.

## 7.6 Venous disease

**The short version**
- Varicose veins = dilated tortuous superficial veins from **valvular incompetence and venous hypertension**: **great saphenous** (medial thigh/calf → sapheno-femoral junction, groin) or **small saphenous** (posterior calf → sapheno-popliteal junction).
- Aching **worse at the end of the day, relieved by elevation** — the opposite of arterial pain.
- **Duplex** defines the incompetent junction and **excludes deep venous obstruction** before any superficial vein is destroyed; **endovenous ablation has largely replaced stripping**.
- **CVI skin changes: haemosiderin staining, varicose eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche.**

**Risk factors:** age, female sex, parity, family history, obesity, prolonged standing, previous DVT. **Intervene for:** persistent symptoms despite conservative measures; **CVI skin changes**; **venous ulceration**; **superficial thrombophlebitis**; **bleeding from a varicosity** (elevation and direct pressure). Cosmesis alone is not an indication.

**Treatment.** Conservative: weight loss, calf-pump exercise, elevation, **graduated compression stockings after an ABI check**. **Endovenous thermal ablation** (radiofrequency or laser) under tumescent LA — first-line; alternatives are glue, mechanochemical ablation, **ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy** and **surgery** (SFJ ligation and stripping, phlebectomy) for very large veins. Complications: recurrence, **saphenous nerve injury with GSV below knee, sural nerve with SSV**, DVT.

**Chronic venous insufficiency.** Sustained ambulatory venous hypertension → capillary leak of fibrinogen and red cells → **haemosiderin staining** (brown gaiter pigmentation); **varicose eczema** (itchy scaly weeping dermatitis — watch for contact allergy to topicals); **lipodermatosclerosis** (dermal fibrosis, tight woody tapered calf = ⚑ **"inverted champagne bottle" leg**); **atrophie blanche** (ivory-white atrophic plaques, ulcer-prone); **ulceration at the medial gaiter area**. Graded by **CEAP**. Manage with compression (after ABI), elevation, exercise and emollients, plus ⚑ **early endovenous ablation of superficial reflux, which speeds ulcer healing and reduces recurrence** (EVRA).

**Superficial thrombophlebitis.** Tender erythematous palpable **cord** along a superficial vein. ⚑ Not trivial — it extends into the deep system in a meaningful minority, so **do a duplex**, especially above the knee or near the sapheno-femoral junction. NSAIDs, compression, mobilise; **anticoagulate (prophylactic LMWH or fondaparinux, ~6 weeks) for long-segment (≥5 cm) or junction-proximal disease**. Recurrent migratory thrombophlebitis (**Trousseau's sign**) → seek occult malignancy.

## 7.7 Leg ulcers

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Venous ~70%, arterial ~10%, neuropathic ~10%** — but **assume mixed until the ABI says otherwise**.
- ⚑ **Compression treats venous ulcers and is dangerous in arterial disease — NEVER compress without measuring the ABI first.**
- **Any ulcer unhealed at ~3 months, or with heaped rolled everted edges, gets biopsied** — Marjolin's.

| Feature | **Venous** | **Arterial** | **Neuropathic (diabetic)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Site** | **Medial gaiter area**, above medial malleolus | **Pressure/end points** — toes, heel, lateral malleolus, bony prominences | **Plantar pressure points** — **metatarsal heads**, heel, under callus |
| **Edge and depth** | Sloping, irregular, **shallow** | **Punched-out, well-demarcated, deep** | **Punched-out with thick surrounding callus; deep** |
| **Base** | Granulating, sloughy, exudative | **Pale, grey, necrotic**; may expose tendon/bone | Deeper than it looks — **probe it** |
| **Pain** | Aching, **relieved by elevation** | **Severe, worse on elevation and at night, eased by dependency** | ⚑ **Painless** — the diagnostic clue |
| **Surrounding skin** | Haemosiderin, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche | Cool, shiny, hairless; dependent rubor | **Callus**, dry cracked skin, deformity (clawed toes, **Charcot**) |
| **Pulses** | **Present** | **Absent/reduced** | Often present — **may be absent (neuroischaemic)** |
| **ABI** | **>0.8** | **<0.8, usually <0.5** | ⚑ **May be falsely normal or >1.4** — use **TBI** |
| **Management** | ⚑ **Graduated multilayer compression**, elevation, **early ablation of superficial reflux** | ⚑ **Revascularise — no compression.** Analgesia, wound care, risk factors | **Offloading** (⚑ **non-removable knee-high device first-line**), debride callus, glycaemic control, MDT foot service |

**The compression safety rule** — write it out in any ulcer answer. **Measure the ABI with Doppler in both legs before any compression.** **≥0.8** takes full compression (~40 mmHg at the ankle, multilayer); **below 0.8** Wounds Australia does **not** authorise routine compression — refer for vascular assessment and specialist-supervised reduced compression at most; ⚑ international guidelines put the hard contraindication anywhere from **<0.5 to <0.8**, so the safe answer in a written paper is **compress above 0.8, refer below it**; **>1.40 (§7.1), or diabetes/CKD**, means the reading is unreliable, so get toe pressures. Re-measure periodically. ⚑ **Antibiotics only for clinical infection; never treat a colonised ulcer.**

**Marjolin's ulcer.** ⚑ **Squamous cell carcinoma arising in chronically inflamed or scarred skin** — long-standing venous ulcers, burn scars, sinus tracts, chronic osteomyelitis. Suspect on **heaped rolled everted edges, exuberant granulation, or failure to heal**; **biopsy the margin, not the necrotic centre**. Also consider **pyoderma gangrenosum** (violaceous undermined border, pathergy — ⚑ **do not debride**).

## 7.8 The diabetic foot

**The short version**
- **Neuropathy + ischaemia + infection** — **neuropathy is the permissive lesion**, removing the pain that would force protection; it is **sensory**, **motor** (clawing → new pressure points) and **autonomic** (dry cracked skin, AV shunting).
- ⚑ **ABI is unreliable in diabetes** — use **toe pressure/TBI**. **Screening intervals shorten with risk**, from annually at very low risk to every 1–3 months at high risk.
- ⚑ **Charcot: a hot, red, swollen, relatively painless foot with intact skin and bounding pulses** — immobilise; it is not cellulitis.

**Pathophysiology.** Sensory neuropathy removes protective sensation, so repetitive trauma goes unnoticed. Motor neuropathy wastes the intrinsics → **clawed toes, prominent metatarsal heads** → focal pressure → callus → subcallus haemorrhage → ulcer. Autonomic neuropathy → **dry fissured skin** and AV shunting that makes a poorly perfused foot feel deceptively warm. **PAD** (infrapopliteal, diffuse, calcified) impairs healing and antibiotic delivery; infection then spreads along tendon sheaths.

**Assessment.** **10 g monofilament** at defined plantar sites (inability to feel it = **loss of protective sensation**), 128 Hz tuning fork, ankle reflexes; pulses with **ABI and TBI/toe pressures** — ⚑ never the ABI alone, and **ankle pressure <50 mmHg, ABI <0.5 or toe pressure <30 mmHg → urgent vascular imaging/revascularisation**. Inspect skin, interdigital spaces, nails, deformity and footwear; ⚑ **previous ulcer or amputation is the strongest predictor of a future ulcer**. **Screen and prevent:** risk-stratify at least annually — **very low risk annually; low 6–12 monthly; moderate 3–6 monthly; high risk (previous ulcer/amputation, ESKD, severe deformity/PAD) every 1–3 months** — with education (daily self-inspection, never barefoot), **therapeutic footwear**, podiatry and risk-factor control.

**Charcot neuroarthropathy.** Unrecognised microfracture in an insensate, well-perfused foot triggers uncontrolled inflammation and bone resorption, then collapse → the **rocker-bottom foot**.

| | **Acute Charcot** | **Infection / osteomyelitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Skin | ⚑ **Intact — no wound, no portal of entry** | Ulcer, wound or fissure present |
| Pain / temperature / elevation | Disproportionately **mild**; ⚑ **>2 °C hotter than the other foot**; ⚑ **redness resolves on elevation** | More painful; warm; redness **persists** |
| Bloods / X-ray | CRP/WCC normal or mildly raised; film early normal, later fragmentation and midfoot collapse | Fever; CRP/ESR/WCC raised (blunted in diabetes); cortical erosion, periosteal reaction, sequestrum |
| Treatment | ⚑ **Immediate offloading in a total contact cast / non-removable knee-high device for months**, until temperature normalises; failure to immobilise causes irreversible deformity | Antibiotics ± drainage/debridement |

MRI distinguishes them if uncertain.

**Osteomyelitis.** Suspect in any ulcer that is deep, overlies bone, has lasted >2 weeks or fails to heal; a **"sausage toe"** is classic. ⚑ **Probe-to-bone test:** a **hard gritty** surface on advancing a sterile blunt probe is positive, and in a deep infected ulcer largely **rules in** bone. ⚑ **First-line workup: probe-to-bone + ESR/CRP + plain radiograph**; films lag by ~2 weeks, so **repeat in 2–4 weeks** if suspicion persists, and ⚑ **MRI is the imaging test of choice** if still uncertain. **Bone biopsy and culture** is the gold standard — superficial swabs must not direct antibiotics. Treat with prolonged targeted antibiotics ± resection of infected bone, and ⚑ **revascularise first if ischaemic — antibiotics do not reach tissue with no blood supply.**

**Management principle.** ⚑ **Multidisciplinary "high-risk foot service"** — strong evidence for reducing amputation, and the answer the examiner wants: physician, vascular surgeon, podiatrist, wound/diabetes nurse, orthotist, infectious diseases and orthopaedic surgeon, with rapid access to revascularisation. Concurrently **offload, debride** (⚑ not in ischaemic or dry gangrenous feet), **treat infection, revascularise and control glycaemia and cardiovascular risk**.

## 7.9 Vascular access and other

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Fistula first: autologous AV fistula > AV graft > tunnelled central venous catheter** — best patency, least infection. **Radiocephalic (wrist) before brachiocephalic (elbow) before brachiobasilic transposition**; fistulae need **weeks to months to mature**. ⚑ **Never take a BP, venepuncture or cannula from a fistula arm.**

**AV fistula.** The anastomosis **arterialises** the vein, which dilates and thickens for repeated large-bore cannulation. A healthy fistula has a **palpable thrill and continuous machinery bruit**; ⚑ a **pulsatile fistula with only a systolic bruit suggests outflow stenosis**. Complications: failure to mature; **stenosis and thrombosis** (commonest cause of access loss — rising venous pressures on dialysis, prolonged bleeding after needle removal, loss of the thrill; treat by fistuloplasty); ⚑ **steal syndrome** (cold, painful, pale hand, worse **during dialysis** — banding, DRIL or ligation); ⚑ **high-output cardiac failure**; cannulation-site aneurysm risking catastrophic haemorrhage; infection.

**Central venous access.** *Insertion:* ⚑ **pneumothorax** (subclavian > IJ; ⚑ **ultrasound guidance is now standard and markedly reduces mechanical complications**), arterial puncture, haemothorax, air embolism, guidewire arrhythmia, malposition, thoracic duct injury, retained guidewire. *Delayed:* **catheter-related bloodstream infection** (avoid femoral; strict asepsis; remove the line once not needed), thrombosis, central vein stenosis. **Confirm the tip at the cavo-atrial junction and exclude pneumothorax before use.**

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Buttock/thigh claudication + erectile dysfunction + absent femoral pulses | **Leriche syndrome** — aorto-iliac occlusion at the bifurcation |
| ABI diagnostic of PAD; ABI >1.40 in a diabetic | **≤0.90**; >1.40 = **incompressible**, so use **TBI** (PAD if **<0.70**) |
| Foot pain worse lying flat, eased by hanging the leg out of bed | **Ischaemic rest pain** → CLTI (⚑ replaces "critical limb ischaemia") |
| The two of the six Ps marking a threatened limb | **Paraesthesia and paralysis** |
| ALI, sudden onset, AF, **normal** contralateral pulses, no claudication | **Embolic** → Fogarty **embolectomy** + anticoagulation (**thrombotic** = absent contralateral pulses, prior claudication → thrombolysis/bypass) |
| Peaked T waves, dark urine, ↑↑CK after revascularisation | **Reperfusion injury**; check for **compartment syndrome** → fasciotomy |
| Elective AAA repair thresholds (AAA = aorta ≥3.0 cm) | **≥5.5 cm men, ≥5.0 cm women, growth >1.0 cm/yr, or symptomatic** |
| Incidental asymptomatic 4.2 cm AAA | **12-monthly ultrasound + risk factor therapy**, no operation. ⚑ **No national Australian screening programme** |
| Sudden back pain + hypotension + pulsatile mass, unstable | **Ruptured AAA → theatre, no CT**; permissive hypotension (~70–90 systolic); often misread as **renal colic** |
| Commonest endoleak after EVAR | **Type II** (lumbar/IMA) — observe; **I and III need treatment**. Tongue deviating to the operated side after CEA = **hypoglossal (XII)** injury |
| Uncomplicated type B dissection — drug order | ⚑ **Beta-blocker (HR ~60) FIRST, then vasodilator**; type A → theatre |
| Symptomatic 70–99% carotid stenosis — treatment, timing | **Endarterectomy, ideally within 2 weeks (14 days)** of the event |
| ABI below which compression is contraindicated | ⚑ **<0.5** (full compression safe **≥0.8**; **0.5–0.8** modified only) |
| Leg ulcer unhealed at ~3 months, rolled everted edges | **Biopsy the margin — Marjolin's ulcer (SCC)** |
| Hot, swollen, red, painless foot, skin intact, redness settles on elevation | **Acute Charcot** → total contact cast, not antibiotics |
| Suspected diabetic foot osteomyelitis — first tests | **Probe-to-bone + ESR/CRP + plain film**; **MRI** if uncertain |
| Cold painful hand during dialysis in a fistula arm | **Steal syndrome** |

## 8.3 Confusable pairs

| Pair | The discriminator |
|---|---|
| Extradural vs subdural haematoma | Extradural: young, middle meningeal artery, **lucid interval**, biconvex, **no suture crossing**. Subdural: elderly/anticoagulated, bridging veins, crescentic, **crosses sutures** |
| Tension pneumothorax vs massive haemothorax | Tension **hyper-resonant, veins distended**; haemothorax **stony dull, veins flat** |
| Neurogenic vs haemorrhagic shock | Neurogenic **bradycardic, warm** (sympathetic loss above T6); haemorrhagic **tachycardic, cool, clammy**. Assume bleeding |
| Direct vs indirect inguinal hernia | **Medial** to inferior epigastrics = direct (acquired, wide neck, rarely scrotal, low strangulation risk); **lateral** = indirect (congenital, narrow neck, may be scrotal) |
| Inguinal vs femoral hernia | Neck **superomedial** to pubic tubercle = inguinal; **inferolateral** = femoral. Judge by the neck, not the fundus |
| Incarcerated vs strangulated | Incarcerated **mechanical**, perfused. Strangulated **vascular**: constant pain, exquisite tenderness, no cough impulse — never reduce |
| Small vs large bowel obstruction | SBO **early vomiting, late constipation**, central loops, valvulae cross lumen, >3 cm. LBO **early constipation**, peripheral loops, >6 cm |
| Ileus vs mechanical obstruction | Ileus **quiet, continuous from surgery**, gas to rectum, **no transition point**. Obstruction **colicky, tinkling**, **transition point** |
| Adhesional obstruction vs strangulation | Simple: **colic with pain-free intervals**, soft abdomen. Strangulation: **constant** pain, fever, guarding, ↑lactate (both late) |
| Appendicitis vs mesenteric adenitis | Appendicitis: **migration**, anorexia→pain→vomiting, RIF percussion tenderness. *Sections 1–7 give adenitis no features (§3.1 only)* |
| Diverticulitis vs colorectal cancer | **CT cannot separate them** — hence interval colonoscopy 6–8 weeks after any CT-proven episode |
| Sigmoid vs caecal volvulus | Sigmoid **elderly, institutionalised**, coffee-bean **RUQ** → decompress, then sigmoidectomy. Caecal **younger**, caecum **LUQ**, SBO behind → right hemicolectomy |
| Acute mesenteric ischaemia vs ischaemic colitis | AMI occlusive SMA, **pain out of proportion**, soft abdomen, shocked → revascularise. Colitis watershed, **cramps then bloody diarrhoea**, well → conservative |
| Acute limb ischaemia — embolic vs thrombotic | Embolic **sudden**, AF, **no claudication**, **normal contralateral pulses** → **Fogarty**. Thrombotic gradual on known PAD, **contralateral pulses abnormal** → thrombolysis/bypass |
| Claudication vs CLTI vs spinal stenosis | Claudication **relieved by standing still**. CLTI **rest pain** worse flat, eased by dependency, ± tissue loss. Spinal needs **flexion/sitting** |
| Venous vs arterial vs neuropathic ulcer | Venous **medial gaiter**, shallow, haemosiderin, ABI >0.8. Arterial **punched-out, deep**, pulseless, **worse on elevation**. Neuropathic **painless**, plantar callus, ABI unreliable |
| Fibroadenoma vs phyllodes vs cancer | Fibroadenoma 15–35 y, <3 cm, mobile. Phyllodes **40–55 y, rapid, >4 cm** → excision ≥1 cm, no axilla. Cancer hard, fixed. **Most suspicious leg governs** |
| Lactational abscess vs inflammatory breast cancer | Abscess lactating, **febrile**, wedge erythema, improves by 48 h. IBC weeks-progressive, **afebrile**, ≥⅓ breast, peau d'orange, **antibiotic-unresponsive** |
| Paget's of the nipple vs eczema | Paget's **starts on the nipple**, unilateral, **no steroid response**, cancer beneath. Eczema **starts on areola, spares nipple**, steroid-responsive |
| Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcaemia vs RLN injury vs haematoma | Hypocalcaemia **12–48 h**: tingling, long QTc. RLN: **voice** — bilateral gives **stridor with near-normal voice**. Haematoma **hours**: expanding neck |
| Anastomotic leak vs ileus vs collection | Leak day 5–7: **tachycardia, new AF, fever**, CRP not falling → CT. Ileus quiet, afebrile — stubborn ileus is a leak. Collection **day 5+**: swinging fever |
| Atelectasis vs pneumonia vs PE as post-op fever | **Atelectasis does not cause fever** — hypoxia only. Pneumonia day ≥3: purulent sputum, consolidation. PE day 5+: pleuritic pain, hypoxia, new AF — **CTPA, not D-dimer** |
| Compartment syndrome vs acute limb ischaemia | Compartment: **pulses normal until late**, tense, agonising passive stretch → fasciotomy. ALI: **pulselessness early**, perishing cold → heparin, revascularise |
| Superficial vs deep partial thickness burn | Superficial partial: pink, blistered, **brisk refill**, very painful, ~2 weeks. Deep partial: blotchy red/white, **sluggish refill**, **blunted sensation**, grafted |
| Papillary vs follicular thyroid cancer | Papillary ~80%, **lymphatic to cervical nodes**, **FNA diagnostic**. Follicular ~10%, **haematogenous to bone/lung**, **FNA cannot diagnose** — needs lobectomy |
| Primary vs secondary vs tertiary hyperparathyroidism | Primary **↑Ca, ↑/inappropriately normal PTH, ↓PO₄** → surgery. Secondary **↓/normal Ca, ↑PTH** (CKD) → medical. Tertiary **↑Ca AND ↑↑PTH** → subtotal excision |
| Cellulitis vs necrotising fasciitis | Cellulitis pain **proportional**, **improves on antibiotics**. Nec fasc pain **out of proportion**, spreads over **hours**, toxic, crepitus, **worsens on antibiotics** |
| Mid-point of inguinal ligament vs mid-inguinal point | Mid-point of **ligament** (ASIS→pubic **tubercle**): deep ring 1.5 cm above. Mid-**inguinal** point (ASIS→**symphysis**): femoral artery |
| DCIS vs LCIS | DCIS **true precursor at that site**, microcalcification → excision ± RT. LCIS **risk marker, both breasts**, imaging-occult → surveillance |
| Acute Charcot vs foot osteomyelitis | Charcot **skin intact**, **redness resolves on elevation** → total contact cast. Osteomyelitis: wound, redness **persists**, ↑CRP → probe-to-bone, MRI |
| Type A vs type B aortic dissection | **A involves the ascending aorta** wherever the tear — surgery. **B does not** — uncomplicated: beta-blocker to HR ~60 **first**, then vasodilator |
| Rutherford IIb vs III limb ischaemia | IIb loss beyond toes with **weakness**, **venous Doppler audible** → immediate revascularisation. III **anaesthetic, paralysed, rigid** → primary amputation |

| Diagnosis | Feature that should raise it | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| Tension pneumothorax | Hyper-resonant hemithorax, hypotension, distended neck veins | Needle decompression before imaging, then ICC |
| Cardiac tamponade | Beck's triad; PEA arrest | Subxiphoid FAST → surgical decompression |
| Massive haemothorax | Stony dull hemithorax, shock, flat veins | Blood with ICC; thoracotomy >1500 mL or >200 mL/hr |

## 8.6 High-yield reasoning chains

**1. Post-operative fever by day.** Days 0–2 cytokine response to injury; 3–5 catheter UTI then pneumonia; 5–7 SSI and, after bowel surgery, leak; beyond 7 collection or VTE; inside 24 h necrotising infection, malignant hyperthermia, transfusion. **Therefore** date the fever, inspect wound, cannulae and legs, send lactate and cultures; if bowel was joined, CT with contrast. Never "atelectasis": that causes hypoxia, not pyrexia.

**2. The acute abdomen by quadrant.** Visceral pain is midline by gut division (foregut epigastric, midgut periumbilical, hindgut suprapubic) and localises only once the parietal peritoneum is inflamed. **Therefore** migration is the key history item: RIF → appendicitis, LIF → diverticulitis or sigmoid cancer, epigastrium → perforated ulcer or ruptured AAA, diffuse → perforation or mesenteric ischaemia. βhCG in every woman of reproductive age.

**3. The anticoagulated head injury.** The Canadian CT Head Rule excludes anticoagulated patients — their risk is uncaptured and these bleeds evolve late. **Therefore** CT regardless of GCS, and reverse without awaiting neurosurgery: **idarucizumab 5 g** for dabigatran, **Beriplex (4-factor PCC) 50 IU/kg** for apixaban/rivaroxaban (⚑ andexanet alfa was removed from the ARTG on 20 May 2026), and **vitamin K 5–10 mg IV + Beriplex 50 IU/kg, without FFP**, for warfarin — ⚑ FFP was only required alongside the old **3-factor** Prothrombinex-VF, which Beriplex replaced in **June 2024**. Platelets are **not** routine.

**4. The burn fluid calculation** (80 kg, 30% TBSA, 1 h after injury). ANZBA: 3 mL × kg × %TBSA Hartmann's/24 h, clock from **injury**, half within 8 h. **Therefore** 3 × 80 × 30 = **7200 mL**; first half **3600 mL** over the remaining **7 h ≈ 514 mL/hr**; second half over 16 h **≈ 225 mL/hr**; urine target **40 mL/hr**.

**5. The groin lump.** The **neck** defines it — superomedial to the pubic tubercle inguinal, inferolateral femoral — and the inferior epigastric vessels separate indirect (lateral) from direct (medial). **Therefore** examine standing, find the neck, test cough impulse. Young man, reducible, asymptomatic → elective mesh repair; a femoral hernia is **never** watched; tender, irreducible, unwell → never reduce, theatre.

**6. The post-thyroidectomy patient.** Three complications compete, each with its own tempo. **Therefore** expanding neck with distress at **hours** = haematoma → open the wound at the bedside before attempting intubation; stridor at extubation with a near-normal voice = bilateral RLN palsy → reintubate; hoarse bovine cough = unilateral palsy; tingling and carpopedal spasm at **12–48 h** = hypocalcaemia → Ca, Mg, PTH, QTc.

**7. The ischaemic limb.** Six Ps in order; **paraesthesia and paralysis** mark a threatened limb, and muscle tolerates only ~4–6 h of ischaemia. **Therefore** IV **unfractionated** heparin (titratable, reversible before surgery), opioids, K⁺/CK/lactate, urgent referral. AF with normal contralateral pulses and no claudication = embolic → Fogarty; abnormal contralateral pulses with claudication = thrombotic → thrombolysis or bypass.

**8. The vomiting patient's acid–base picture and the correct fluid** (Na 138, Cl 92, HCO₃ 34, K 3.0). Gastric HCl loss strips H⁺ and Cl⁻; volume and chloride depletion force renal Na⁺ reabsorption in exchange for K⁺ and H⁺, wasting potassium and acidifying urine despite alkalosis (**paradoxical aciduria**, urine Cl⁻ <20). **Therefore** give chloride: **0.9% saline with potassium**, not Hartmann's.
