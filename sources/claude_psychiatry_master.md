# Psychiatry Notes — Section 1: Assessment, Formulation and Risk
*Sources: RANZCP CPGs (deliberate self-harm; schizophrenia GRADE 2026; initial physical health assessment for psychosis ANZ 2026), Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, RACGP, AIHW, ACSQHC NSQHS Standards 2nd ed, National SEWB Framework 2017–2023. Scope: taking, examining, formulating and risk-assessing a psychiatric presentation, and excluding organic mimics before committing to a psychiatric diagnosis.*

## 1.1 The psychiatric history

**The short version**
- Same skeleton as a medical history plus five domains medicine omits: **risk, forensic, developmental/personal, premorbid personality, substance use**.
- Presenting complaint goes in **the patient's own words, in quotes** — the idiom carries diagnostic information ("my thoughts are being taken out") that paraphrase destroys.
- **Collateral is not optional in psychosis, mania or eating disorders.**
- Always ask what has changed *from this person's baseline* — psychiatry grades deviation from premorbid function.

**Structure.** HOPC (onset, course, precipitants, prodrome, functional impact); past psychiatric history (admissions **voluntary vs involuntary**, treatment response, prior attempts, treatment orders); medications, adherence, agents ceased and why; medical and family history (psychiatric *and* completed suicide); substance use; forensic; personal/developmental; premorbid personality; social circumstances; risk.

**Substance use** — alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, methamphetamine, opioids, benzodiazepines: quantity, route, frequency, duration, **last use**, withdrawal history (seizures, DTs), dependence. Route matters (injecting → BBV screening); last use matters because separating substance-induced from primary psychosis rests on temporal relationship and persistence beyond the expected intoxication/withdrawal window.

**Forensic history** — charges, convictions, custodial time, bail/parole/AVOs, violence irrespective of charge. Not voyeurism: it changes risk formulation, disposition and legal framework (forensic vs civil), and pending charges are a live stressor.

**Developmental and personal history** — birth complications, milestones, schooling (attainment, bullying, disruption), abuse and neglect, relationships, sexual history, occupation, supports, finances. The predisposing factors for the formulation (§1.3) live here.

**Premorbid personality** — how the person was when well, ideally from an informant. Distinguishes a personality disorder (lifelong, pervasive) from the state change of an episodic illness. Do not diagnose personality from one presentation in crisis.

⚑ **Why collateral is mandatory in psychosis, mania and eating disorders:** all three feature *impaired insight into the abnormality itself*. The psychotic patient does not experience the delusion as a symptom; the manic patient experiences elevated mood, disinhibition and reduced sleep need as wellness; the eating-disordered patient minimises restriction, purging and exercise as ego-syntonic. Self-report systematically *under*states severity, so an assessment without collateral is not shorter — it is unreliable. It also establishes rate of change and premorbid baseline. Sources: family, GP, case manager, ambulance/police, old records. Consent is preferred, but where the patient lacks capacity or refuses and there is risk, jurisdictions permit *receiving* third-party information.

## 1.2 The Mental State Examination

**The short version**
- MSE = cross-sectional observation *now*; the history is the longitudinal narrative. Do not import history into the MSE.
- Order: appearance/behaviour → speech → mood & affect → thought form → thought content → perception → cognition → insight → judgement.
- **Mood is subjective and sustained; affect is observed and momentary** — mood is climate, affect is weather.
- **Form** = how thought is organised; **content** = what it is about.

**Appearance and behaviour** — self-care, grooming, weight, stigmata (track marks, self-harm scars, jaundice, tremor), psychomotor retardation vs agitation, abnormal movements (tardive dyskinesia; akathisia — inner restlessness, distinguish from agitation), eye contact, rapport. Catatonic signs: stupor, mutism, negativism, posturing, waxy flexibility. **Speech** — rate, volume, quantity, tone, latency: pressured (rapid, hard to interrupt) vs poverty of speech (alogia); dysarthria/dysphasia signal neurology.

**Mood vs affect** — *Mood*: the patient's own sustained, pervasive emotional state, quoted, with a severity rating. *Affect*: observed moment-to-moment expression on four axes — **quality**, **range** (full, restricted, blunted, flat), **reactivity**, **congruity** with stated mood and content. Incongruent affect (laughing while describing a bereavement) is a schizophrenia-spectrum flag.

**Thought form** — organisation of thought, inferred from speech:

| Term | Definition and association |
|---|---|
| Circumstantiality | Over-inclusive, digressive, but **eventually reaches the point** — obsessionality, epilepsy, normal |
| Tangentiality | Veers off and **never returns** — psychosis, mania, organic |
| Flight of ideas | Rapid ideas with **discernible links** (puns, clang, distraction) — mania |
| Loosening of associations (derailment, "knight's move") | Links between ideas **absent or opaque to the listener** — schizophrenia |
| Word salad | **No coherent syntax or meaning** — extreme derailment |

Also thought blocking (abrupt cessation mid-stream), neologisms, perseveration (organic), echolalia.

**Thought content** — preoccupations, ruminations, obsessions (intrusive, ego-dystonic, resisted, **recognised as own** — contrast thought insertion), compulsions, overvalued ideas, delusions, suicidal/homicidal ideation.

- **Delusion**: a fixed, false belief held with total conviction, out of keeping with the person's cultural and educational background, unshakeable by argument or contrary evidence.
- **Overvalued idea**: a comprehensible, non-bizarre belief pursued beyond reasonable proportion and dominating the person's life, but **held with less than delusional conviction and conceded as possibly wrong**. The distinction is conviction, not content — anorexia, dysmorphophobia, morbid jealousy.

**Named delusional themes**

| Theme | Content |
|---|---|
| Persecutory | Being harmed, followed, poisoned, conspired against (commonest) |
| Grandiose | Inflated worth, power, identity, special mission — mania, neurosyphilis |
| Nihilistic | Self, a body part or the world does not exist / is destroyed / rotting |
| Reference | Neutral events, media or remarks carry personal significance for the patient |
| Capgras | Familiar person replaced by an identical impostor (cf. Fregoli — strangers in disguise) |
| Cotard | Being dead, having no organs, damned — psychotic depression, elderly |
| de Clérambault (erotomania) | A higher-status person is secretly in love with the patient |

**Passivity and thought alienation** — the self/world boundary is breached. **Thought insertion**: thoughts placed in the mind by an external agency, experienced as *not the patient's own*. **Thought withdrawal**: thoughts removed. **Thought broadcasting**: thoughts escape and become accessible to others unspoken. **Passivity (made phenomena)**: actions, impulses, emotions or bodily sensations controlled from outside ("made volition", "somatic passivity"). ⚑ These Schneiderian first-rank symptoms are **no longer diagnostically specific for schizophrenia** — DSM-5 dropped their special weighting; they occur in mania, psychotic depression and organic psychoses.

**Perception** — **illusion**: misperception of a *real* external stimulus (curtain shadow seen as a figure); normal and common in delirium. **Hallucination**: percept *without* external stimulus, in external space, with the full force of a true percept, not under voluntary control. **Pseudohallucination**: in *inner subjective space* and/or recognised as unreal, insight retained (contested term — describe the phenomenon). Specify modality. Auditory: elementary vs second-person ("you are worthless") vs third-person running commentary or voices conversing. **Visual, olfactory, gustatory and tactile hallucinations point to an organic or substance cause** until proven otherwise (visual → delirium, Lewy body dementia, withdrawal; olfactory/gustatory → temporal lobe epilepsy aura; tactile formication → stimulants, alcohol withdrawal). Also record depersonalisation/derealisation; **hypnagogic** and **hypnopompic** hallucinations are normal.

**Cognition** — orientation, attention, registration and recall, language, praxis, executive function. Screen with MMSE, MoCA (better for executive deficits) or RUDAS (built for CALD populations). Acute fluctuating inattention = delirium (§1.5).

**Insight** — not binary; three axes: does the patient recognise the experiences as abnormal, attribute them to mental illness, and accept treatment? Treatment can be accepted while illness is denied, and vice versa. **Judgement** — reasoning through consequences and acting on it, tested on the **actual current situation** ("what will you do tonight if the voices tell you to leave the ward?"), not hypotheticals about stamped letters; it is not legal capacity, which is decision-specific and statutory.

## 1.3 Case formulation

**The short version**
- **Diagnosis** classifies the disorder (what); **formulation** explains *this* person's presentation *now* (why this person, why this problem, why now) and drives the plan.
- The syllabus wants an **aetiological statement** integrating **biological, psychological and cultural/social** contributors.
- Tool: the **4 P grid** — predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective — crossed with biopsychosocial.
- **Never leave the protective column empty** — it is where the plan gets its leverage, and the column students forget.

| | Biological | Psychological | Social / cultural |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predisposing** (remote) | Family history, birth complications, chronic illness, ABI | Early trauma, attachment, cognitive style | Poverty, migration, dislocation from Country, disrupted schooling |
| **Precipitating** (trigger) | Substance use/withdrawal, non-adherence, new illness | Loss, humiliation, anniversary reaction | Relationship breakdown, job loss, eviction |
| **Perpetuating** | Ongoing substance use, side effects, pain, poor sleep | Avoidance, rumination, hopelessness | Homelessness, unemployment, isolation, high expressed emotion |
| **Protective** | Treatment response, physical health, abstinence | Insight, help-seeking, coping skills | Engaged family, housing, employment, cultural connection |

**Worked example.** 24-year-old man, second admission with persecutory delusions and third-person hallucinations, three weeks after ceasing aripiprazole and escalating cannabis, following a relationship breakdown. **Predisposing:** paternal uncle with schizophrenia; isolated adolescence; cannabis-using peer group since 15. **Precipitating:** medication cessation, escalating cannabis, humiliating relationship breakdown, loss of housing. **Perpetuating:** ongoing cannabis; sedation driving non-adherence; belief his life is over; unstable housing, no income, disengaged. **Protective:** prior response to aripiprazole; physically well; mother willing to be involved; presented voluntarily. Management maps off the grid — reinstate the agent that worked while fixing the side effect driving non-adherence (consider LAI), cannabis intervention, housing referral, psychoeducation with the mother, assertive follow-up. **Aetiological statement:** *"An acute psychotic relapse in a young man with familial vulnerability and long-standing cannabis use, precipitated by antipsychotic cessation and interpersonal loss, maintained by ongoing substance use and housing instability, with good prior treatment response and family support."*

**Why formulation ≠ diagnosis.** A diagnosis is a category label with defined criteria (ICD-11/DSM-5-TR): reliable, communicable, linked to evidence-based treatment — but it says nothing about *this* individual. A formulation is individual, explanatory, testable, revisable and immediately actionable. Diagnosis says *schizophrenia*; formulation says *why this man relapsed in March and what to change.*

## 1.4 Risk assessment

**The short version**
- Three domains: **risk to self** (suicide, self-harm, misadventure), **risk to others** (violence; risk to dependent children), **risk from self-neglect** (nutrition, hydration, untreated illness, exposure, exploitation).
- Factors split **static** (unmodifiable, set the baseline), **dynamic** (modifiable — the treatment targets) and **protective** (built upon).
- ⚑ **Risk-stratification tools do not predict individual suicide and must not allocate care.** RANZCP (2016, deliberate self-harm CPG) recommends verbatim: *"Do not use risk assessment scales or tools to determine the need for clinical services or follow-up in people treated in hospital after deliberate self-harm."* No empirical study has shown that low-/high-risk categorisation reduces overall rates of self-harm; "high risk" has poor positive predictive value and most suicides occur in the far larger "low-risk" group.
- ⚑ **Asking about suicide does not plant the idea** (Dazzi 2014; DeCou & Schumann 2018). Ask directly and unapologetically.
- Risk is **not a number** — it is a *narrative* linking the formulation to a specific, time-limited, contingency-based plan.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Static | Male sex, older age, past attempt (**strongest single predictor**), family history of suicide, chronic illness, childhood abuse, prior violence |
| Dynamic | Ideation/intent/plan, hopelessness, depression severity, psychosis (esp. command hallucinations), agitation/insomnia, intoxication, recent discharge, access to means |
| Protective | Dependent children, engaged supports, treatment alliance, help-seeking, religious/cultural prohibition, future orientation |

**Assessing suicidality.** Progress ideation → intent → plan → preparation → rehearsal → access to means, asking about reasons for living in the same breath. Record what you asked and what was answered, not just your conclusion.

**Features after an attempt that raise concern:**
- **Planning** — premeditated over days/weeks vs impulsive.
- **Lethality** — objective lethality *and* the patient's belief about it; belief matters more (a believed-lethal but benign overdose signals high intent).
- **Precautions against discovery** — acting alone, locked door, cancelled visitors.
- **Final acts** — will, giving away possessions, notes, settling debts, care of pets.
- **Regret at survival** — persisting wish to be dead, disappointment at being found. The corollary (relief, future orientation) is reassuring but not alone a discharge criterion.
- Also: no help-seeking afterwards, continuing access to means, ongoing intent.

**Risk to others** — nature, target, imminence and means of any threat; command hallucinations with intent to comply; persecutory delusions with an identified persecutor; morbid jealousy (**high risk of serious violence to the partner**); history of violence (the strongest predictor); intoxication; weapons. Consider duty to warn an identifiable third party — jurisdiction-specific.

**Risk to dependent children** — mandatory in every assessment of an adult with mental illness. Who is caring for them now? Are they safe, fed, at school? Do they see or hear the symptoms, or carry a caring role? Is a child incorporated into a parent's delusional system? Australian clinicians are **mandatory reporters** of reasonable suspicion of abuse or neglect; thresholds vary by jurisdiction.

**Absconding risk** — previous absconding, involuntary status, denial of illness or refusal of treatment, intent to leave, agitation, recent admission, withdrawal. The period after unauthorised leave, and the first weeks post-discharge, carry markedly elevated suicide risk.

⚑ **Governance.** The NSQHS Comprehensive Care Standard requires systems for *predicting, preventing and managing self-harm and suicide*, *aggression and violence*, *minimising restrictive practices* and *preventing delirium* — a system and a response, not a score.

## 1.5 Organic causes before psychiatric diagnosis

**The short version**
- Psychiatric diagnoses are made **after exclusion** — a new presentation with atypical features is organic until proven otherwise.
- Red flags: first presentation aged <14 or >40, abrupt onset, fluctuating course, **impaired consciousness or inattention**, focal neurology, seizures, catatonia, cognitive deficits, non-auditory hallucinations, abnormal vitals, no psychiatric history.
- **Delirium is the most important mimic** — see Geriatrics notes §1 for diagnosis, causes, 4AT/CAM and management; do not re-derive it here.
- Baseline bloods are cheap and justified at first presentation; **imaging is not a screening test**.

**The mimics:** delirium; thyroid disease (hypothyroidism → depression, cognitive slowing, psychosis; hyperthyroidism → anxiety, agitation, mania-like state); B12/folate deficiency (neuropsychiatric features can precede anaemia); substance intoxication and withdrawal; autoimmune encephalitis (esp. anti-NMDAR — young, often female, viral-like prodrome, psychosis with **catatonia, seizures, movement disorder, autonomic instability, cognitive decline**; ovarian teratoma); medication-induced (corticosteroids, dopamine agonists, isotretinoin, interferon, anticholinergics); intracranial pathology (frontal/temporal tumour, subdural haematoma, stroke, MS, epilepsy, neurosyphilis, HIV, NPH).

| Routine at first presentation | Rationale |
|---|---|
| FBC | Infection, anaemia, macrocytosis (alcohol, B12); baseline before clozapine/carbamazepine |
| UEC ± calcium | Renal function (lithium!), hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia → depression/confusion |
| LFT | Alcohol, hepatic dysfunction; baseline before hepatically metabolised agents |
| BSL or HbA1c, lipids | Dysglycaemia as a cause; cardiometabolic baseline (§1.7) |
| ECG (QTc) | Baseline before QT-prolonging psychotropics; arrhythmia, electrolytes |
| Urine drug screen | Shows recent use, not causation; misses many novel agents |
| β-hCG | Anyone who could be pregnant — changes every prescribing decision |

| Targeted, not blanket | When |
|---|---|
| TFT | ⚑ In **first-episode psychosis specifically**, the 2026 ANZ recommendations (Warren et al., ANZJP 2026) move TFT out of the universal baseline panel to **testing where history/examination raise concern** (prominent mood/anxiety, weight or heat/cold intolerance, goitre, tachycardia, sleep, menstrual or hair/skin change). This scoping does **not** extend to other presentations: **TSH stays in the routine baseline panel in depression (§2.2) and anxiety (§3.1)**, where hypothyroidism is the classic mimic. Keep a low threshold; **mandatory before and during lithium** |
| B12/folate | Older adults, malnutrition, alcohol, restrictive diet, macrocytosis, cognitive impairment |
| Vitamin D | ⚑ Not an organic screen, but part of Australian **cardiometabolic monitoring** on antipsychotics |
| CRP/ESR, ANA, encephalitis antibodies (serum ± CSF) | Atypical psychosis: catatonia, rapid onset, fluctuating course, seizures, movement disorder |
| Syphilis, HIV, hepatitis B/C | Sexually active with risk factors, injecting or methamphetamine use, cognitive decline |
| CT/MRI brain (MRI preferred) | **On indication only**: focal neurology, seizures, head injury, headache/vomiting, catatonia, cognitive deficits, onset <14 or >40, treatment resistance. Routine imaging in uncomplicated first-episode psychosis has low yield |
| EEG / LP | Seizure-like activity, suspected encephalitis or CNS infection, unexplained fluctuating conscious state |

⚑ QT: **QTc >500 ms**, or a rise **>60 ms** from baseline, is the usual threshold for concern and review of QT-prolonging agents; correct K⁺ and Mg²⁺ and check interacting drugs first. ⚑ Australian caveat: Australian Prescriber argues fixed QTc cut-offs are unreliable (500 ms is "problematic for patients with tachycardia", 440 ms "too sensitive") and prefers the **QT nomogram** (QT plotted against heart rate) for risk assessment.

## 1.6 Cultural and social dimensions

**The short version**
- **Idiom of distress** varies: distress is expressed somatically, spiritually or relationally in many cultures — not a lesser or "masked" disorder.
- A belief is only delusional if **out of keeping with the person's own cultural and religious background** — the reference group is the patient's community, not yours.
- For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, work within the **social and emotional wellbeing (SEWB)** framework.
- Migration, trauma and disaster shape what is presented and whether the person presents at all.

**Presentation.** Somatic presentation of depression and anxiety (pain, fatigue, dizziness, GI symptoms) is common globally; explanatory models (spirit involvement, sorcery, punishment) shape help-seeking and adherence. Ask: what do you think is causing this, what do you call it, what treatment do you expect? Stigma shapes disclosure, especially of suicidality and substance use.

**Interpreters.** Use a qualified interpreter whenever language proficiency is uncertain, not only when there is no English — psychiatric assessment demands nuance far above transactional English. Family members introduce distortion, censorship (psychotic content, sexual history, family violence, suicidality) and confidentiality breach; **children must never interpret**. Brief the interpreter first, speak to the patient in the second person, allow double the time, and check whether dialect, gender or community matching is needed. ⚑ **TIS National 131 450**; **Doctors Priority Line 1300 131 450**, free to eligible private practitioners.

**Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients.** SEWB is holistic and collectivist: wellbeing arises from **seven overlapping domains of connection — body; mind and emotions; family and kinship; community; culture; Country; spirituality and ancestors** — the self embedded in family and community. ⚑ SEWB problems and mental illness overlap but are not the same: strong SEWB can coexist with mental illness, and poor SEWB with no diagnosable disorder. The framework treats **social, historical and political determinants** — colonisation, dispossession, the Stolen Generations, intergenerational and racial trauma, incarceration, racism — as causal, not contextual. Practically: offer an Aboriginal Health Worker or SEWB worker; ask who should be involved in decisions, about connection to Country, family and community, and about grief and loss (sorry business, often cumulative); do not read culturally normative experiences (seeing a deceased relative, spiritual explanations) as psychosis; weigh distance from Country when considering admission.

**Migration, trauma and disaster.** Refugees and asylum seekers carry pre-migration trauma (war, torture, persecution) and post-migration stressors — visa insecurity, detention, family separation, unemployment, discrimination — the last often the strongest *current* driver and the most modifiable. Screen sensitively and only where there is capacity to respond; work trauma-informed (safety, choice, collaboration, trustworthiness, empowerment); avoid detailed re-narration at first assessment. After disaster most acute distress is normal and resolves — watch for the minority with persisting PTSD, depression, alcohol misuse and prolonged grief; effects peak well after the event.

## 1.7 Physical health of people with severe mental illness

**The short version**
- ⚑ People with severe mental illness die **roughly 10–20 years earlier** than the general population; the WA population analysis found **15.9 years for men, 12.0 for women**, and the gap **widened** to 2005.
- ⚑ **~80% of that excess mortality is preventable physical illness** — chiefly cardiovascular and metabolic — **not** suicide.
- Drivers: antipsychotic-induced weight gain, dyslipidaemia and dysglycaemia, very high smoking rates, inactivity, poor diet, plus **diagnostic overshadowing** and lower rates of screening, statin prescribing and revascularisation.
- Examinable as a *principle*, not a platitude: physical complaints in a patient with a psychiatric diagnosis get attributed to that diagnosis far too readily.

**Diagnostic overshadowing** is the mechanism to name: symptoms in a person with known mental illness are misattributed to that illness (chest pain called anxiety, delirium called relapse), delaying diagnosis. Australian general practice data show higher dyslipidaemia (25% vs 18%), hypertension (27% vs 22%) and obesity (29% vs 19%) in severe mental illness; under 50, coronary heart disease mortality risk is roughly threefold.

| Parameter | Schedule |
|---|---|
| Weight/BMI | Weekly for the first 6–8 weeks after starting/changing an antipsychotic, then 3, 6, 9, 12 months, then 6-monthly |
| Waist circumference | Weekly for the first 6–8 weeks after starting/changing an antipsychotic, then baseline, 3, 6, 9, 12 months, then 6-monthly |
| BP, fasting/random glucose or HbA1c, lipids | Baseline, **3, 6, 9, 12 months**, then 6-monthly |

Thresholds prompting action: weight gain **>5 kg (or waist +5 cm) in 3 months**, BMI >25 kg/m² (>23 in higher-risk populations), waist **>94 cm men / >80 cm women**, BP **>140 mmHg systolic and/or >90 mmHg diastolic**, **HbA1c ≥6.0% (42 mmol/mol)** or fasting glucose **≥5.6 mmol/L**, LDL ≥2.0, HDL <1.0, triglycerides ≥1.7 mmol/L. Respond by reviewing the antipsychotic (metabolic burden highest with olanzapine and clozapine, lower with aripiprazole and lurasidone), starting lifestyle intervention *before* the weight is gained, considering metformin, treating lipids and BP to the same standard as anyone else, and offering smoking cessation — it does not worsen psychiatric outcomes, but ⚑ **stopping smoking raises clozapine and olanzapine levels** via CYP1A2 de-induction and may need dose reduction.

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Digressive but eventually gets to the point | Circumstantiality (tangentiality never arrives) |
| Links between consecutive ideas absent or incomprehensible | Loosening of associations — schizophrenia (cf. flight of ideas: links audible, mania) |
| Sustained subjective state vs observed momentary expression | Mood vs affect (climate vs weather) |
| Misperception of a real stimulus vs percept with no stimulus | Illusion vs hallucination |
| Less than full conviction, conceded as possibly wrong | Overvalued idea, not a delusion |
| Familiar person replaced by an identical impostor | Capgras (cf. Fregoli) |
| Higher-status person secretly in love with the patient | de Clérambault; belief one is dead/has no organs = Cotard |
| Visual, olfactory or tactile hallucinations at first presentation | Organic or substance cause until excluded |
| Predisposing / precipitating / perpetuating / protective × biopsychosocial | The 4 P grid — formulation, not diagnosis |
| Strongest single predictor of completed suicide | Previous attempt |
| Does asking about suicide plant the idea? | No — meta-analyses show no increase in ideation |
| Stratifying "low/high risk" to decide follow-up | Not supported — RANZCP advises against risk scales allocating care |
| Life expectancy gap in severe mental illness | ~10–20 years (WA: 15.9 men, 12.0 women); ~80% preventable physical illness |
| Weight gain prompting action on an antipsychotic | >5 kg, or waist +5 cm, in 3 months |
| Body; mind and emotions; family and kinship; community; culture; Country; spirituality | The seven SEWB domains of connection |
| Catatonia, seizures, autonomic instability, rapid cognitive decline in a young woman | Anti-NMDAR encephalitis — seek ovarian teratoma |

# Psychiatry Notes — Section 2: Mood Disorders
*Sources: RANZCP mood disorders CPG (2015, updated 2020), Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, RACGP, TGA safety advisories (valproate; antidepressants in youth), COPE perinatal guideline, beyondblue/Black Dog, AIHW. Scope: unipolar depression, bipolar disorder, perinatal mood disorders, grief/adjustment and suicide risk — diagnosis, stratified management, and the drug-monitoring detail finals test.*

## 2.1 Major depressive disorder

**The short version**
- A **sustained** change from baseline, **≥2 weeks**, built on two core features — **persistent low mood** and **anhedonia** — plus cognitive, somatic and psychomotor features.
- **Functional impairment is part of the definition** — feeling flat *and* no longer working, parenting or self-caring.
- Severity = symptom count + functional impact + psychosis/suicidality, and **severity selects the treatment** (§2.3).
- **Ask about past mania/hypomania before treating** — changes diagnosis and drug (§2.5).
- Organic mimics (thyroid, anaemia, OSA, alcohol) are cheap to exclude and embarrassing to miss.

**The picture.** Low mood most of the day, nearly every day, characteristically **not reactive** to what normally lifts the person; with it anhedonia — previously rewarding activity now effortful and empty. Around these cluster fatigue out of proportion to activity; disturbed sleep (classically early-morning wakening, but initial insomnia and hypersomnia both occur); appetite/weight change either way; impaired concentration (often presenting as "memory problems"); psychomotor retardation or agitation **observable to others**; and the triad of **worthlessness, inappropriate guilt and hopelessness**, ending in thoughts of death or acts.

| Specifier | Picture | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Melancholic/somatic** | Profound anhedonia, **non-reactive mood**, **early-morning wakening**, **diurnal variation worse in the morning**, psychomotor change, weight loss, guilt | Most "biological" subtype; best evidence for antidepressants and **ECT**; SNRI/TCA may beat SSRI |
| **Atypical** | **Mood reactivity preserved**, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, longstanding rejection sensitivity | Often misread as personality; raises suspicion of **bipolar II** |
| **Psychotic** | Mood-congruent delusions — guilt, poverty, nihilism (Cotard), deserved punishment | Antidepressant **alone inadequate** → add antipsychotic, or **ECT**; high suicide risk; usually admit |
| **Seasonal** | Regular seasonal onset, remission out of season, often atypical | ⚑ Rare at Australian latitudes — don't over-diagnose |

**Severity drives management.** **Mild** — few symptoms beyond threshold, function largely maintained → **therapy first-line, antidepressant not routine**. **Moderate** — clear impairment → therapy first-line, antidepressant co-equal. **Severe** — marked functional loss, ± psychosis, ± suicidality, ± food refusal → **combined** drug + therapy, admission, antipsychotic if psychotic, **ECT if speed matters**. Exam line: severity decides whether medication is offered at all; psychosis, suicidality and food refusal decide whether treatment must be *fast*.

| Differential | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Bipolar depression** | Any past **mania/hypomania** — ask directly, take collateral. Earlier onset, more episodes, atypical/psychotic/postpartum features, prior antidepressant switch |
| **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)** | Chronic low-grade depression **≥2 years**, no symptom-free interval >2 months; "double depression" = MDE on top |
| **Adjustment disorder** | **Identifiable stressor**; onset **within 1 month (ICD-11) / within 3 months (DSM-5-TR)**, distress disproportionate but **sub-threshold** (§2.8) |
| **Grief** | Waves centred on the deceased, **self-esteem preserved**, pleasure between waves (§2.8) |
| **Hypothyroidism** | Cold intolerance, constipation, weight gain, bradycardia, delayed reflex relaxation → **TSH** |
| **Anaemia/iron deficiency** | Fatigue and poor concentration dominate over guilt/anhedonia → FBC, ferritin |
| **OSA** | Snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence — gets treated as "depression" and fails |
| **Substance-induced** | Alcohol (commonest mimic *and* perpetuator), benzodiazepines, cannabis, stimulant withdrawal, corticosteroids — temporal relationship to use |

## 2.2 Assessment and screening

**The short version**
- **PHQ-9** = depression-specific severity, **0–27**. **K10** = general distress, **10–50** (the instrument embedded in Medicare items and ABS surveys).
- **Two-question case-finding**: over the past month, low mood? and loss of interest/pleasure? Negative to both makes depression unlikely; either positive → full assessment.
- ⚑ **Scales grade severity and track change — they do not make the diagnosis.** Diagnosis is clinical.
- Baseline bloods exclude mimics and make prescribing safe — a short defensible list, not a shotgun.

| PHQ-9 | Band → action | K10 | Distress |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–4 | None/minimal → no treatment | 10–15 | Low |
| 5–9 | Mild → self-help, review | 16–21 | Moderate |
| 10–14 | Moderate → therapy ± antidepressant | 22–29 | High |
| 15–19 | Moderately severe → active treatment | 30–50 | Very high |
| 20–27 | Severe → combined treatment, refer | | |

**≥10 on PHQ-9** is the conventional threshold for probable major depression, and the suicidality item is followed up regardless of total. K10 is **not depression-specific** — a high score warrants assessment, not a diagnosis.

**Baseline workup.** **FBC** (anaemia, alcohol macrocytosis); **UEC** (renal baseline — essential before lithium; SSRI hyponatraemia); **LFTs**; **TSH** (the classic mimic); **BSL/HbA1c and lipids**; **B12/folate**; **iron studies**; **calcium** (mandatory if lithium planned); **βhCG** in anyone of childbearing potential; **ECG** if cardiac history, elderly, overdose risk or a QT-prolonging agent; **urine drug screen** if substances suspected.

⚑ **Not routine**: CT/MRI brain, EEG, autoimmune or infectious serology, "depression biomarkers", pharmacogenomic testing. Neuroimaging only for atypical features — first episode in the elderly, focal neurology, seizures, rapid cognitive decline or personality change.

## 2.3 Management of depression, stepped

**The short version**
- **Mild–moderate: psychological therapy first-line** (CBT, IPT, behavioural activation), plus exercise, sleep and alcohol reduction.
- **Severe: combined antidepressant + therapy**, together, not sequentially.
- **SSRI first-line** — between-class efficacy is comparable, so the differentiators are tolerability and **overdose safety**.
- **2–4 weeks at a therapeutic dose** before judging response; continue **≥6–12 months after remission** at the dose that achieved it.
- ⚑ **Taper on stopping** — discontinuation syndrome is neither relapse nor dependence.

**The ladder.** At every severity: psychoeducation, sleep, alcohol, exercise, structured activity, treat comorbidity, safety plan if any suicidality. **Mild** — review at 2 weeks, guided self-help or digital CBT (This Way Up, MoodGYM). **Moderate** — therapy first-line; antidepressant equally defensible with prior response or preference. **Severe** — antidepressant **plus** therapy, antipsychotic if psychotic, **ECT** if urgent. **Non-response at 4 weeks** — recheck adherence, dose and diagnosis (bipolarity, alcohol, thyroid), optimise dose; **switch** at 6–8 weeks, **augment** if partial.

**Why SSRI first.** SSRIs lack the anticholinergic, antihistaminergic and α-blocking load of TCAs and — decisively where suicide risk is elevated — are **far safer in overdose** than TCAs (cardiotoxic, proconvulsant) or MAOIs (tyramine, hypertensive crisis). Choice within the class is by side-effect profile.

| Agent | Start → usual range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sertraline** | 50 → 50–200 mg | Fewest interactions; safest in cardiac disease and pregnancy — the default |
| **Escitalopram** (citalopram 20 → 20–40) | 10 → 10–20 mg | ⚑ Dose-dependent **QT prolongation** — **maximum maintenance 10 mg/day if >65 y**; in hepatic impairment start 5 mg for 2 weeks, maximum 10 mg |
| **Fluoxetine** | 20 → 20–40 mg (**registered maximum 80 mg/day**) | Long half-life, self-tapering; **best evidence under 18**; potent CYP2D6 inhibitor |
| **Paroxetine** | 20 → 20–40 mg (**registered maximum 50 mg/day for depression; 40 mg usual, max 60 mg for OCD/panic**) | Most anticholinergic, **worst discontinuation**; avoid in pregnancy |
| **Venlafaxine** (SNRI); **mirtazapine** | 75 → 75–225 mg; 15 → 15–45 mg nocte | Second-line. Venlafaxine: **dose-dependent hypertension**. Mirtazapine: sedating, orexigenic, least sexual dysfunction |

**SSRI adverse effects** — nausea (early, settles), headache, **jitteriness/akathisia in the first 1–2 weeks**, sexual dysfunction (commonest cause of covert non-adherence), **hyponatraemia via SIADH** (elderly, women, diuretics — check Na⁺ at 2–4 weeks if unwell), **bleeding** (additive with NSAIDs/anticoagulants), **serotonin syndrome** with MAOIs, tramadol, triptans or St John's wort.

**Latency and duration.** Sleep, appetite and energy often improve within 1–2 weeks; mood and cognition lag, so judge response only after **2–4 weeks at a therapeutic dose**, remission typically ~6 weeks. Then continue at the **same dose ≥6–12 months** (RANZCP: ≥6 months, up to 12, longer if recurrent). Indefinite maintenance after ≥3 lifetime episodes, psychotic episodes, or residual symptoms.

**Stopping.** Taper over **≥4 weeks**, longer for paroxetine, venlafaxine or years of use (hyperbolic taper). **Discontinuation syndrome** starts within days of a cut: flu-like malaise, insomnia and vivid dreams, nausea, dizziness, sensory disturbance (classic **"electric shock" brain-zaps**), irritability. Versus relapse — it is **early (onset within a day or two of a cut), physical rather than mood-dominant, and resolves within hours of reinstatement**. ⚑ Do **not** use duration as the discriminator: withdrawal symptoms are mild and brief only after short-term (6–12 weeks) therapy; after longer treatment they "can sometimes be severe and protracted", lasting months. Calling a protracted withdrawal syndrome "relapse" is the classic error and leads to unnecessary reinstatement or dose escalation. Not dependence: no craving, no escalation.

⚑ **Two Australian specifics.**
- **Antidepressants and suicidality in the young.** TGA-mandated warnings state antidepressants may increase **suicidal thinking and behaviour under 25**, greatest in the **first few weeks** and after dose changes. The consequence is not "don't prescribe" — untreated depression is the larger risk — but: clear indication, **monitor closely for the emergence or worsening of suicidal thoughts during the first 2–4 weeks of treatment and after any dose change, and through the initial months** — the TGA sets no fixed review interval; arrange frequent review (in practice weekly to fortnightly early on) and warn patient and family what to watch for, safety plan, limit quantity dispensed. **Fluoxetine** is preferred under 18, with therapy and CAMHS involvement.
- **Better Access.** A GP-prepared **Mental Health Treatment Plan** gives **up to 10 Medicare-rebated individual psychological therapy sessions per calendar year** (plus up to 10 group), released in blocks with a GP review between; unused sessions do not carry over. ⚑ MBS items were revised from **1 November 2025**: the **Mental Health Treatment Plan keeps its name**, and preparation items (2715/2717) are unchanged, but the **MHTP review item (2712 and equivalents) and the GP mental health treatment consultation items (2713, 277, 279, 92114/92115, 92120/92121, 92126/92127, 92132/92133) were removed** — ⚑ **2712 was the *review of a MHTP* item and 2713 the ≥20-minute *ongoing mental health treatment consultation* item; don't swap them** — reviews and mental health consultations are now billed on **time-tiered general attendance items** (e.g. 23, 36). Plans, referrals and reviews must be linked to the patient's **MyMedicare-registered practice or usual medical practitioner**. The 10 individual + 10 group session cap was unchanged.

## 2.4 Treatment-resistant depression

**The short version**
- **TRD = failure of ≥2 antidepressants at adequate dose for ≥4–6 weeks each in the current episode.**
- Exclude **pseudo-resistance** first: wrong diagnosis (especially **undetected bipolarity**), inadequate dose/duration, non-adherence, untreated comorbidity (alcohol, OSA, thyroid, pain).
- Augment with **lithium**, an **atypical antipsychotic**, or **T3**; or switch class.
- **ECT is the most effective acute treatment for severe depression — not a last resort.**

**Augmentation.** **Lithium** — best-established, target **0.4–0.8 mmol/L** (the range Australian Prescriber gives for lithium in depression), best in recurrent depression with positive family history. **Atypical antipsychotic** — aripiprazole 2–5 mg or quetiapine; metabolic/EPSE burden. **T3** even when euthyroid. **Psychological therapy** if not yet adequately delivered — often the missing step, not a fallback. **rTMS** — MBS items **14216/14217** require: age **≥18**, a current major depressive episode, inadequate response despite adequate trials of antidepressants from **at least two different classes** (unless contraindicated), **psychological therapy already undertaken** unless inappropriate, and **no previous rTMS in any setting** (a once-per-lifetime course, plus one re-treatment course). **Esketamine** is specialist-initiated and not PBS-listed. **Refer** for diagnostic uncertainty, suspected bipolarity, psychosis, escalating risk, two failed trials, perinatal presentation, adolescents, or any consideration of lithium, ECT or rTMS.

**ECT.** A brief generalised seizure under GA with muscle relaxation, **2–3× weekly for 6–12 treatments**; unilateral (fewer cognitive effects) or bilateral (faster, more effective).

*Where ECT is **first**-line*: **psychotic depression** (remission ~90%+, far above pharmacotherapy alone); **catatonia** of any cause after a lorazepam trial; **severe suicidality** needing rapid response; **food and fluid refusal**, particularly **in the elderly**, where death is weeks away and drug latency is not survivable; severe melancholia; previous good response. Also refractory mania and severe depression in pregnancy.

*Speed*: response typically within **1–2 weeks** (often 3–6 treatments) versus 4–6 weeks for drugs — the whole rationale for early use. Relapse without continuation is high, so ECT is **always followed by maintenance pharmacotherapy**.

*Consent*: informed consent from a patient with **capacity**, assessed and documented for ECT specifically. Without capacity, ECT proceeds only under the **mental health legislation of the relevant state or territory** — ⚑ requirements differ across Australia but generally need authorisation by a **mental health tribunal/board** with independent psychiatric opinion, and several jurisdictions bar ECT in minors without it.

*Adverse effects*: those of a brief GA (the main mortality risk), post-ictal headache, myalgia, nausea, transient confusion. Cognitively — **anterograde amnesia** (difficulty forming new memories during the course, resolving over days–weeks) and **retrograde amnesia** (loss of memories from around the course; largely recovers but **some autobiographical loss may be permanent**), which must be consented for explicitly and is reduced by unilateral brief-pulse placement. ⚑ **No absolute contraindication** — raised ICP, recent MI/stroke and unstable aneurysm are relative.

## 2.5 Bipolar affective disorder

**The short version**
- **Bipolar I** = at least one manic episode (depression not required). **Bipolar II** = at least one hypomanic **and** one major depressive episode, never mania.
- Bipolar spends most of its course **depressed** — which is why it presents as "depression" and gets mistreated.
- **Rapid cycling** = ≥4 episodes in 12 months. **Mixed features** = both poles at once, highest suicide risk.
- ⚑ **The trap: antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar depression can precipitate a manic switch or rapid cycling.**

| Feature | Hypomania | Mania |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | ≥4 consecutive days | **≥1 week** (or any length if admitted) |
| **Psychosis** | **Never** — its presence makes it mania by definition | May be present (grandiose, persecutory) |
| **Function** | Change obvious to others, function preserved or enhanced | **Marked** impairment of work/social/family function |
| **Hospitalisation** | Not required | Often required |
| **Insight** | Often partial | Usually absent |

Shared content: elevated, expansive or **irritable** mood; increased goal-directed activity; **reduced need for sleep** (not insomnia — rested on 3 hours); pressured speech; flight of ideas; distractibility; grandiosity; disinhibition. **Reduced sleep need and increased goal-directed activity discriminate best**; euphoria alone does not.

**Mixed features** — mania with prominent depression, or depression with racing thoughts, agitation and distractibility. Dangerous because it pairs **hopelessness with energy and disinhibition** → highest suicide risk of any bipolar state, and antidepressants worsen it. **Rapid cycling** — associated with **antidepressant exposure**, hypothyroidism and substance use; predicts poorer lithium response.

⚑ **The examinable trap.** Known bipolar patient presents depressed; the tempting answer is "start an SSRI". **Antidepressant monotherapy risks switching into mania/hypomania and destabilising into rapid cycling.** Instead: (1) **optimise a mood stabiliser or atypical antipsychotic first** — quetiapine and lurasidone have the best evidence in bipolar depression, with lithium and lamotrigine (the latter for the depressive pole); (2) if an antidepressant is genuinely needed, use it **only under adequate mood-stabiliser cover**, prefer SSRI over SNRI/TCA (higher switch risk), for the shortest effective period, watching for emerging mania; (3) **never** with mixed features or rapid cycling; (4) screen every depressed patient for past hypomania **before** prescribing — failure to ask is the commonest way an iatrogenic switch is produced.

## 2.6 Management of bipolar disorder

**The short version**
- **Acute mania**: stop antidepressants, **atypical antipsychotic ± mood stabiliser**, benzodiazepine for sedation and sleep, admission (often involuntary) if severe.
- **Bipolar depression**: quetiapine or lurasidone; lithium or lamotrigine; antidepressants only under cover.
- **Maintenance**: lithium first-line and the **only agent with robust anti-suicide evidence**.
- Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index — level **12 hours post-dose**, toxicity above ~1.5 mmol/L.
- ⚑ **Valproate is effectively contraindicated in anyone of childbearing potential.**

**Acute mania.** Sedate, restore sleep (deprivation propagates mania), reduce stimulation, stop antidepressants and any stimulant, steroid or substance driving it. **Atypical antipsychotic** (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole) gives fastest behavioural control; add **lithium or valproate**, with **benzodiazepines** for agitation and sleep. **ECT** if refractory, or if exhaustion/dehydration makes it urgent. Severe mania usually needs inpatient, often **involuntary**, care given absent insight. **Bipolar depression** — quetiapine or lurasidone first-line, lithium (also reduces suicide risk), lamotrigine for the depressive pole; **not** antidepressant monotherapy. **Maintenance** — continue what achieved remission (lithium first-line for both poles), plus psychoeducation, regular sleep–wake routine, relapse-signature identification and adherence support.

**Lithium.** *Indications*: acute mania, maintenance prophylaxis of both poles, augmentation in TRD, **suicide risk reduction**.

⚑ *Therapeutic range — sources differ, a common exam trap.* Australian Prescriber gives **0.6–0.8 mmol/L maintenance** (0.6–1.0 acute mania); many hospital protocols quote **0.8–1.2 acute mania** and **0.4–1.0 maintenance**. Safe answer: **maintenance ~0.6–0.8, acute mania up to ~1.0–1.2, lower (0.4–0.6) in the elderly.**

*Level timing*: **exactly 12 hours after the last dose** (morning sample for nocte dosing), for once- and twice-daily regimens alike. Steady state at 5–7 days (7–10 if elderly or renally impaired) → check **5–7 days after starting and after each dose change**.

| Test | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Baseline | UEC/eGFR, TFT, **calcium**, FBC, BSL, lipids, ECG, **βhCG** |
| Lithium level | 5–7 days after start/change, then **3–6 monthly** once stable (Australian Prescriber sequences baseline → 7 d → 14 d → 28 d → 3, 6, 12 months → annually) |
| **Renal** (UEC, eGFR) | **3–6 monthly** |
| **Thyroid** (TSH) | **6-monthly** |
| **Calcium** | **6–12 monthly** |
| ECG, weight/metabolic | Annually |

*Adverse effects*: fine tremor, polyuria/polydipsia (**nephrogenic DI**), weight gain, acne/psoriasis, **hypothyroidism**, **hyperparathyroidism with hypercalcaemia**, long-term **chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy**.

*Toxicity* — usually above **~1.5 mmol/L**. Mild (1.5–2.0): coarsening tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, blurred vision. Moderate (2.0–2.5): **coarse tremor**, ataxia, dysarthria, hyperreflexia, myoclonus, confusion. Severe (>2.5): seizures, arrhythmias, renal failure, coma, death. *Precipitants* — **dehydration**, **vomiting/diarrhoea**, **NSAIDs**, **ACE inhibitors/ARBs**, **thiazides**, sodium restriction, AKI; lithium is handled like sodium by the proximal tubule, so anything causing sodium avidity increases reabsorption. *Management* — stop lithium, **IV normal saline**, levels 4–6 hourly with UEC and ECG, **haemodialysis** if >4 mmol/L (or >2.5 with significant symptoms or renal failure); levels **rebound after dialysis**. ⚑ **Activated charcoal does not bind lithium.**

*Pregnancy*: first-trimester exposure is associated with cardiac malformations including **Ebstein anomaly**. The classic "400-fold increase" is a gross overestimate. Cohort data give cardiac malformations in **~2.4% of lithium-exposed** vs **~1.2% of unexposed** live births (adjusted RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.02–2.68); meta-analysis gives cardiac malformation OR 1.86 (1.16–2.96) and any congenital anomaly OR 1.81 (1.35–2.41). **Dose-dependent**: adjusted RR versus *unexposed* is 1.11 at ≤600 mg/day, 1.60 at 601–900 mg/day and **3.22 (1.47–7.02) at >900 mg/day**. See §2.7.

**Sodium valproate.** Effective in acute mania, maintenance and rapid cycling. Adverse effects: weight gain, tremor, hair loss, sedation, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, PCOS.

⚑ **Pregnancy-prevention requirements (TGA).** The highest-risk psychotropic teratogen in common use: **major congenital malformations ~3.3% at <1100 mg/day, rising to ~30% at ≥1100 mg/day**, including neural tube defects, plus dose-related neurodevelopmental harm (intellectual disability OR ~2.4–4.5, autism OR ~1.7–4.4, ADHD OR ~1.4–1.8). Therefore **do not use valproate for bipolar disorder in anyone pregnant or of childbearing potential** unless nothing else works; if used, ensure **effective contraception is co-prescribed**, **documented counselling about teratogenic and neurodevelopmental risk**, a **prescriber experienced in managing epilepsy or bipolar disorder**, and pregnancy excluded before starting. ⚑ Australia has **no formal Pregnancy Prevention Programme** — the *annual risk acknowledgement form* signed by patient and specialist is a **UK MHRA / Irish HPRA** requirement, not a TGA one. ⚑ The TGA also advises **males** to use effective contraception during treatment and for **3 months after stopping**, over an unconfirmed neurodevelopmental signal.

**Lamotrigine.** Best for the **depressive pole** and prophylaxis; ineffective in acute mania. ⚑ **The rash rule**: **SJS/TEN and DRESS** risk is driven by starting dose and titration speed, so **25 mg daily for 2 weeks, then 50 mg daily for 2 weeks**, then stepwise to a usual 200 mg daily. ⚑ **Halve dose and titration rate with valproate** (which doubles levels — start 25 mg *alternate* days); **double it with carbamazepine**. **Any rash → stop immediately**, especially with mucosal involvement, purpura or fever. If stopped >~5 days, restart the full titration.

**Carbamazepine.** Second/third-line. A **potent CYP3A4 inducer that also induces its own metabolism (autoinduction)** — levels fall over the first weeks. Reduces efficacy of **hormonal contraception**, **warfarin/DOACs**, other anticonvulsants (**lowers lamotrigine**) and many antipsychotics. Also hyponatraemia, agranulocytosis/aplastic anaemia, hepatitis, and ⚑ **SJS/TEN strongly linked to HLA-B*15:02.** The Australian position (Epilepsy Society of Australia) is that testing **should be *considered*** before first prescribing carbamazepine **in patients of Han-Chinese ethnicity**. The broader list (South Asian Indian, Filipino, Malaysian, Thai) is the **US FDA** recommendation, not the Australian one. Teratogenic (NTDs).

## 2.7 Perinatal mental health

**The short version**
- **Baby blues** — ~50–80%, day **3–5**, gone by **day 10–14**, reassurance only.
- **Postnatal depression** — ~1 in 7 Australian mothers; a major depressive episode needing treatment.
- **Puerperal psychosis** — **1–2 per 1000**, first **2 weeks**, a **psychiatric emergency**.
- **EPDS ≥13** is the referral threshold; **any positive score on the self-harm item is actioned regardless of total**.
- **Valproate contraindicated**; **sertraline** the usual first choice in pregnancy and breastfeeding (practice convention — COPE recommends SSRIs as a class, naming no preferred agent).

| | Baby blues | Postnatal depression | Puerperal psychosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | ~50–80% | ~10–15% (≈1 in 7) | **1–2 per 1000** |
| **Onset** | Day **3–5** | Any time in the first 12 months, commonly 1–3 months; often starts antenatally | **First 2 weeks** |
| **Duration** | Self-limiting by **day 10–14** | Months if untreated | Days–weeks with treatment |
| **Features** | Tearfulness, lability, irritability, poor sleep — mood **reactive**, function preserved | Anhedonia, "bad mother" guilt, impaired bonding, anxiety about the infant, suicidal ideation | **Rapid onset, marked fluctuation**, confusion, delusions often about the infant, mania or severe depression |
| **Action** | Reassure, support, review | Assess and treat — therapy ± SSRI, review risk | **EMERGENCY** — same-day assessment, almost always admission |

**Puerperal psychosis.** Strongest risk factor is **pre-existing bipolar disorder** (postpartum psychotic episode risk **~30%**) or **previous puerperal psychosis** (**>50%**). Risk is to **both mother and infant** — infanticidal ideation may be concealed and delusionally framed, and maternal suicide is a leading cause of perinatal maternal death in Australia. Manage with **urgent admission, ideally to a mother–baby unit**, as acute affective psychosis — antipsychotic ± mood stabiliser, benzodiazepine, **ECT if severe, catatonic or refusing food/fluid**. Exclude organic causes (sepsis, eclampsia, thyroiditis) first. Prophylaxis at delivery in known bipolar reduces recurrence.

**EPDS.** A 10-item self-report screen (**0–30**), validated antenatally and postnatally, deliberately excluding somatic items pregnancy confounds. COPE guidance: screen **at least once, preferably twice, antenatally and again postnatally — ideally 6–12 weeks after birth**. **≤9** routine care; **10–12** monitor and **repeat in 2–4 weeks**; **≥13** flags possible depressive disorder — antenatally repeat in 2–4 weeks and refer if still ≥13, postnatally **arrange referral or ongoing care**. ⚑ **Any score >0 on the self-harm item** → assess the safety of the woman **and any children in her care** immediately, **irrespective of total**. ⚑ EPDS **screens, it does not diagnose**.

**Prescribing.** Governing principle: **untreated maternal illness is itself harmful** — poor antenatal care, substance use, preterm birth, impaired attachment, maternal suicide — so do not reflexively stop effective treatment. **Sertraline** is widely used first in pregnancy and breastfeeding (extensive data, low milk transfer) — but note this is **practice convention and AMH/product-level data, not a named guideline recommendation**: the COPE 2023 Australian guideline recommends **SSRIs as a class** as first-line and deliberately does **not** nominate a preferred agent; **avoid paroxetine**. SSRIs carry small risks of **persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn** and **neonatal adaptation syndrome** (jitteriness, feeding and respiratory difficulty, self-limiting) — do not stop abruptly before delivery. **Lithium**: continue in severe bipolar where relapse risk is high, since destabilisation usually outweighs the ~2.4% cardiac malformation risk (vs ~1.2% background) — lowest effective dose, **monitor levels more often** (clearance rises 24–36% in pregnancy then falls sharply post-delivery: hold or reduce around labour, recheck within 24 h), **fetal echocardiography at ~18–20 weeks**. **Valproate contraindicated**; **carbamazepine** avoided (NTDs); **lamotrigine** safer but levels **fall substantially in pregnancy** and rebound postpartum. Olanzapine and quetiapine have the most antipsychotic safety data. **ECT** is safe where drug exposure is the concern; therapy is first-line for mild–moderate perinatal depression. **Mother–baby unit admission** where severe illness would otherwise separate mother and infant; mandatory child-protection reporting if the infant is at risk.

## 2.8 Grief, loss and adjustment

**The short version**
- **Normal grief** is not a disorder, has no fixed timetable, and comes in **waves triggered by reminders**, with **self-esteem preserved**.
- **Prolonged grief disorder** — disabling grief persisting **beyond 6 months (ICD-11)** or **beyond 12 months in adults / 6 months in children and adolescents (DSM-5-TR)**, dominated by yearning and preoccupation.
- **Depression** after a loss is diagnosed on its own features — pervasive mood, global anhedonia, worthlessness beyond the loss.
- **Adjustment disorder** — a disproportionate but sub-threshold reaction to an identifiable stressor.
- ⚑ **Don't medicate normal grief.**

| | Normal grief | Prolonged grief disorder | Major depression |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | The deceased and the loss | The deceased — intense yearning, preoccupation, disbelief | **The self** — worthlessness, guilt, hopelessness |
| **Mood** | **Waves/pangs**, triggered; pleasure between them | Persistent yearning, numbness, difficulty re-engaging | **Pervasive**, unremitting; **global anhedonia** |
| **Self-esteem** | **Preserved** | Largely preserved | **Impaired** — self-loathing |
| **Death wishes** | "I'd rather be with them" — passive | Wish to join the deceased | Belief of deserving death; active plans; may be delusional |
| **Course/treatment** | Improves gradually; support and normalisation | **≥6 months (ICD-11) / ≥12 months in adults (DSM-5-TR)** disabling; grief-focused CBT | Persistent; treat as §2.3 |

**Adjustment disorder.** A maladaptive emotional or behavioural reaction to an **identifiable psychosocial stressor** (illness diagnosis, relationship breakdown, job loss), beginning **within 1 month of the stressor (ICD-11) / within 3 months (DSM-5-TR)**, with disproportionate distress or functional impairment, **not meeting criteria for another disorder**. A diagnosis of exclusion — check it is not an MDE, anxiety disorder or PTSD. Typically resolves within ~6 months of the stressor ending. Subtypes follow the predominant affect: depressed, anxious, mixed, or conduct disturbance.

**When to medicate.** For normal grief and most adjustment disorders, **no antidepressant** — they do not accelerate grief resolution and they medicalise a normal process. Offer psychoeducation, practical support, problem-solving, sleep hygiene, cultural and religious supports, and brief therapy where distress is significant; a few nights of a hypnotic is occasionally defensible. **Do** treat when a full **major depressive episode** supervenes — pervasive anhedonia, worthlessness, psychomotor retardation, psychosis or active suicidality after a loss is depression, not grief.

## 2.9 Suicide and self-harm in mood disorders

**The short version**
- **2024 (latest ABS release): 3,307 deaths by suicide in Australia (≈9/day), age-standardised rate 11.8 per 100,000, ~76% male** (2023: 3,214 deaths, 11.8 per 100,000, 75% male). Suicide remains **the leading cause of death in people aged 15–44**.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rates are over 2.5× non-Indigenous**, peaking at 25–44 years.
- **Previous attempt is the strongest single predictor**; mixed states carry the highest risk of any mood state.
- ⚑ **Risk-stratification scales do not predict individual suicide and must not allocate care** (RANZCP explicit) — use **collaborative safety planning**.
- **The post-discharge period is the highest-risk window** of the entire illness course.

**Risk factors.** *Historical* — **previous attempt (strongest)**, self-harm history, family history of suicide, childhood abuse. *Clinical* — mood disorder with **mixed states highest**, substance use (especially alcohol), command hallucinations, personality disorder, recent discharge or medication change. *Demographic* — male (higher completion; women higher attempt rates), men 40–59, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, LGBTIQ+, rural/remote, veterans, people in custody. *Psychological* — **hopelessness** (a better predictor than depression severity), impulsivity, perceived burdensomeness, agitation, severe insomnia. *Situational* — recent relationship, job, financial or legal loss; isolation; **access to means**. *Protective* — family connection, dependent children, engagement with care, reasons for living.

**Safety planning** — a brief **written plan made *with* the patient**, evidence-supported and superior to a "no-suicide contract" (no evidence, no place). Six components: personal **warning signs**; **internal coping strategies**; **distracting settings and people**; **people to contact**, named with numbers; **professionals and services** — GP, case manager, state mental health line, Lifeline 13 11 14, 13YARN, 000; and **making the environment safe**.

**Means restriction** is one of the few interventions with population-level evidence. Remove or secure firearms with family involvement; **limit quantities dispensed**, use staged supply or family-held medication; ⚑ prefer **SSRIs over TCAs** where overdose risk is real; secure ligature points. Structural measures — bridge barriers, pack-size limits — have reduced deaths.

**Follow-up after discharge.** ⚑ The **first week and first month after discharge from an inpatient unit or ED are the highest-risk period of the whole illness course**. So: arrange **contact within 7 days** (many services aim for 24–72 hours) as an actual appointment, not a letter; discharge summary to the GP **same day** naming follow-up, medications and warning signs; involve family/carers with consent; use **assertive follow-up** — non-attendance is a risk marker, not a discharge from care; add brief-contact interventions (caring letters, texts, calls), which cheaply reduce repetition. **Lithium** and **clozapine** are the two psychotropics with direct anti-suicide evidence.

# Psychiatry Notes — Section 3: Anxiety, Trauma-Related and Somatic Disorders
*Sources: RANZCP CPG for panic, social anxiety and GAD (Andrews et al., ANZJP 2018) — ⚑ **withdrawn from the RANZCP Clinical Guidelines Library in 2023 as beyond the NHMRC five-year lifespan and retained for reference only; no replacement RANZCP anxiety CPG has been issued**, so verify drug and dose claims against AMH/TG Psychotropic, Phoenix Australia/NHMRC PTSD guidelines (2020–21), Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, RACGP (drugs of dependence; medically unexplained illness), TGA scheduling advisories, ANZCA Faculty of Pain Medicine PS01(PM) (2025), Australian Prescriber. Scope: GAD, panic/agoraphobia, social anxiety and phobias, OCD-spectrum, ASD/PTSD/complex PTSD, their management, and somatic symptom, functional and chronic pain presentations.*

## 3.1 Generalised anxiety disorder

**The short version**
- **Free-floating, multi-domain worry** — health, money, work, family, the future — that shifts topic but never switches off.
- **≥6 months**, most days, with the worry experienced as **uncontrollable**.
- Obligatory somatic tail: **restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, disturbed sleep**.
- Discriminator from normal worry = **uncontrollability + disproportion + impairment**, not the presence of worry.
- **GAD-7: 0–21.** Bands **5 / 10 / 15** = mild / moderate / severe; **≥10** warrants further assessment.

**The picture.** Usually presents somatically — tension headache, neck/shoulder ache, IBS-type symptoms, palpitations, insomnia — not as "I'm anxious." Onset is gradual, often traceable to adolescence; the patient says they have "always been a worrier." Worry is **anticipatory**, generating reassurance-seeking, checking on family, over-preparation and avoidance of uncertainty. Sleep disturbance is characteristically **initial insomnia**, which discriminates from the early-morning wakening of melancholic depression.

**Normal vs pathological worry.** Normal worry is proportionate, circumscribed, problem-solving, and stops when the problem resolves. Pathological worry is **disproportionate to actual likelihood**, **mobile** (resolve one worry and it lands on the next), felt as **beyond voluntary control**, persistent for months, and **costs function**.

| Differential | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Panic disorder** | Discrete crescendo attacks; fear is of the attack itself, not of life events |
| **Social anxiety** | Worry confined to **scrutiny/evaluation**; absent when alone |
| **OCD** | Ego-dystonic, often senseless content with **compulsions**; GAD worries are plausible real-life problems |
| **Depression** | Anhedonia, guilt, retardation; comorbidity is the rule — establish which came first |
| **Organic/substance** | Hyperthyroidism (tremor, heat intolerance, AF → **TSH**); caffeine, stimulants, salbutamol; **withdrawal states** |

**Baseline workup** (not a shotgun): FBC, EUC, LFT, **TSH**, glucose/HbA1c, ECG if palpitations or a QT-liable drug is planned. Anything further should follow a positive finding, not the anxiety (§3.8).

## 3.2 Panic disorder and agoraphobia

**The short version**
- **Panic attack** = discrete surge of fear **peaking within minutes**. A **descriptor, not a diagnosis** — occurs across anxiety, mood, psychotic and substance disorders and in healthy people.
- **Panic disorder** = **recurrent unexpected** attacks **plus ≥1 month** of anticipatory fear or maladaptive avoidance.
- Cognitive model: bodily sensation → **catastrophic misinterpretation** → adrenergic surge → more sensation, a closed loop maintained by **safety behaviours**.
- **Agoraphobia** = fear of situations where **escape is difficult or help unavailable** — transport, open/enclosed spaces, queues, crowds, being outside home alone.
- Exclude the mimics with a **small targeted workup**, not repeated cardiology referral.

**The mechanism.** A benign internal cue (postural tachycardia, caffeine, exertion, heat) is read by a hypervigilant patient as **imminent catastrophe — heart attack, suffocation, going mad**. That interpretation triggers sympathetic discharge, amplifying the sensation and confirming the belief. Hyperventilation drops pCO₂ → dizziness, paraesthesiae, chest tightness, derealisation, all reading as "something is seriously wrong." **Safety behaviours** (sitting near the exit, gripping a trolley, carrying a benzodiazepine) prevent disconfirmation — the patient concludes the catastrophe was *averted*. Treatment is therefore **interoceptive exposure**: deliberately provoking the sensations (hyperventilation, spinning, breath-holding) **while dropping safety behaviours**, so the prediction is falsified.

| Mimic | Doubt panic if… | Targeted test |
|---|---|---|
| **Arrhythmia** (SVT, AF, WPW, long QT) | Abrupt start *and* stop, rate >150 or irregular, **syncope**, symptoms from sleep, family history of sudden death | **ECG**; Holter/event monitor |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | **Pleuritic** pain, hypoxia, unilateral leg swelling, immobility/surgery/OCP/pregnancy | Wells → D-dimer/CTPA |
| **Thyrotoxicosis** | Weight loss with preserved appetite, heat intolerance, tremor, goitre, tachycardia **between** attacks | **TSH** ± free T4 |
| **Phaeochromocytoma** | Triad of **episodic headache, sweating, palpitations** with **paroxysmal hypertension**; pallor not flushing; ⚑ rare — don't screen everyone | Plasma free **metanephrines** |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Tied to fasting/exertion, relieved by eating, on insulin/sulfonylurea | BSL **during** the episode (Whipple's triad) |
| **Substance / caffeine** | New stimulant, energy drinks, salbutamol, pseudoephedrine, cannabis, amphetamines; or **withdrawal** | Quantified history; UDS if indicated |
| **Asthma** | Wheeze, cough, nocturnal symptoms; dyspnoea **precedes** the fear | Spirometry with bronchodilator response |

**What is actually indicated.** For a young patient with recurrent unexpected attacks, normal examination and no red flags: **ECG, FBC, EUC, TSH, glucose** plus a caffeine/substance/medication history — that is the whole of it. ⚑ **Repeated normal investigation is itself a treatment failure**: it feeds the catastrophic belief and delays effective treatment. Escalate only for syncope, exertional onset, hypoxia, focal signs, abnormal ECG, first attack over 45, or attacks arising from sleep.

## 3.3 Social anxiety disorder and specific phobias

**The short version**
- **Social anxiety** — fear of **scrutiny and negative evaluation**; the feared outcome is **humiliation or visible signs of anxiety** (blushing, tremor, voice shake). **≥6 months**, avoidance or endurance under distress, functional cost.
- **Performance-only subtype**: fear restricted to **performing/speaking in public**, ordinary social interaction unimpaired.
- **Specific phobia** — fear cued by **one object/situation** (animal, blood-injection-injury, natural environment, situational) with near-immediate anxiety on exposure.
- **Blood-injection-injury phobia is the exception** — a **biphasic vasovagal response with syncope**, treated with **applied tension**, not relaxation.
- Exposure is the treatment; drugs have little role in specific phobia.

| Presentation | Fear is of… | Discriminator |
|---|---|---|
| **Social anxiety** | Being judged or seen to be anxious | Symptoms only where **evaluation is possible**; fine alone |
| **Panic + agoraphobia** | Having an attack and being unable to escape | Fear is of the **body**, not the audience |
| **Avoidant PD** | Same content, but **lifelong, pervasive, trait-level** | Dimensional overlap — AvPD is the severe end |
| **Autism spectrum** | Not fear — **difficulty reading/using social cues**; early onset, restricted interests | Social *skill* deficit vs social *fear* |
| **Paranoid ideation** | Being harmed or targeted, with **conviction** | Social anxiety retains insight |

⚑ Performance-only anxiety is where the beta blocker question lives: Australian Prescriber notes **little evidence for beta blockers in anxiety disorders including social anxiety**, and Australian guidelines do not endorse them for generalised social anxiety — **propranolol before a discrete performance** is at best adjunctive and off-label. Generalised social anxiety: **CBT with exposure and video feedback first-line; SSRI/SNRI if moderate–severe or CBT unavailable**. Specific phobia: **graded in-vivo exposure**, often effective in single extended sessions.

## 3.4 Obsessive-compulsive disorder

**The short version**
- **Obsessions** = recurrent, intrusive, **unwanted** thoughts, images or urges causing marked anxiety/disgust, which the person tries to suppress or neutralise.
- **Compulsions** = repetitive behaviours **or mental acts** felt driven to perform in response, aimed at reducing distress or preventing a feared event, but **not realistically connected** or clearly excessive.
- Time-consuming (working threshold **>1 h/day**) or markedly impairing.
- **Insight is a specifier, not a requirement** — good/fair, poor, or absent (delusional). Absent insight is still OCD if the obsession–compulsion structure holds.
- Discriminator from **OCPD** = **ego-dystonic vs ego-syntonic**.

**Symptom dimensions.** (1) **Contamination** → washing, avoidance; (2) **Harm/responsibility** → checking locks and stove, driving back to see if someone was hit, reassurance-seeking; (3) **Symmetry and "just right"** → ordering, counting, repeating; (4) **Taboo thoughts** (aggressive, sexual, blasphemous) → mental rituals, praying, trigger avoidance. ⚑ Taboo obsessions are routinely misread as risk — they are **ego-dystonic and repugnant**, provoke avoidance of the feared target, and carry **no intent**: the opposite of a genuine violent or paraphilic drive.

| | **OCD** | **OCPD** |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | **Ego-dystonic** — alien, resisted, distressing | **Ego-syntonic** — seen as correct and virtuous |
| Structure | Discrete obsessions with linked compulsions | Pervasive **trait**: perfectionism, orderliness, rigidity |
| Insight | Recognises excessiveness | Sees no problem; distress is in **those around them** |
| Response | ERP + high-dose SSRI | Psychotherapy; poor SSRI response |

**In brief.** **Body dysmorphic disorder** — preoccupation with a defect unobservable or slight to others, with mirror-checking, camouflaging and **cosmetic-procedure seeking**; high suicide risk; ⚑ **procedures do not help and typically worsen it** — CBT + SSRI. **Hoarding disorder** — difficulty discarding regardless of value, with congestion rendering living areas unusable; ⚑ **a diagnosis in its own right, not an OCD subtype**, with poorer treatment response and major fire/falls implications in the elderly.

## 3.5 Acute stress disorder and PTSD

**The short version**
- Trauma exposure = direct experience, witnessing, learning of violent/accidental trauma to a close other, or repeated **occupational** exposure to aversive detail. ⚑ **Media exposure does not count** unless work-related.
- Four clusters: **intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, altered arousal and reactivity**.
- **ASD = 3 days to 1 month post-trauma; PTSD = beyond 1 month** (delayed expression if full criteria first met ≥6 months out). ⚑ **Adjustment disorder** is the third member of this trio and the commonest exam distractor: emotional/behavioural symptoms **beginning within 3 months of an identifiable stressor** (which need not meet criterion A), out of proportion to the stressor, and **resolving within 6 months of the stressor or its consequences ending** — no criterion A trauma, no intrusion/avoidance cluster structure required.
- **Complex PTSD (ICD-11)** = full PTSD **plus disturbances in self-organisation**.
- ⚑ **Single-session psychological debriefing is not recommended and may increase PTSD risk.**

**The four clusters, in plain terms.** **Intrusion** — the trauma re-enters awareness uninvited: involuntary memories, nightmares, and **flashbacks** in which the person acts or feels as if it is happening now (dissociative re-experiencing, not vivid recall), with autonomic reactivity to reminders. **Avoidance** — steering away from internal reminders (memories, feelings) and external ones (people, places, activities); this cluster entrenches the disorder and shrinks the person's world. **Negative alterations in cognition and mood** — inability to recall key parts of the event (dissociative, not head injury or substance), distorted beliefs about self and world ("nowhere is safe"), distorted **self-blame**, persistent fear/anger/shame, anhedonia, detachment, inability to feel positive emotion. **Arousal and reactivity** — irritability and unprovoked anger, reckless behaviour, **hypervigilance**, exaggerated startle, poor concentration, disturbed sleep. Specify **dissociative subtype** if depersonalisation/derealisation is prominent.

**Complex PTSD** (ICD-11; Ch. 7 of the Australian guidelines): full PTSD **plus** the three **disturbances in self-organisation (DSO)** — **affect dysregulation**, **persistent negative self-concept** and **interpersonal disturbance** — with **at least one symptom from each DSO cluster**. Typically follows **prolonged, repeated, inescapable interpersonal trauma**: childhood abuse, family violence, trafficking, torture. ⚑ Commonly mislabelled **borderline personality disorder**; in CPTSD the negative self-concept is stable rather than unstable, frantic abandonment-avoidance is less prominent, and re-experiencing is required.

**What does and does not predict PTSD.** Meta-analyses (Brewin 2000; Ozer 2003) show **peritraumatic and post-trauma variables outperform pre-trauma demographics**. Strongest: **perceived life threat and peritraumatic distress/dissociation**; **interpersonal/intentional trauma** (assault, sexual violence, torture > accident/disaster); and above all **lack of social support afterwards** and **subsequent life stress**. Moderate: prior psychiatric history, previous trauma, childhood adversity, physical injury/TBI, acute stress symptoms in the first month. Weak/inconsistent: age, sex, education. ⚑ **Most trauma-exposed people do not develop PTSD** — spontaneous recovery is the norm, which is why universal early intervention fails.

⚑ **Psychological debriefing.** The Cochrane review of single-session individual debriefing (Rose 2002) found **no reduction in PTSD** and some trials showed **worse outcomes at follow-up**; the Australian guidelines make a **conditional recommendation against** it, advising **information, emotional support and practical assistance** instead. The plausible harm is re-exposure consolidating the trauma memory during a physiologically aroused window, plus medicalisation of normal recovery. Correct early response = **psychological first aid** — monitor wellbeing and stabilise if needed, self-care advice, encourage social support, encourage limiting substance use — and, per the Australian guideline's **stepped care model, regular screening for PTSD symptoms across the three months following exposure**, with early referral for trauma-focused psychological treatment if symptoms emerge or persist. ⚑ The "review at 4 weeks" figure is a NICE convention, not the Australian recommendation.

## 3.6 Management of anxiety and trauma disorders

**The short version**
- **CBT with exposure is first-line for essentially every anxiety disorder**, at least as effective as medication with more durable gains.
- PTSD: **trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy, prolonged exposure and EMDR all carry strong recommendations** in the Australian guideline.
- Drugs: **SSRIs first-line for all anxiety disorders; SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine) also first-line for many** — RANZCP ranks them first-line "to a lesser extent" and switches to an SNRI where an SSRI is ineffective or not tolerated; ⚑ **start at half the lowest tablet strength** in panic; onset **4–6 weeks**; continue **≥12 months**.
- **OCD needs higher doses and longer trials** — maximum tolerated dose for **≥12 weeks** before declaring failure.
- ⚑ **Benzodiazepines are not first-line**, are **ineffective in OCD and PTSD**, and are for short-term use only.

**Stepped care, Australian version.** (1) **Treat comorbidity** — depression, substance use, sleep disorder. (2) **Psychoeducation, sleep, exercise, alcohol and caffeine reduction**, plus low-intensity digital CBT (**THIS WAY UP, MindSpot**) — the right answer for mild disease and rural access. (3) **Structured CBT with exposure** under a Mental Health Treatment Plan (Better Access). (4) **Pharmacotherapy** if moderate–severe, comorbid depression, or therapy declined/unavailable/failed. (5) **Psychiatry referral** for treatment resistance, diagnostic doubt, high risk, complex trauma.

**Exposure is the active ingredient**: **in-vivo** for phobias and agoraphobia, **interoceptive** for panic, **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** for OCD, **imaginal/in-vivo trauma-focused** for PTSD, with cognitive restructuring throughout. ⚑ **Relaxation-only and supportive counselling are inferior** — a common distractor. Beta blockers: ⚑ little evidence in any anxiety disorder.

| Drug | Australian place | Practical points |
|---|---|---|
| **SSRI** (sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, fluvoxamine) | **First-line, all anxiety disorders**; sertraline/paroxetine/fluoxetine also support PTSD | Start **half the lowest tablet strength available** — the rule applies across the anxiety disorders (OCD excepted, where ~75% do *not* respond to minimum doses), and matters most in panic, where initial jitteriness is the commonest cause of abandonment |
| **SNRI** (venlafaxine, duloxetine) | Also **first-line for many anxiety disorders** (RANZCP: first-line "to a lesser extent" than SSRIs); switch to one where an SSRI is ineffective or not tolerated; **venlafaxine has PTSD evidence**; duloxetine if comorbid pain | Check BP at higher doses; discontinuation symptoms |
| **Pregabalin** | Effective in GAD; ⚑ **not PBS-subsidised for anxiety**; recognised drug of misuse | Sedation, weight gain |
| **Quetiapine** | Some GAD efficacy (off-label); ⚑ **insufficient evidence in the Australian PTSD guideline** — not a recommended PTSD adjunct | ⚑ Long-term use inadvisable — metabolic, tardive dyskinesia |
| **Prazosin** | ⚑ **Not recommended by the Australian PTSD guideline** — insufficient evidence; the definitive multisite RCT (Raskind, NEJM 2018, n=304, 26 weeks) found **no difference from placebo** for nightmares, sleep or PTSD severity. Off-label; sometimes still trialled for refractory nightmares | Off-label; first-dose postural hypotension |
| **Clomipramine** | OCD after ≥2 failed SSRIs (tolerability, not efficacy, is why it is second-line). Australian PI dose for OCD: build up stepwise by 25 mg every few days to **100–150 mg/day**, maintenance **50–100 mg/day**; elderly **50–75 mg/day**. ⚑ Doses above this (the 75–300 mg range quoted in Australian Prescriber) are **off-label**, and the PI warns the recommended dose should not be exceeded because seizure risk is dose-dependent | Anticholinergic; **dose-dependent seizure risk**; cardiotoxic in overdose — baseline ECG |
| **Benzodiazepines** | **Not first-line**; short-term/crisis or refractory only | See below |

**Timing rules.** Anxiety responds **more slowly than depression — 4–6 weeks**; treat **≥12 months** after response before a slow taper. **OCD is the outlier**: increase every ~2 weeks toward maximum tolerated dose; full effect may take **12 weeks**, and an adequate trial is **maximum dose for ≥12 weeks**; continue **≥12 months** — **>50% relapse within 12 weeks** of stopping. Doses used in OCD run high: fluoxetine **40–80 mg**, sertraline **50–200 mg**, paroxetine **20–60 mg**, fluvoxamine **50–300 mg** — all within the TGA-approved maxima for OCD. ⚑ **Two SSRIs are capped and cannot be pushed:** citalopram at **40 mg** (**20 mg** if >65 y, hepatic impairment or CYP2C19 poor metaboliser) and **escitalopram at 20 mg** (**10 mg** if >65 y) — both for **dose-dependent QT prolongation**. The 10–40 mg escitalopram range quoted in Australian Prescriber is **above the TGA-approved maximum and off-label**; if used it needs specialist involvement, baseline and on-treatment ECG, and electrolyte checks. When these two are inadequate the move is to **switch to a different, uncapped SSRI** (fluoxetine, sertraline, fluvoxamine, paroxetine) — not to exceed the cap.

⚑ **Why benzodiazepines are not first-line, and the harm they do.** They relieve arousal in minutes but **do not treat the disorder**: by aborting anxiety they act as a **safety behaviour**, blocking the extinction learning exposure depends on, and they **worsen CBT outcomes**. **No benefit in OCD or PTSD.** Harms: **tolerance within days to weeks**; **withdrawal symptoms after 4–6 weeks of use in ~15–30%** — the RACGP figure derives specifically from benzodiazepines **prescribed for insomnia**; with regular therapeutic-dose use for more than a few months the estimate rises to **30–45%**; withdrawal brings rebound anxiety, insomnia, tremor and seizures on abrupt cessation; **falls and hip fracture** in the elderly; **anterograde amnesia and cognitive slowing** with a debated dementia association; **driving impairment — 60–80% higher crash risk, around sevenfold with alcohol**; **paradoxical disinhibition**; and **respiratory depression and death with opioids or alcohol**.

**The Australian position.** RACGP: **lowest effective dose, shortest clinical timeframe**; continuation **beyond 4 weeks is uncommon** and requires explicit justification; if long-term use is unavoidable, use **intermittent dosing with regular attempts at reduction**. ⚑ **Alprazolam was rescheduled to Schedule 8 in 2014** after misuse, overdose deaths and paradoxical excitation. All S8s and many benzodiazepines are captured by **real-time prescription monitoring (SafeScript / national RTPM)** — check before prescribing. Deprescribe by **stepwise taper**. ⚑ **Routine conversion to diazepam is not recommended in Australia** — switching short- to long-acting has not been shown to improve cessation rates or reduce withdrawal severity, and it is specifically discouraged in older people; RACGP reserves it for patients with **problematic withdrawal symptoms on reduction**. Rate: no evidence-based optimum, individualise — a commonly cited approach is **~10–25% of the daily dose every 1–2 weeks** (RACGP: minimum 6 weeks, up to 6 months overall), with **slower final steps (~10–12.5%)** as the dose gets low.

## 3.7 Somatic symptom and related disorders

**The short version**
- The organising question is no longer "are the symptoms explained?" but **"are the thoughts, feelings and behaviours about the symptoms excessive and disabling?"**
- **Somatic symptom disorder** — distressing somatic symptoms (which **may be medically explained**) **plus** disproportionate health-related thoughts, anxiety and time/energy, usually **>6 months**.
- **Illness anxiety disorder** — preoccupation with *having or acquiring* illness with **minimal or no somatic symptoms**.
- **Functional neurological disorder** — neurological symptoms with **positive rule-in signs**.
- **Factitious disorder vs malingering** turns on the **presence of an obvious external incentive**, not on proving motive — both involve conscious falsification.

| Disorder | Production | External incentive | Core feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Somatic symptom disorder** | **Unconscious** | No | Excessive thought/anxiety/behaviour about real symptoms; distress genuine |
| **Illness anxiety disorder** | **Unconscious** (few/no symptoms) | No | Fear of *having* disease; reassurance does not hold |
| **Functional neurological disorder** | ⚑ **Not assessed** — DSM-5 deliberately **dropped** the DSM-IV requirement that symptoms be judged "not intentionally produced or feigned", because assessment of conscious intention is unreliable. FND is diagnosed on **positive rule-in signs**, not on inferring the patient's intent | No | Internal inconsistency, incompatible with recognised disease |
| **Factitious disorder** (imposed on self or on another) | **Conscious** falsification | **No obvious external reward** — ⚑ DSM-5 defines it by the *absence of an obvious external incentive*, and **removed** the DSM-IV requirement to establish that the motive is assuming the sick role; you do not have to prove motive to diagnose it | Deception, falsification of history/signs/investigations, self-induced illness or injury; often healthcare-connected |
| **Malingering** | **Conscious** | **Yes** — money, drugs, avoiding work/court | Not a psychiatric disorder; a description of behaviour |

⚑ Two traps: **SSD does not require the symptom to be unexplained** — a patient with genuine IHD can have SSD if the psychological response is disproportionate; and **la belle indifférence and secondary gain are unreliable** and must not be used to diagnose FND or imply malingering.

**FND is a rule-in diagnosis.** ⚑ Not diagnosis by exclusion — it rests on **positive signs of internal inconsistency** (symptom varies with attention/distraction) or **incompatibility** with neuroanatomy.

| Sign | Elicited by | Shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Hoover's sign** | Weak hip **extension** normalises when the patient flexes the *contralateral* hip against resistance | Voluntary weakness with intact automatic movement. **Highly specific, moderately sensitive** — pooled estimates across studies ~94% sensitivity / ~99% specificity; the only prospective study (McWhirter, Stone et al., 2011) gave **63% sensitivity (95% CI 24–91) and 100% specificity (95% CI 97–100)** but rested on only 8 patients with functional weakness, hence the very wide CI. Quote it as "highly specific, sensitivity uncertain", not as a hard number. |
| **Tremor entrainment/distraction** | Ask patient to copy a rhythmic tap in the unaffected limb | Functional tremor **entrains to** or drops out at the external rhythm |
| **Tubular (non-expanding) field** | Confrontation/perimetry at two distances | Field fails to widen with distance — geometrically impossible |
| **Give-way (collapsing) weakness** | Sustained resistance testing | Normal initial power then abrupt ratchety yielding |
| **Dissociative seizures** | Event semiology | >2 min, waxing–waning, **eyes closed with resistance**, side-to-side head movement, pelvic thrusting, no post-ictal confusion, **normal prolactin/lactate**, event recall |

Caveat: no sign is infallible, and **FND frequently coexists with neurological disease** — a positive Hoover's does not exclude MS or stroke.

## 3.8 Managing medically unexplained symptoms in practice

**The short version**
- **Take the symptoms seriously and say so** — validation precedes any psychological framing.
- **Investigate once, well, and early**; then stop. Each extra test buys false positives, cascades and reinforced illness belief.
- **Explain what it is, not what it isn't** — a positive, mechanism-based, non-blaming explanation.
- **One GP coordinates**, with scheduled time-based reviews replacing symptom-triggered attendances.
- The goal is **function and quality of life**, not symptom eradication.

**The consultation.** Take an unhurried history including the patient's **own explanatory model** ("what do you think is causing this? what worries you most?") — you cannot correct a belief you have not elicited. Examine properly: the examination is therapeutic as well as diagnostic, and it is where the **positive signs** are found. Screen for depression, anxiety, trauma and substance use, and ask about **function** — work, sleep, exercise, relationships — making that the outcome measure.

**Avoiding iatrogenic harm.** Repeated testing generates incidentalomas, radiation, procedural complications, cost, and above all **entrenchment** — each new test implies you still suspect serious disease. RACGP guidance: **negotiate a bounded investigation plan up front**, explain what will and will not be done and why, then **monitor for genuine change** rather than re-testing on distress. Avoid opioids, benzodiazepines and invasive procedures — while remembering these patients remain at risk of **under-treatment of real disease**.

**Explaining it without implying it is imaginary.** Name it, because an unnamed problem is an unmanaged one. Then: **the symptoms are real and genuinely disabling**; the problem is one of **function, not structure** — "the hardware is intact, the software is misfiring"; **show them the positive sign** ("your leg moved normally when you weren't focusing on it — the pathway works"), which convinces far more than a normal MRI; it is **common, understood and treatable**; and it is explicitly **not "all in your head", not malingering, and not a diagnosis of exclusion**. Acknowledge uncertainty and offer to revisit if the picture changes.

**The GP as single coordinating clinician.** Fewer clinicians means fewer contradictory explanations, duplicated tests and less doctor-shopping. Structure: one named GP; **scheduled appointments at fixed intervals independent of symptoms**; a written management plan; referral only with a clear question. Treatment is **rehabilitative and multidisciplinary** — CBT (best evidence across MUS), graded activity and pacing, **physiotherapy with a retraining rather than strengthening focus in FND**, and treatment of comorbid depression/anxiety.

## 3.9 Chronic pain and the psychological dimension

**The short version**
- Chronic pain = pain persisting or recurring **>3 months**; beyond that it behaves as **a disease in itself**, not a symptom of tissue damage.
- **Biopsychosocial model**: nociception is modulated by central sensitisation, mood, cognition, sleep, behaviour, work and compensation context — imaging correlates poorly with disability.
- **Catastrophising** (rumination, magnification, helplessness) and **fear-avoidance** are the strongest modifiable predictors of disability.
- First-line care is **active and multimodal**: education, graded exercise and pacing, CBT/ACT, sleep, treat depression.
- ⚑ **Opioids have no demonstrated long-term benefit in chronic non-cancer pain.**

**The fear-avoidance cycle.** Pain → catastrophic interpretation ("something is being damaged") → **kinesiophobia** → avoidance and guarding → deconditioning, sleep loss, withdrawal, loss of role → depression and further disability → lower pain threshold → more pain. The opposite path, **confrontation**, leads to graded resumption of activity and recovery. Hence the intervention is **graded exposure to movement with pacing**, not rest — and catastrophising predicts outcome better than imaging does.

⚑ **The Australian position on opioids in chronic non-cancer pain** (ANZCA Faculty of Pain Medicine PS01(PM), 2025 update): opioids are **not first-line**; **self-management and non-opioid options must be considered first**, with multimodal therapy the foundation. Any opioid use is a **time-limited trial of 6–8 weeks** against a defined **functional** goal, **limited to about 100 mg oral morphine equivalent daily**, reviewed with the **"5 As" — analgesia, activity, adverse effects, affect, aberrant behaviour** — documented at intervals of no more than a month before repeats, with a colleague involved in any decision to continue and **at least annual peer or specialist review**. Cease if functional goals are not met. Cautions: age >65 or <18, household members with substance use disorder or suicidality, respiratory/hepatic/renal disease, and **concurrent sedatives or alcohol** — FPM lists this as a **risk factor demanding heightened caution and documented risk assessment**, not a formal contraindication: "the combination of these, potentially with other sedatives and alcohol, is associated with further increased risk of apnoea and death." The 2020 TGA opioid reforms (smaller pack sizes, tightened indications, boxed warnings) and national RTPM have moved practice toward deprescribing and referral to **multidisciplinary pain programmes** rather than dose escalation.

# Psychiatry Notes — Section 4: Psychosis and Schizophrenia
*Sources: **ANZJP GRADE guidelines for the management of schizophrenia (Suetani S, Dark F, Every-Palmer S, Galletly C, … Siskind D; ANZJP 2026) — these supersede the 2016 RANZCP CPG; INTEGRATE international algorithmic guidelines (McCutcheon R, Pillinger T et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2025, RANZCP-hosted); Warren N et al., Initial physical health assessment for psychosis in Australia and New Zealand: 2026 recommendations (ANZJP 2026)**, Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, Australian clozapine monitoring requirements (TGA-approved manufacturer programs and state protocols), Australian Prescriber, RACGP, Orygen/headspace, Lancet Psychiatry clozapine consensus (2025). Scope: psychosis as a syndrome, schizophrenia and its differentials, neurobiology, first-episode workup, antipsychotics and their emergencies, clozapine, catatonia, psychosocial care and prognosis.*

## 4.1 What psychosis is

**The short version**
- Psychosis = **loss of contact with consensual reality** — a syndrome, not a diagnosis; every presentation needs a cause found (§4.4).
- Four domains: **positive** (added), **negative** (subtracted), **cognitive**, **disorganised**.
- **Negative and cognitive symptoms determine long-term function**; positive symptoms determine admission.
- Name the negative symptoms precisely — a standard written-paper mark.
- **Visual, olfactory or tactile hallucinations point organic** until proven otherwise.

**Positive.** *Delusions* — fixed false beliefs, held with conviction, not culturally shared, impervious to counter-evidence. Persecutory commonest; also referential, grandiose, nihilistic, somatic, and the schizophrenia-flavoured **passivity phenomena** (thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcast; made acts, feelings, impulses). *Hallucinations* — percept without stimulus; **auditory** dominates in primary psychotic illness (third-person commentary, voices conversing), formication is classic in stimulant use.

**Disorganised.** Formal thought disorder — derailment, tangentiality, circumstantiality, neologisms, thought block, word salad — plus disorganised behaviour and catatonia (§4.8).

**Negative — learn all five.** **Avolition** (loss of self-initiated goal-directed activity — not laziness); **alogia** (poverty of speech and of content of speech); **anhedonia** (more a deficit of *anticipatory* than consummatory pleasure); **asociality** (reduced interest in and initiation of contact, distinct from paranoid avoidance); **blunted/flat affect** (reduced facial, prosodic and gestural expression).

⚑ **Primary vs secondary negatives** is the clinically load-bearing split. Secondary negatives come from depression, drug-induced parkinsonism or sedation, untreated positive symptoms (withdrawing to escape voices), substances, or institutional understimulation — all treatable. Primary (deficit) negatives are intrinsic, persistent and respond poorly to antipsychotics. Hunt the secondary causes first.

**Cognitive.** Working memory, processing speed, attention, verbal learning, executive function and **social cognition** — present at first episode, largely stable rather than progressive.

**Why negatives drive function.** Positive symptoms fluctuate and respond to dopamine blockade; negative and cognitive symptoms are drug-refractory, persist between episodes, and predict employment and independent living. *Treat positives with drugs and negatives with everything else* (§4.9).

## 4.2 Schizophrenia

**The short version**
- Positive, negative, disorganised and cognitive features with **functional decline**; **≥6 months** of continuous disturbance including **≥1 month** of active-phase symptoms.
- Preceded by a **prodrome** of months–years: withdrawal, falling grades/work, odd beliefs, attenuated perceptual disturbance.
- Lifetime risk ~**0.3–0.7%**; incidence ~**15/100,000/year**; **men onset late teens–mid 20s, women mid 20s–early 30s with a second peri-menopausal peak**.
- Differentials separate on three questions: **duration**, **mood–psychosis relationship**, **function outside the delusion**.
- **SMR 2–3×**, life expectancy shortened ~**15–20 years** — mostly cardiovascular; ~**5%** die by suicide.

**The picture.** A young person, often male, drifts over a year or two — withdraws, drops out of study, sleeps by day, becomes preoccupied and irritable, "not themselves." Then frank psychosis: beliefs about being watched or targeted, voices commenting on actions, passivity experiences, speech hard to follow. The acute phase settles with treatment; what remains is the flattening — less speech, less drive, less contact, the degree never resumed. Course is **relapsing–remitting on accumulated functional loss**, with the first 2–5 years setting the trajectory — hence early intervention (§4.4).

⚑ Modern classifications abolished the old subtypes (paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic, simple); catatonia is now a *specifier* across diagnoses (§4.8) — don't use subtype labels as current. Male:female incidence ~1.4:1; women have later onset, more affective colouring, better short-term outcome. Australian SHIP data (2010): **1-month treated prevalence ~3.1/1000 and 12-month treated prevalence ~4.5/1000** adults aged 18–64 in contact with services; **only 21.5% currently employed**, 66.1% currently smoking, 54.8% with metabolic syndrome.

| Differential | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Schizoaffective disorder** | Mood episode present for **most of total illness duration**, *but* **≥2 weeks of psychosis with mood symptoms absent** — that mood-free window is the test |
| **Schizophreniform disorder** | Same picture, duration **1–6 months**; decline not required. ~1/3 recover, ~2/3 progress |
| **Brief psychotic disorder** | **≥1 day to <1 month**, then **full return to premorbid function**; often stressor-precipitated |
| **Delusional disorder** | **≥1 month** of delusions, **no prominent hallucinations, disorganisation or negatives**, **function outside the delusional theme preserved** |
| **Psychotic depression** | Psychosis **only within** depressive episodes, usually mood-congruent — guilt, poverty, nihilism (§2.1) |
| **Mania with psychosis** | Psychosis confined to mania; elevated/irritable mood, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech dominate |
| **Substance-induced** | Onset in intoxication/withdrawal, **resolves within ~1 month of abstinence** |

## 4.3 Aetiology and neurobiology

**The short version**
- **Dopamine hypothesis**: excess **mesolimbic** D2 transmission → positive symptoms; blockade of three other pathways generates the entire side-effect profile.
- Refined: presynaptic dopamine dysregulation in the **associative striatum** → **aberrant salience** — random stimuli feel urgently significant, and delusions are the explanation built around that.
- **Glutamate/NMDA hypofunction** explains what dopamine cannot — ketamine/PCP reproduce all domains, amphetamine only the positive.
- **Heritability ~80%**, highly polygenic; strongest signal the **complement C4/MHC** region (synaptic pruning).
- **Neurodevelopmental model**: early insult sets vulnerability; adolescent pruning and myelination unmask it.

| Pathway | Role | Effect of D2 blockade |
|---|---|---|
| **Mesolimbic** (VTA→n. accumbens) | Salience, reward | **Therapeutic** — reduces positive symptoms |
| **Mesocortical** (VTA→PFC) | Motivation, executive, affect | **Worsens negative/cognitive** symptoms — already hypoactive |
| **Nigrostriatal** (SN→dorsal striatum) | Motor control | **EPS** — dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, TD (§4.5) |
| **Tuberoinfundibular** (hypothalamus→pituitary) | Dopamine tonically **inhibits** prolactin | **Hyperprolactinaemia** |

Narrow window: ~**65–70% striatal D2 occupancy** for effect, **>80%** for EPS — escalating past response buys side effects, not efficacy.

**Glutamate.** NMDA antagonism on **cortical GABAergic interneurons** disinhibits glutamatergic output and secondarily dysregulates subcortical dopamine — placing glutamate upstream of dopamine and giving a mechanism for the drug-refractory domains.

**Genetics.** Population lifetime risk ~**0.7%** (conventionally rounded to 1% in the classic family-risk figures); one affected parent ~10%; both ~40%; **MZ twin ~50%** — the gap from 100% is the standing argument for environment. Hundreds of common variants of small effect plus rare high-impact CNVs — **22q11.2 deletion** confers up to ~25% risk; think of it with palatal, cardiac or learning problems.

**Environment.** **Cannabis** is the one most likely asked: dose-dependent. **Ever use raises risk ~40% (adjusted OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–1.65); the most frequent users roughly double it (OR 2.09, 1.54–2.84)** (Moore 2007). In EU-GEI, **daily use OR ~3.2** and **daily high-potency (THC ≥10%) use OR ~4.8** vs never-users (Di Forti 2019), and **adolescent exposure matters more than adult**. ⚑ Counsel as risk-modifying and modifiable, not deterministic. Also **obstetric complications** (maternal infection, famine, pre-eclampsia, hypoxia, low birth weight), **advanced paternal age**, **migration/minority status** (largest where own-group ethnic density is low — social defeat, not genetics), **urbanicity**, childhood adversity, methamphetamine, winter/spring birth.

**Neurodevelopmental model.** Genetic liability plus pre-/perinatal insult disturbs migration and connectivity; silent through childhood beyond subtle motor, cognitive and social markers; declares in late adolescence when **synaptic pruning and prefrontal myelination** remove the redundancy that had compensated. Structural correlates — enlarged ventricles, reduced temporal/prefrontal grey matter — are ⚑ **group-level, not diagnostic, and not a reason to image routinely**.

## 4.4 First-episode psychosis

**The short version**
- **Duration of untreated psychosis (DUP)** is one of few modifiable outcome predictors — longer DUP, poorer remission, function and relapse rate.
- Australia leads on early intervention: the **EPPIC model** (Orygen/McGorry), scaled as **headspace** and early psychosis services.
- **Every first episode gets an organic workup** — primary psychotic illness is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Atypical features force investigation**: onset <14 or >40, non-auditory hallucinations, fluctuating consciousness, seizures, focal neurology, autonomic instability, catatonia, poor response.
- Dose **low and slow** — neuroleptic-naive patients are far more EPS-sensitive.

**Why DUP matters.** Untreated psychosis costs study, work, relationships and housing, with self-harm and forensic contact accruing. Whether psychosis is itself neurotoxic is contested; that the consequences compound is not — hence rapid referral and services a frightened 19-year-old will actually attend. **headspace** — national youth centres (12–25), free/low-cost, no referral barrier, integrated GP/psychology/psychiatry/vocational care. **EPPIC-model early psychosis services** — 2–5 years of specialised care, case management, low-dose antipsychotic, CBTp, family work, **vocational/educational recovery a core outcome**; gains attenuate if discharged to standard care too early.

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Methamphetamine** | Paranoia, formication, hypertension, mydriasis, bruxism, dental disease; psychosis persisting >1 month after abstinence → reconsider diagnosis |
| **Cannabis / synthetic cannabinoids** | ⚑ Synthetics ("spice") cause severe agitation, seizures, hyperthermia and **do not show on standard urine drug screens** |
| **Delirium** | **Fluctuating attention and consciousness**, disorientation, reversed sleep–wake, visual hallucinations, acute onset |
| **Anti-NMDAR encephalitis** | Young woman, viral prodrome, rapid psychiatric onset then **seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability, reduced GCS**; **ovarian teratoma**. ⚑ Confirm on **CSF** antibodies — serum can be falsely negative |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Stereotyped brief episodes, olfactory/gustatory aura, déjà vu, automatisms, post-ictal confusion |
| **Wilson disease** | **Age <40**, tremor/dystonia/dysarthria, deranged LFTs, Kayser–Fleischer rings → **caeruloplasmin**, 24-h urinary copper |
| **Drugs** | **Corticosteroids** (dose-related), dopamine agonists, isotretinoin, interferon |
| **Infection / endocrine / metabolic** | **HIV, neurosyphilis** (risk history, cognitive decline); hyper- and hypothyroidism; **B12/folate** deficiency |
| **SLE / cerebral vasculitis** | Rash, arthralgia, serositis, cytopenias, renal disease → ANA, ENA, dsDNA |

**Investigations indicated (2026 ANZ recommendations).** Routine for all: **FBC, UEC, LFT, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipids, urine drug screen, ECG (baseline QTc), weight, waist and BP**. **Targeted, not routine: TSH** — thyroid function is no longer a universal baseline test in first-episode psychosis (§1.5); order it only where history or examination raises concern (**prominent mood or anxiety symptoms, unexplained weight change, heat or cold intolerance, goitre, tachycardia**, menstrual, sleep or hair/skin change) — **B12/folate** (guided by dietary/lifestyle assessment and the FBC), **CRP/ESR** (nonspecific and insensitive — order only if other features point that way), **βhCG**, and **HIV/syphilis serology** (offer opportunistically with consent to anyone sexually active, methamphetamine-using or injecting).

⚑ **Neuroimaging is not routine in every first episode** (many Australian services do one by local protocol). It *is* indicated with focal neurology, seizures, head injury, onset <14 or >40, delirium, rapid cognitive decline, catatonia or poor response — **MRI beats CT**. **EEG** if seizures, fluctuating consciousness or catatonia. **LP and autoimmune antibodies only when the picture points that way**, not as a screen. Microarray (22q11.2) if dysmorphism, congenital cardiac disease or intellectual disability.

## 4.5 Antipsychotics

**The short version**
- All effective agents are **D2 antagonists or partial agonists**; second-generation drugs add **5-HT2A antagonism** (aripiprazole/brexpiprazole/cariprazine are **D2 partial agonists**).
- **First vs second generation is a side-effect distinction, not an efficacy one** (clozapine excepted): FGAs → more EPS and prolactin; SGAs → more metabolic harm.
- **Start low, titrate slowly, one drug at a time, review at 4–6 weeks** before declaring failure.
- Choice is driven by **side-effect tolerability, comorbidity and preference**, not class; **offer LAIs early**, not as a punishment for non-adherence.

**Classes.** FGA high-potency (haloperidol, fluphenazine) — more EPS; low-potency (chlorpromazine) — more sedation, hypotension, anticholinergic. SGA — olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride, lurasidone, plus partial agonists.

| | **Acute dystonia** | **Akathisia** | **Parkinsonism** | **Tardive dyskinesia** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | **50% <48 h, 90% <5 days** | Days–weeks, usually **<4 weeks** of start/dose rise | Weeks to ~3 months | **Months–years** (>3 months exposure; >1 month if elderly) |
| **Risk** | Young men, naive, high-potency FGA | Any; commonly missed | Elderly, women, higher dose | Elderly, women, FGAs, diabetes, cumulative dose |
| **Features** | Sustained contraction — **torticollis, trismus, oculogyric crisis, opisthotonus**; **laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency** | **Subjective inner restlessness** + pacing, inability to sit; linked to **suicidality** | **Bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity, symmetrical tremor, masked facies, shuffling gait** (symmetry vs idiopathic PD) | **Choreoathetoid orofacial movements** — lip smacking, tongue protrusion; often persists after withdrawal |
| **Treatment** | **Benztropine 1–2 mg IM/IV** (repeat prn), or promethazine — relief in minutes; airway support; then reduce/switch | **Reduce dose or switch**; **propranolol** first-line adjunct; short-term benzodiazepine; **mirtazapine 7.5–15 mg nocte**. ⚑ **Anticholinergics do not help akathisia** | Reduce dose/switch to a lower-EPS agent; **amantadine** or anticholinergic if switching impossible | ⚑ **Stop the anticholinergic** (worsens TD); reduce slowly/switch — **clozapine** best evidence; **VMAT2 inhibitors** are specific: **deutetrabenazine (Austedo) is TGA-registered in Australia for tardive dyskinesia in adults and for Huntington chorea, but is not PBS-subsidised for TD; valbenazine is not registered in Australia.** Tetrabenazine is available. Abrupt withdrawal can unmask TD |

⚑ **No routine prophylactic anticholinergics** — reserve for high-risk cases and plan withdrawal.

**Other adverse effects.** **Hyperprolactinaemia** — worst with **risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride** and FGAs; **aripiprazole and quetiapine** spare it. Galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, gynaecomastia, sexual dysfunction, **reduced BMD**; manage by dose reduction, switch, or low-dose aripiprazole. **Metabolic syndrome/weight gain** — worst **clozapine and olanzapine**, least aripiprazole/ziprasidone/lurasidone/amisulpride; **fastest in the first 6–12 weeks** and in the naive, so intervene early (**metformin** attenuates it). Monitor to the Australian **Positive Cardiometabolic Health Resource** (Mindgardens/Equally Well 2023) — **weight and waist weekly for the first 6–8 weeks** after starting or changing an antipsychotic, and **all parameters (weight/waist, BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids) at baseline, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months, then 6-monthly**; see §1.7 for the full monitoring table. **QT prolongation** — haloperidol (esp. IV), droperidol, ziprasidone, pimozide, amisulpride; **QTc >500 ms** (or rise >60 ms) → stop/switch, correct K⁺/Mg²⁺. **Sedation** (clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, chlorpromazine) and **sexual dysfunction** are the under-asked drivers of covert non-adherence. **Anticholinergic burden** — dry mouth, blurred vision, retention, constipation, **delirium in the elderly**. **Postural hypotension** (α1 blockade) — clozapine, quetiapine, chlorpromazine; falls. Also lowered seizure threshold and ⚑ **increased mortality and stroke in elderly people with dementia — not first-line for BPSD**.

**Which agent for whom.** First episode, young, weight-conscious → the 2026 ANZ GRADE first-line agents, chosen for cardiometabolic profile: **aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine or lurasidone**; low-dose risperidone remains reasonable but carries more prolactin elevation and EPS. Agitation/insomnia → olanzapine or quetiapine. QT concern → avoid haloperidol, ziprasidone, amisulpride. Elderly → lowest dose, no anticholinergics. Affective comorbidity → quetiapine or lurasidone. Repeated relapse → **LAI**. **Two failed trials → clozapine** (§4.6).

**LAIs.** Risperidone (**2-weekly IM, or 4-weekly subcutaneous**), paliperidone palmitate (**loading doses day 1 and day 8, then** monthly, 3- and 6-monthly), aripiprazole (**monthly or 2-monthly**), olanzapine pamoate (**2- or 4-weekly**; ⚑ **post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome** can occur up to 3 hours after injection — post-dose observation is mandatory), flupentixol (2–4 weekly), zuclopenthixol decanoate (2–4 weekly), haloperidol decanoate (4-weekly). ⚑ RANZCP: discuss LAIs **early, including at first episode** — they cut relapse and rehospitalisation; establish oral tolerability first. ⚑ **Zuclopenthixol acetate (Acuphase)** is short-acting (2–3 days) for acute disturbance — *not* a maintenance depot; classic trap.

## 4.6 Clozapine

**The short version**
- The **only agent with proven superiority in treatment-resistant schizophrenia**; also reduces suicidality, TD and aggression.
- **TRS = failure of ≥2 antipsychotics**, adequate dose, **≥6 weeks each**, adherence confirmed.
- **Agranulocytosis** drives the mandatory FBC program; ⚑ **myocarditis in the first 4 weeks and constipation/ileus are what actually kill people**.
- Monitoring is a **condition of supply** — no blood result, no dispensing.
- **>48 h of missed doses → re-titrate from 12.5 mg; >28 days → treat as a new patient.**

**Indications.** TRS — inadequate response to **≥2 different antipsychotics**, each at **adequate dose** (≥600 mg chlorpromazine equivalent daily) for **≥6 weeks with adherence confirmed (>80%)**, plus at least moderate functional impairment. **Australian guidance does not require one of the two to be second-generation.** Also severe EPS/TD intolerance, **persistent suicidality**, and psychosis in Parkinson disease. ⚑ Chronically under- and late-prescribed; RANZCP warns against delay.

**Dosing.** Inpatient **12.5 mg day 1**, rising in 25–50 mg increments; community initiation slower (from 6.25 mg). Maintenance **200–600 mg/day**, maximum **900 mg/day**. Level ~350–600 ng/mL for response; **>1000 ng/mL** raises seizure risk. ⚑ **Smoking induces CYP1A2** — cessation (including forced abstinence on admission) can **raise levels sharply into toxicity**; restarting drops them. Fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin and systemic inflammation also raise levels.

**Haematological monitoring (Australia): FBC before starting, weekly for 18 weeks, then at least 4-weekly for the duration of treatment.**

| Light | WCC (×10⁹/L) | Neutrophils (×10⁹/L) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green** | ≥3.5 | **≥2.0** | Continue, routine monitoring |
| **Amber** | 3.0–<3.5 | **1.5–<2.0** | Continue but **≥twice-weekly FBC** until green |
| **Red** | <3.0 | **<1.5** | **Cease immediately**, daily FBC, haematology, watch for infection |

- **Agranulocytosis = ANC <0.5** — emergency: treat as febrile neutropenia (cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, consider G-CSF). Incidence ~0.8%, **highest in the first 18 weeks**.
- **Any fever or sore throat on clozapine → urgent FBC**, whenever the last one was.
- ⚑ A red result normally means **permanent cessation and "non-rechallengeable" registration**; rechallenge is exceptional and needs haematologist plus senior psychiatrist agreement. **Benign ethnic neutropenia** (African/Middle Eastern ancestry) is the main legitimate reason for individualised thresholds, agreed with haematology in advance.
- ⚑ **Practice is shifting**: the 2025 international Delphi consensus (*Lancet Psychiatry*) recommends weekly ×18 weeks, monthly to 2 years, then **discussing stopping routine ANC monitoring** if no abnormality; the EU has relaxed its schedule. **Australian requirements remain as tabulated** — answer with the current Australian protocol.

**Myocarditis.** ⚑ Peaks in the **first 3–4 weeks** (rare beyond 8); Australia reports higher rates than most countries and drove the standard protocol: **baseline then weekly troponin and CRP for 4 weeks** with ECG and clinical review, plus baseline echocardiogram. Suspect with **tachycardia out of proportion, fever, dyspnoea, chest pain, unexplained CRP rise**. Rising **troponin (>2× ULN) and/or CRP >100 mg/L** → **stop clozapine, echocardiogram, cardiology**. Later **cardiomyopathy** also occurs; rechallenge only with cardiologist input.

**Constipation — the genuine killer.** Affects up to ~60%, and clozapine-related **GI hypomotility carries higher mortality than agranulocytosis**; patients under-report because visceral sensation is blunted. **Ask every review**, prescribe aperients prophylactically (osmotic ± stimulant, not bulking agents alone), avoid stacking anticholinergics, and treat abdominal pain, distension, vomiting or absent bowel sounds as **ileus/toxic megacolon until excluded** — imaging and surgical review.

**Other.** **Seizures** — dose/level-dependent, ~3–5% cumulative at one year; consider valproate cover at higher doses (⚑ not in childbearing potential — §2.6). **Sialorrhoea** — **reported by ~30–80%** (one study 91%), nocturnal, aspiration risk; dose reduction, sublingual atropine 1% eye drops 1–2 drops nocte, or hyoscine. **Weight gain, dyslipidaemia, diabetes** — worst of any antipsychotic, including DKA without preceding weight gain. Also sedation, postural hypotension, tachycardia (benign only once myocarditis excluded), enuresis, and early fever (usually benign, but triggers FBC, troponin/CRP and NMS consideration).

**Missed doses.** ≤48 h — resume usual dose. **>48 h — no grace period: obtain psychiatric review and recommence at 12.5 mg once or twice daily, re-titrating** (⚑ tolerance to hypotension and sedation is lost), or risk severe hypotension, seizures and collapse. **A break of >72 h to 28 days additionally requires weekly FBC for 6 weeks. A break of >28 days means the patient is treated as new: full pre-treatment workup, re-registration, restart at 12.5 mg with the full 18 weeks of weekly monitoring.**

## 4.7 Psychiatric emergencies of antipsychotics

**The short version**
- **NMS**: rigidity + hyperthermia + autonomic instability + altered consciousness over **days**, **grossly raised CK**.
- **Serotonin syndrome**: **clonus and hyperreflexia** over **hours**, after a serotonergic drug added.
- **Malignant catatonia**: near-identical to NMS but preceded by **catatonic signs**; responds to **benzodiazepines and ECT**.
- **Acute dystonia is not NMS**: no fever, no altered consciousness, resolves in minutes with IM benztropine.

**NMS.** Idiosyncratic, not dose-related; risk rises with high-potency agents, rapid titration, parenteral dosing, dehydration, agitation, catatonia and prior NMS. Incidence **~0.01–0.03% with second-generation antipsychotics (~0.2% in the first-generation era)**, mortality now **~5–8%**. **Develops over 1–3 days**, usually within 2 weeks of starting or increasing a dopamine blocker (or after abrupt withdrawal of a dopamine *agonist*). **Lead-pipe rigidity** (uniform, not cogwheel), **hyperthermia — the diagnostic threshold is >38.0 °C on at least two occasions measured orally, and it is often far higher**, **autonomic instability**, **fluctuating consciousness**. Labs: **CK markedly raised** (often >1000, sometimes tens of thousands), leukocytosis, raised transaminases/LDH, metabolic acidosis, myoglobinuria.

**Management.** **Stop all antipsychotics** and other dopamine blockers (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine); **medical/ICU transfer**. Aggressive **IV fluids** against rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI, **active cooling**, electrolyte correction, **VTE prophylaxis**, serial renal function and CK. **Benzodiazepines** (lorazepam) first pharmacologically — treat agitation, rigidity and any coexisting catatonia. Severe/refractory: **dantrolene — IV bolus 1–2.5 mg/kg, then 1 mg/kg 6-hourly, maximum 10 mg/kg/day** — and/or **bromocriptine 2.5 mg 2–3 times daily, increased by 2.5 mg every 24 h to a maximum of 45 mg/day**, or amantadine — case-series evidence only, and dantrolene monotherapy is linked to worse outcomes. **ECT** if refractory or malignant catatonia cannot be excluded. Resolves over **1–2 weeks** (up to a month after a depot). **Rechallenge** ≥2 weeks after full resolution, **low-potency or second-generation agent**, low dose, monitoring CK and temperature.

| | **NMS** | **Serotonin syndrome** | **Malignant catatonia** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Dopamine **blocker** started/increased, or agonist withdrawn | Serotonergic drug added/increased (SSRI+MAOI, tramadol, linezolid) | Underlying psychiatric illness, often mood; follows simple catatonia |
| **Onset** | **Days (1–3)** | **Hours (<24 h)** | Days, on catatonic background |
| **Tone** | **Generalised lead-pipe rigidity** | Lower-limb predominant rigidity + **inducible/ocular clonus, tremor** | Rigidity, **waxy flexibility, posturing, negativism** |
| **Reflexes** | Normal or reduced | **Hyperreflexia** — best single discriminator | Variable |
| **Pupils / gut** | Normal; bowel sounds normal or reduced | **Mydriasis**; **hyperactive bowel sounds, diarrhoea** | Normal |
| **CK** | **Very high** | Normal or mildly raised | Raised |
| **Treatment** | Stop drug, cool, fluids, benzodiazepine ± dantrolene/bromocriptine; ECT if refractory | Stop drug, supportive, benzodiazepine; **cyproheptadine** if severe | **Lorazepam** then **ECT** — ⚑ antipsychotics may worsen it |

**Acute dystonia.** **Benztropine 1–2 mg IM/IV** (or promethazine), repeat once if needed — response within minutes is diagnostic. Secure the airway if laryngeal; then reduce dose or switch.

## 4.8 Catatonia

**The short version**
- A **psychomotor syndrome**, not a diagnosis — a specifier applied across mood, psychotic, medical and neurological conditions.
- ⚑ **More often affective than psychotic in origin** — bipolar disorder is the commonest single cause.
- Diagnosed on **≥3 characteristic signs**; **Bush–Francis scale** is the standard screen.
- **Lorazepam challenge** is diagnostic and therapeutic; **ECT** is definitive and first-line if malignant.
- Untreated it kills — dehydration, VTE, pressure injury, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis.

**Features.** *Reduced* — **stupor**, **mutism**, **negativism** (motiveless resistance to instruction or movement), **withdrawal** (food/fluid refusal), **staring**. *Abnormal* — **catalepsy** (posture held against gravity), **waxy flexibility**, posturing, mannerisms, stereotypies, **echolalia/echopraxia**, automatic obedience, ambitendency. *Excited catatonia* — purposeless hyperactivity. **Malignant (lethal) catatonia** adds **fever, autonomic instability and rigidity** — the NMS look-alike of §4.7.

**Assessment.** Bush–Francis screen; examine for waxy flexibility and negativism. FBC, UEC, CMP, **CK**, LFT, TSH, glucose, CRP, **ECG**, plus **CT/MRI and EEG** if the cause is unclear (EEG separates catatonia from non-convulsive status); consider **autoimmune encephalitis** (§4.4). **VTE prophylaxis, hydration, pressure-area and nutritional care from day one.**

**Lorazepam challenge.** **1–2 mg IV/IM/oral** (0.5–1 mg if elderly/frail), reassess at ~10 min (IV) or 30 min. **Improvement within minutes supports the diagnosis** and predicts response; then scheduled lorazepam to **6–16 mg/day**, tolerated without sedation in true catatonia. ⚑ Non-response does not exclude catatonia; ⚑ a challenge in a delirious patient may worsen confusion.

**ECT.** When benzodiazepines fail or only partly work, and **first-line without delay in malignant catatonia**, life-threatening food/fluid refusal or rapid deterioration; response ~80–90%. ⚑ **Antipsychotics can precipitate or worsen catatonia and raise NMS risk** — withhold or minimise while it is active.

## 4.9 Psychosocial management and prognosis

**The short version**
- Medication controls positive symptoms; **psychosocial intervention determines function** — offer both.
- Best evidence: **CBT for psychosis**, **family intervention**, and **Individual Placement and Support (IPS)** supported employment.
- **High expressed emotion** predicts relapse, and family intervention modifies it.
- Continue antipsychotic **≥1–2 years after a first episode**; ~**80% of first-episode patients who remit relapse within 5 years regardless of treatment, and discontinuing antipsychotics multiplies that risk about 5-fold.**
- **Cannabis cessation** is one of the few prognostic levers the patient directly controls.

**CBTp.** Normalises psychotic experience, examines the evidence for beliefs collaboratively rather than confronting them, builds coping strategies for voices, and targets the distress as much as the symptom. Recommended for **persistent positive symptoms despite adequate medication**. **Cognitive remediation** targets the cognitive domain, best paired with vocational rehabilitation.

**Family intervention and expressed emotion.** **EE** = household emotional climate on three dimensions — **criticism, hostility, emotional over-involvement**. High EE predicts relapse; the relationship is bidirectional and ⚑ **not a matter of blame**, usually reflecting exhausted carers reading negative symptoms as wilfulness. **Family intervention** (psychoeducation about negative symptoms, communication training, problem-solving, crisis planning; ≥10 sessions over ≥3 months) **reduces relapse and admission**. Address carer burden and respite.

**Supported employment.** **IPS** — rapid placement into open employment with on-the-job support — outperforms prevocational training and sheltered work; vocational recovery belongs in the plan from first episode.

**Other components.** Assertive community treatment, NDIS psychosocial supports, **housing first**, exercise and diet programs; smoking cessation (~60–70% smoke — varenicline/NRT effective, ⚑ cessation raises clozapine and olanzapine levels); **cardiometabolic care** via GP management plan with annual CV risk assessment; a relapse plan naming **early warning signs** (sleep loss, suspiciousness, withdrawal).

**Relapse prevention.** Continue **≥1–2 years after a first episode** (indefinitely after multiple episodes) at the **lowest effective dose**; taper slowly if stopping. Non-adherence is the commonest cause of relapse — ask non-judgementally about **sexual dysfunction, weight gain, akathisia and sedation**, use LAIs early, involve family.

**Prognosis.** ~20% good outcome, ~50% fluctuating with residual symptoms, ~20–30% severe persistent illness. **Suicide ~5%**, highest early in illness in young men with preserved insight; **cardiovascular disease drives the 15–20-year mortality gap**.

| Better prognosis | Worse prognosis |
|---|---|
| Good premorbid function, later onset | Insidious onset in early adolescence |
| **Acute onset** with clear precipitant | Long prodrome and **long DUP** |
| Prominent **affective** symptoms | Prominent **negative and cognitive** symptoms |
| Female sex, in a relationship | Male sex, socially isolated, high EE |
| **No substance use** | **Ongoing cannabis or methamphetamine use** |

**Cannabis cessation.** Continued use after a first episode predicts relapse, admission and worse function; **cessation improves outcome in proportion to the reduction**. Motivational interviewing framed around the person's own goals (staying out of hospital, keeping study or work), addressing the sleep and anxiety being self-medicated — a conversation across years, not one warning.

# Psychiatry Notes — Section 5: Substance Use and Addiction Medicine
*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines: Addiction Medicine and Psychotropic; NHMRC Australian guidelines to reduce health risks from drinking alcohol (2020); Australian guidelines for the treatment of alcohol problems (2021); National guidelines for medication-assisted treatment of opioid dependence; AMH; RACGP; TGA; NSW/SA/Qld Health drug and alcohol guidelines. Scope: addiction medicine as a written-paper domain — concepts and motivational interviewing, screening, alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants, cannabis and nicotine, dual diagnosis and harm reduction.*

## 5.1 Concepts, language and behaviour change

**The short version**
- Spectrum: **use → hazardous/risky use → substance use disorder**; only the last is a diagnosis, and the middle band is where brief intervention pays.
- **Physiological dependence ≠ addiction.** Tolerance + withdrawal occur in patients taking opioids or benzodiazepines as prescribed; disorder needs **impaired control, salience, use despite harm**.
- ⚑ **"Abuse/dependence" language is obsolete** — DSM-5 collapsed them into one graded disorder and "abuse" predicts worse engagement. Say *use disorder*, *person who uses drugs*, *return to use*.
- **Motivational interviewing**: collaborative, evocative, autonomy-respecting — you resolve ambivalence, you don't supply motivation.

**Building blocks.** **Tolerance** — falling effect at constant dose; **not uniform across effects** (opioid analgesic tolerance outstrips respiratory tolerance — why escalation kills), and **lost after 3–5 days abstinence**: release from custody or rehab is the classic overdose-death window. **Withdrawal** — the pharmacological *opposite* of the acute effect. **Craving** — intrusive, cue-triggered. **Salience** — the substance outranks work, relationships and health; this, not volume consumed, is the clinical core. Diagnosis (criteria not reproduced) clusters **impaired control**, **social impairment**, **risky use** and **pharmacological features**; ICD-11 uses *hazardous use*, *harmful pattern*, *dependence*.

**Stages of change (Prochaska & DiClemente)** — pre-contemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance, **relapse part of the cycle, not failure**. **Match the intervention to the stage** — a detox referral to a pre-contemplative patient just generates resistance.

**Motivational interviewing — the technique.** Spirit: partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation. Microskills = **OARS**: **Open questions** ("what worries you about your drinking?" not "do you drink too much?"); **Affirmations** of genuine effort; **Reflective listening** — the workhorse, reflecting the feeling or the unspoken half of the ambivalence; **Summarising** — collect change talk and hand it back. Then **develop discrepancy** between behaviour and the patient's *own* values; **roll with resistance** — argue, and the patient voices the counter-arguments and talks themselves out of change; **support self-efficacy** via past successes. Commonest error is the **righting reflex**.

## 5.2 Substance history and screening

**The short version**
- **Quantity, frequency, route, time of last use, pattern**, plus withdrawal and overdose history. **Route matters**: IV → BBV, endocarditis, abscess; smoked → faster onset and higher addictive liability.
- ⚑ Australian **standard drink = 10 g ethanol** (UK unit 8 g, US 14 g — don't transpose).
- ⚑ **NHMRC 2020: ≤10 standard drinks/week AND ≤4 on any one day**, sex-neutral — replacing the 2009 "2/day, 4/occasion".

Per substance: age of first use, quantity/frequency, route, last use, escalation, features of a use disorder, **withdrawal history — ever had seizures or DTs?** (the single most predictive question), prior treatment and outcome, overdose and injecting practice, BBV status, and legal/driving/occupational/child-safety consequences. Corroborate with collateral, stigmata and targeted bloods: FBC (**macrocytosis without anaemia**), LFTs (**GGT rise, AST:ALT >2:1**), UEC, CDT, BBV serology, βhCG. Patients under-report: **750 mL wine ≈ 7–8 standard drinks**, 700 mL spirits ≈ 22.

| NHMRC 2020 | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **1. Healthy adults** | ≤**10 standard drinks/week** and ≤**4 on any one day** |
| **2. Under 18** | **No alcohol** |
| **3a. Pregnant / planning pregnancy** | **No alcohol** — no known safe level |
| **3b. Breastfeeding** | Not drinking is safest for the baby |

| Tool | Measures | Range | Bands |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AUDIT-C** | Consumption only | 0–12 | ≥**4 men**, ≥**3 women** → do the full AUDIT. ⚑ Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adaptation: ≥5 at-risk, ≥6 higher-risk, ≥9 dependence |
| **AUDIT** | Consumption + dependence features + harm | 0–40 | **0–7** low risk → education; **8–15** hazardous → brief advice; **16–19** harmful → brief counselling + monitoring; **≥20** → refer for assessment and treatment |

⚑ **Sources disagree**: auditscreen.org collapses these to 8–14 hazardous/harmful and **≥15** likely dependence; Australian primary care uses the four-zone WHO version. Common to both: **≥8** screen-positive, **≥20** always refer. Others: **ASSIST**, **SDS**, **CAGE** (superseded), **Fagerström**, **CIWA-Ar**, **COWS**. Bands only — item text is copyrighted.

## 5.3 Alcohol: complications and withdrawal

**The short version**
- Withdrawal onset **6–24 h**, peak **24–72 h**, settles day **5–7**; seizures **6–48 h**; **DT 48–72 h** (to 96 h), mortality ~**1–4% treated**, historically **15–20%+ untreated**.
- **Benzodiazepines are the treatment** — symptom-triggered or loading; **oxazepam/lorazepam in liver failure or the frail elderly**.
- **Thiamine before any glucose.**

**Complications by system.** **Neuro** — Wernicke–Korsakoff, cerebellar degeneration, peripheral neuropathy, alcohol-related brain injury. **Hepatic** — steatosis → alcoholic hepatitis → cirrhosis → varices, ascites, SBP, encephalopathy, **HCC**. **GI** — gastritis, PUD, **Mallory–Weiss**, **acute and chronic pancreatitis**. **Cardiac** — **dilated cardiomyopathy**, hypertension, **holiday-heart AF**; ⚑ light drinking is *not* cardioprotective. **Haem** — macrocytosis, thrombocytopenia, folate deficiency. **Metabolic** — ketoacidosis, hypoglycaemia, low Mg/K/PO₄. **Oncological** — Group 1 carcinogen: oropharynx, oesophagus, liver, colorectal, **breast**. **Obstetric** — **FASD**. Plus trauma and suicide.

| Phase | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated** | Onset **6–24 h**, peak **24–72 h**, gone day 5–7 | Tremor, sweats, anxiety, agitation, nausea, insomnia, tachycardia, hypertension |
| **Alcoholic hallucinosis** | **12–48 h** | **Hallucinations (classically auditory, also visual/tactile) with intact orientation and an otherwise clear sensorium** — this is a *complicated* withdrawal feature, not part of uncomplicated withdrawal, and mandates escalation of benzodiazepine dosing |
| **Seizures** | **6–48 h** (peak ~24 h) | Generalised tonic-clonic, single or short cluster. **Focal, status, or >48 h → image the head** (subdural, sepsis, hypoglycaemia) |
| **Delirium tremens** | **48–72 h** (to 96 h), lasts 3–5 days | **Clouded consciousness and disorientation** — what separates it from hallucinosis — fluctuating, coarse tremor, **vivid visual/tactile hallucinations**, fever, tachycardia, drenching sweats |

**Risk for severe withdrawal/DT**: prior DT or withdrawal seizures (strongest), high intake and long duration, in withdrawal despite a high blood alcohol, older age, intercurrent infection, concurrent benzodiazepine dependence. **CIWA-Ar** (0–67) has **ten items in total**, covering autonomic and psychological symptoms, perceptual disturbance in three modalities, and orientation/clouding of sensorium.

| Scale | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CIWA-Ar** (0–67) | **<10** | **10–20** | **>20** |
| **AWS** (Australian scale) | **<4** | **4–14** | **>14** |
| Monitoring | 4–6 hourly | 2–4 hourly | Hourly |
| Diazepam | **0–5 mg** | **10 mg** (or 10–20 mg then 10 mg hourly) | **20 mg** — loading protocol, medical review >80 mg |

⚑ The two scales are **not interchangeable and must not be cross-mapped** — Australian units use both. The "≥8 or ≥10" figure often quoted is the CIWA-Ar threshold for giving *any* medication, not the threshold for *severe* withdrawal.

**Symptom-triggered** dosing gives less total benzodiazepine and shorter treatment than fixed-dose, and is the inpatient default. ⚑ Use a **fixed-dose reducing regimen** instead when: ambulatory setting; **history of withdrawal seizures** (seizures can precede symptom escalation, so waiting on the score is unsafe); comorbidity invalidating the scale; or untrained staff.

**Diazepam loading** — for severe withdrawal, seizures, delirium or prior severe withdrawal: **10–20 mg orally every 1–2 h (e.g. 10 mg hourly) until 60–80 mg given or lightly sedated**; long-acting active metabolites then self-taper over days. **Review if still agitated after 80 mg or 4 h; >120 mg only after specialist review.** Refractory DT → ICU, parenteral benzodiazepine ± phenobarbitone.

⚑ **Change agent when hepatic metabolism is impaired.** Diazepam is hepatically oxidised and accumulates in **cirrhosis** and the **frail elderly** — use **oxazepam** (15–30 mg) or **lorazepam**, glucuronidated with no active metabolites (**LOT**). Also rehydrate, replace **Mg, K, PO₄**, hunt occult infection. Antipsychotics are **adjuncts only** — they lower seizure threshold and never replace benzodiazepines.

**Thiamine.** ⚑ **Give thiamine before any glucose load** — carbohydrate consumes residual thiamine and can precipitate Wernicke. Never withhold glucose from a hypoglycaemic patient: give thiamine first or together.

| Indication | Regimen |
|---|---|
| Prophylaxis, **well-nourished** | **300 mg/day orally** (100 mg tds) 3–5 days, then **100 mg/day** for 4–9 days |
| Prophylaxis, **malnourished / chronic heavy use** | **300 mg/day IM or IV** 3–5 days, then **300 mg/day orally** for several weeks |
| **Wernicke encephalopathy** (suspected or established) | **≥500 mg/day parenterally** (IV in saline over 30 min, or IM) **for ≥3–5 days**, then ≥300 mg/day (up to 1000 mg) for 1–2 weeks. Avoid IM if coagulopathic |

**Wernicke encephalopathy** — acute, reversible, clinical diagnosis. Triad: **confusion, ophthalmoplegia (nystagmus, gaze palsy), ataxia**. ⚑ **Full triad in only ~10–20%** — most present with confusion alone and most cases are missed antemortem, so **treat empirically on suspicion**. MRI (mamillary bodies, periaqueductal grey, medial thalami) supports but never excludes. **Korsakoff syndrome** is the chronic, largely irreversible sequel: **dense anterograde amnesia** with preserved immediate recall, procedural memory and consciousness, plus **confabulation**; only ~20% recover substantially. **Wernicke is a treatable emergency; Korsakoff is what it becomes if you miss it.**

## 5.4 Alcohol relapse prevention

**The short version**
- **Naltrexone** (opioid antagonist) for **cutting down**; **acamprosate** (glutamate/NMDA modulator) for **maintained abstinence**, safe in liver disease **but contraindicated in significant renal impairment (avoid if CrCl <30 mL/min)**; **disulfiram** (ALDH inhibitor) aversive, needs **supervised dosing**.
- None is stand-alone; all sit on psychosocial treatment. **Brief intervention works for hazardous, non-dependent drinking** — much weaker evidence in dependence.

| Drug | Mechanism / dose | Suits | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Naltrexone** | μ-antagonist → blunts alcohol reward and craving; cuts heavy-drinking days. **25 mg daily for 3–5 days, then 50 mg daily**; can start while still drinking | Reduction goal, strong craving | **Any opioid use, incl. OAT or analgesia** — precipitates withdrawal, blocks analgesia; acute hepatitis/liver failure |
| **Acamprosate** | Modulates the glutamatergic hyperexcitability of protracted withdrawal. 666 mg tds (less if <60 kg); start post-detox, ≥6–12 months | **Abstinence** goal; **liver disease** | **Severe renal impairment**; tds burden hurts adherence |
| **Disulfiram** | Blocks ALDH → acetaldehyde accumulates → flushing, headache, vomiting, tachycardia, hypotension on drinking. Start **24–48 h after the last drink**; **needs supervised ingestion**; not PBS-listed | Highly motivated, abstinence goal, supervisor available | **IHD, severe hepatic disease, psychosis, cognitive impairment, pregnancy** |

⚑ Naltrexone and acamprosate are **PBS-listed** within a comprehensive treatment program, no fixed duration; disulfiram is not. Off-label with real evidence: **baclofen** (advanced liver disease), **topiramate**. Where safe, defer committing to an antidepressant until 2–4 weeks of abstinence — comorbid mood symptoms often lift.

**Brief intervention — FRAMES**: **F**eedback, **R**esponsibility (the choice is theirs), **A**dvice, **M**enu of options, **E**mpathy, **S**elf-efficacy. **5–15 minutes in primary care** gives a small but reliable reduction in hazardous and harmful drinkers (NNT ≈ 8–12), sustained 6–12 months. ⚑ It does **not** transfer to **dependent** drinkers, adolescents or ED-only contacts.

## 5.5 Opioids

**The short version**
- Toxidrome: **pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, reduced consciousness** — respiratory rate is the vital sign that kills.
- **Naloxone: titrate.** Non-dependent **100 µg IV** increments; community **400 µg IM** or **1.8 mg intranasal**. Target respiration, **not full arousal**.
- **Naloxone lasts ~30–90 min; methadone >24 h** — re-sedation is the classic trap.
- Opioid withdrawal is **miserable but rarely lethal**, unlike alcohol or benzodiazepines. **OAT roughly halves mortality** — the single most effective intervention.

**Overdose.** Miosis, bradypnoea → apnoea, sedation, hypotension, bradycardia, hypothermia, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema. Pupils may not be pinpoint with **pethidine or tramadol**, in mixed overdose, or after hypoxic injury. Look for track marks and **fentanyl/buprenorphine patches — remove the patch**. Complications: **rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome**, aspiration.

**Naloxone.** Competitive μ-antagonist, onset 1–2 min IV. **Non-dependent**: 100 µg IV titrated every 30–60 s. **Suspected dependence**: only for genuine respiratory/CNS depression (RR <8, GCS <12), **low and slow** — a 2 mg bolus causes violent precipitated withdrawal (vomiting and aspiration, agitation, self-discharge and re-use). **Community**: 400 µg IM/SC or **1.8 mg intranasal (Nyxoid)**, repeated every 2–3 min. ⚑ **Duration mismatch**: re-sedation within 2 h is common after methadone or controlled-release opioids — observe for **at least 2 h after the last naloxone dose in short-acting opioid overdose, and admit for prolonged observation (a day or more, not hours) after methadone or controlled-release opioids**, since methadone's half-life is 20–36 h and it accumulates in tissue; infuse at **two-thirds of the dose needed to achieve reversal in the first hour, per hour**. ⚑ **Take-home naloxone** is **free and prescription-free** nationally, for anyone who may witness an overdose.

**Withdrawal.** Short-acting opioids: onset **6–24 h**, peak **24–48 h**, resolving over **5–10 days**. **Methadone**: onset **24–48 h**, peak day 3–6, dragging **2–3 weeks+**. Mirror image of intoxication — **mydriasis, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, yawning, piloerection, myalgia, cramps, vomiting, diarrhoea, insomnia, craving**; graded by **COWS**. ⚑ **Not usually life-threatening** — the danger is **loss of tolerance then relapse at the old dose**. Symptomatic: clonidine, antiemetics, loperamide, NSAIDs. **Detox alone has >80–90% relapse and, by stripping tolerance, raises overdose death risk.**

| | **Methadone** | **Buprenorphine ± naloxone** |
|---|---|---|
| Pharmacology | Full μ-agonist, t½ ~24 h+, **no ceiling** | **Partial** μ-agonist, high affinity, **respiratory ceiling** |
| Induction | **20–30 mg day 1**, slow increases — deaths cluster in the **first 2 weeks**, most on **day 3–4**, as tissue levels accumulate (steady state after 4–7 days); most induction deaths occur at doses of only **40–60 mg/day** | Only once objective withdrawal is established — **COWS ≥13** (crossing from mild into moderate; bands **5–12 mild, 13–24 moderate, 25–36 moderately severe, >36 severe**), usually **>8–12 h after the last short-acting opioid**. Day 1: **8 mg**, or **4 mg then a further 4 mg after review**; titrate to **12–24 mg** over 2–3 days |
| Maintenance | **60–120 mg** daily (max 150 mg) | usual **12–24 mg** daily; **32 mg** is the maximum daily dose |
| Key risk | **Induction overdose**, QT/torsades at high dose, CYP3A4 interactions. Better retention at adequate dose; suits high tolerance | **Precipitated withdrawal** — displaces the full agonist but only partially replaces it. Safer in overdose; naloxone deters injection; easier take-aways |

⚑ **Depot buprenorphine** has reshaped Australian practice: **Buvidal** (weekly 8–32 mg; monthly 64–160 mg) and **Sublocade** (monthly 100/300 mg), removing daily dosing, diversion and supervision burden.

⚑ **Missed doses strip tolerance** — after several consecutive missed doses the dose must be **re-assessed and re-inducted, never resumed at the previous level**. Methadone: 1–3 consecutive missed doses → resume the usual dose only if not intoxicated or withdrawing; **4 missed** → restart at **half the usual dose or 40 mg, whichever is lower**, re-titrating over 5–7 days in ≤10 mg steps every second day; **5–6 missed** → treat as a new induction (**20–30 mg, never >40 mg**); **≥7 missed** → do not dose without prescriber review. Buprenorphine: 1–3 missed → resume; **4–28 missed** → re-induct on withdrawal severity (4 mg then 4 mg if mild; 8 mg or half the previous dose, whichever lower, if moderate–severe), capped at 16 mg on day 2 and 24 mg on day 3.

**Take-aways and regulation.** Start with **supervised daily dosing**; **take-aways** are granted incrementally for demonstrated stability — stable dose, expected urine screens, reliable attendance, safe storage, no injecting. Typical ceilings are **2–4 methadone take-aways per week** for stable, low-risk patients; buprenorphine–naloxone is granted more liberally (up to 4 take-aways, and stable patients may be dispensed 1–4 weeks unsupervised). ⚑ Regulation is **state/territory based**: an **authority/permit per patient**, prescriber approval with caps for non-accredited prescribers, **single prescriber, single pharmacy**. ⚑ Since **July 2023** OAT sits under **s100 HSD (Community Access)** — patients pay only the PBS co-payment, removing the daily dispensing fee.

## 5.6 Benzodiazepines

**The short version**
- Tolerance to hypnotic and anxiolytic effects develops within weeks — how "just a few weeks" becomes years. Withdrawal mirrors alcohol and **can cause seizures and delirium — it can kill**.
- **Taper, never stop abruptly**; convert to **diazepam** where helpful.
- Reduce ~**10–25% of the current dose every 1–2 weeks**, slower at the end; **minimum 6 weeks**, often months.
- Onset **1–2 days** after short-acting agents, **3–7 days** after long-acting.

Prescribed dependence dominates — started for insomnia or anxiety, continued by inertia. Risk rises with **duration >4 weeks**, higher dose, short-acting high-potency agents (⚑ **alprazolam rescheduled to S8 in 2014**) and any prior substance use disorder. Harms: falls and fractures, cognitive impairment, crashes, **respiratory depression and death with opioids or alcohol**, paradoxical disinhibition, and worsening of the treated anxiety via inter-dose rebound.

**Withdrawal**: anxiety, insomnia, irritability, tremor, sweating, palpitations, nausea, muscle pain, and characteristically **perceptual hypersensitivity** — photophobia, hyperacusis, metallic taste, paraesthesiae, depersonalisation. Severe → **seizures, psychosis, delirium**. A protracted syndrome can persist months and must be pre-warned about, or it is read as relapse.

**Tapering.** Confirm the real dose (⚑ **SafeScript/DORA**). Treat the **indication** in parallel — CBT-I for insomnia, CBT/SSRI for anxiety; RACGP cites CBT raising one-year cessation from **15% to 45%**. Taper the current agent or **switch to diazepam** (long half-life, small increments, smoother levels). Reduce ~**10–25% of the current dose every 1–2 weeks**, holding at plateaus, slowing over the final quarter; minimum **6 weeks**, **up to 6 months or longer** for high-dose long-term users. Staged supply, frequent short reviews, no PRN. Specialist withdrawal if very high dose, prior withdrawal seizures or concurrent alcohol dependence. **Do not add a second sedative to cover the taper.**

## 5.7 Methamphetamine and stimulants

**The short version**
- Sympathomimetic toxidrome: **agitation, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, diaphoresis** — hot, **wet**, dilated, agitated. **Hyperthermia kills**, plus arrhythmia, ICH, seizures, rhabdomyolysis.
- **Oral benzodiazepines are first-line** for agitation, hypertension, tachycardia and seizures (⚑ *parenteral* benzodiazepines are **not** recommended for adult acute behavioural disturbance — see below). **Stimulant psychosis**: paranoid, prominent **tactile hallucinations**, resolving in days–weeks with abstinence.
- Withdrawal: a **crash**, then 7–10 days of hypersomnia, hyperphagia, dysphoria and craving — a **suicide-risk window**.

**Intoxication.** Euphoria, hyperalertness, grandiosity, pressured speech, insomnia, anorexia, bruxism, stereotypy, plus the autonomic picture. Distinguish from **anticholinergic** toxidrome (dry, flushed, absent bowel sounds, retention — sympathomimetics are **wet**), **serotonin syndrome** (clonus, lower-limb-predominant hyperreflexia), thyroid storm and NMS. Investigate: **ECG**, **CK**, UEC, sodium, glucose, **temperature**, and **CT brain if headache, focal signs or seizure** (methamphetamine causes both ischaemic stroke and ICH).

**Acute behavioural disturbance.** **De-escalation and environment first** — quiet space, one communicator, remove the audience. Then oral **diazepam 10 mg or olanzapine 5–10 mg**; if that fails, **droperidol 10 mg IM** (the Australian ED workhorse, repeatable once after 15 min; monitor QT and dystonia), or **olanzapine 5–10 mg IM** (**10 mg** is the single dose recommended in the Australian PI). ⚑ **Sequencing rule (ZYPREXA IM PI):** do **not** give a parenteral benzodiazepine until **at least 1 h *after*** IM olanzapine — excessive sedation, cardiorespiratory depression and, rarely, death. The rule runs **one way**: if a parenteral benzodiazepine has already been given, IM olanzapine may follow, but only after careful evaluation of clinical status and with close monitoring. ⚑ **Parenteral benzodiazepines, alone or added to droperidol, are not recommended in adults** — they increase adverse events and the need for further sedation. This is the **general adult ABD** position (Qld Health, *Management of patients with acute severe behavioural disturbance*), not a methamphetamine-specific one; see §7.6. Failed droperidol sedation → **ketamine 4–6 mg/kg IM** under senior supervision. ⚑ Protocols vary by jurisdiction. **Cool aggressively** if >39 °C — antipyretics don't work. Benzodiazepines before antihypertensives; ⚑ avoid **unopposed beta-blockade in cocaine toxicity**.

| | Stimulant psychosis | Schizophrenia |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Abrupt, locked to a binge | Insidious, with a prodrome |
| Hallucinations | **Tactile (formication — bugs under the skin) and visual** | **Auditory** third-person / running commentary |
| Delusions / negative symptoms | Persecutory, hyperaroused; negative symptoms uncommon | Bizarre, passivity phenomena; negative symptoms characteristic |
| Course and treatment | Resolves in **days to ~1 month** with abstinence; benzodiazepines + short-course antipsychotic, then reassess | **Persists** beyond a month; sustained antipsychotic |

⚑ You cannot call it at presentation — **the diagnosis is retrospective, made by what abstinence does**; persistence beyond a month, or recurrence with declining inter-episode function, points to a primary psychotic disorder. **Other complications**: "meth mouth", malnutrition, skin excoriation, cardiomyopathy and accelerated coronary disease, BBV and chemsex-related transmission, cognitive impairment, violence.

**Withdrawal.** **Crash** (24 h – 3 days): exhaustion, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, dysphoria. Then **up to 7–10 days**: labile mood, irritability, vivid dreams, **strong craving**, often marked depression; a protracted phase of anhedonia and episodic craving runs weeks–months. ⚑ **No effective pharmacotherapy exists** — supportive care plus **contingency management and CBT**. **Screen for suicidality.**

## 5.8 Cannabis, nicotine and other drugs

**The short version**
- Cannabis: **dose-dependent psychosis risk**, greatest with high-THC product, daily use and adolescent onset. **Cannabinoid hyperemesis**: cyclical vomiting relieved by **hot showers** in a chronic daily user.
- Cessation: **combination NRT ≈ varenicline > single NRT or bupropion**, all with behavioural support.
- ⚑ **Vaping is now pharmacy-supplied**: adults **18+ without a prescription** after pharmacist consultation; under-18s need one in NSW, Qld, ACT and NT, and cannot be supplied at all in SA, Vic and Tas.

**Cannabis.** Acute: euphoria, altered time sense, conjunctival injection, tachycardia, hyperphagia, impaired short-term memory and reaction time (driving risk; roadside oral fluid testing); paranoia at high dose. **Withdrawal** (irritability, insomnia, vivid dreams, appetite loss) peaks days 2–6, settles in 1–2 weeks. **Psychosis**: dose-dependent. Ever-use **OR 1.41** (Moore 2007, *Lancet*) — a ~40% increase, *not* a doubling; heaviest use **OR 2.09** (Moore 2007); **daily** use **OR 3.2** and **daily high-potency (high-THC)** use **OR 4.8** — a near five-fold risk (Di Forti, EU-GEI 2019, *Lancet Psychiatry*). Worst with **high-potency THC and adolescent onset**; ⚑ continued use is among the strongest modifiable predictors of relapse in first-episode psychosis. **Cannabinoid hyperemesis** — years of daily use, cyclical severe vomiting with well periods, **compulsive hot bathing** for relief, repeated negative workups; antiemetics fail, **topical capsaicin, droperidol and benzodiazepines** work best, **cessation is the cure**. ⚑ Medicinal cannabis is prescribable via TGA Special Access/Authorised Prescriber pathways on a narrow evidence base.

**Smoking cessation.** Australia's leading preventable cause of death. **Ask–Advise–Help**; **Quitline plus pharmacotherapy** roughly triples quit rates.

| Option | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Combination NRT** | **Patch (21 mg/24 h) plus a rapid form** (4 mg gum/lozenge, spray, inhalator). Beats single-form, **as effective as varenicline**; 12 weeks per 12 months on PBS. Higher-dose oral forms if >10/day or first cigarette within 30 min of waking |
| **Varenicline** | α4β2 nicotinic **partial agonist**; titrate over 1–2 weeks to **1 mg bd**, quit ~day 8; **12 weeks**, extendable 12 more. **Most effective single agent** (~15% absolute gain over placebo). ⚑ Neuropsychiatric boxed warning **removed** after EAGLES; ⚑ Australian supply disrupted from 2021 (Champix nitrosamine recall), generics now available |
| **Bupropion** | 150 mg daily ×3 days → **150 mg bd**; quit in week 2; 7–12 weeks. **Contraindicated in seizure disorder, eating disorders, with MAOIs** |
| **Nicotine vaping** | Second-line after first-line pharmacotherapy plus support has failed, with informed consent documented and a defined end point. Never for non-smokers or adolescents |

⚑ **Australian vaping regulation.** Import of **disposable and non-therapeutic vapes banned 1 January 2024**, all other non-prescription vapes **1 March 2024**; from **1 July 2024** lawful supply is **pharmacy-only**, initially prescription-required. From **1 October 2024**, **adults 18+ can buy therapeutic vapes from a pharmacy without a prescription after a pharmacist consultation**; **under-18s need a prescription in NSW, Qld, ACT and NT; South Australia, Victoria and Tasmania prohibit supply to under-18s entirely, even with a prescription**. Plain packaging, **tobacco/mint/menthol only**, nicotine **≤20 mg/mL** without a script. ⚑ **WA and Tasmania still require a prescription for adults.**

**Others in brief.** **GHB/GBL** — steep dose–response, **abrupt coma with rapid spontaneous recovery**, bradycardia, myoclonus; supportive, no antidote; ⚑ **withdrawal in heavy dependent users is severe, benzodiazepine-refractory, resembles DT and can be fatal**. **MDMA** — deaths from **hyperthermia**, serotonin syndrome and **hyponatraemia** (SIADH plus excess water at festivals — collapsed, seizing, Na 118). **NPS** — synthetic cannabinoids, cathinones and ⚑ **nitazenes** (ultra-potent synthetic opioids now in the Australian supply, often needing repeated or larger naloxone doses), invisible to routine screens. **Nitrous oxide** — subacute combined degeneration via B12 inactivation.

## 5.9 Dual diagnosis

**The short version**
- **Comorbidity is the rule** — around half of people in AOD treatment have a mental disorder and vice versa; causality runs **both ways**, plus **shared vulnerability**.
- ⚑ **Treat both concurrently, in one place** — sequential "get clean first" models fail and are no longer accepted practice.
- Attribution is hard; **observed abstinence is the only reliable test** — but don't withhold treatment meanwhile.

Comorbidity worsens adherence, relapse, self-harm and suicide, hospitalisation, homelessness and retention. Pairings to know: **alcohol with depression and suicide**; **cannabis and stimulants with psychosis**; **benzodiazepines and alcohol with anxiety disorders** (each maintaining the other); **PTSD with alcohol and opioids**; **ADHD with stimulant use**; **BPD with polysubstance use**.

**Attribution.** Substance-induced disorders begin **during or shortly after intoxication or withdrawal**, are proportionate to the substance's known effects, and **remit within days to weeks of abstinence**. Independent disorders **predate** use, persist through sustained abstinence (conventionally >1 month), and often carry a family history. A longitudinal timeline plus collateral beats any investigation.

**Management.** Integrated concurrent treatment, one case manager, consistent messaging. ⚑ **Do not withhold antidepressant or antipsychotic treatment simply because someone is still using.** Prescribe with dependence and overdose in mind: avoid benzodiazepines where feasible, SSRIs over TCAs, watch interactions (methadone with QT-prolonging drugs; carbamazepine lowering methadone levels). ⚑ Beware **diagnostic overshadowing** — missing evolving psychosis, hypothyroidism, subdural or delirium. Use **assertive engagement, MI and harm reduction**, and address housing, income and justice contact.

## 5.10 Harm reduction and the Australian system

**The short version**
- The framework is **harm minimisation**: **demand reduction, supply reduction, harm reduction**. **Needle and syringe programs** are among the most cost-effective health interventions evaluated here.
- **Two supervised injecting facilities**: **Sydney MSIC (Kings Cross, 2001)** and **Melbourne (North Richmond, 2018)** — **no deaths on site**.
- ⚑ **Drug checking now exists**: fixed-site **CanTEST** (ACT, from July 2022, funded to at least June 2027); **Victoria** — mobile festival service from December 2024, fixed site (Fitzroy) from August 2025; **NSW** — 12-month mobile festival trial from March 2025. ⚑ **Queensland's CheQpoint service closed in April 2025 and drug checking was outlawed there in September 2025** — the one jurisdiction to reverse course. Harm reduction targets **harm**, not necessarily **use**.

**Needle and syringe programs** supply sterile equipment, disposal, education and referral via fixed sites, outreach, vending machines and pharmacies. They have held **HIV prevalence among people who inject drugs below ~2%** in Australia, return several dollars per dollar spent, and do **not** increase injecting or initiation — the most-tested objection and the most comprehensively refuted.

**Supervised injecting facilities** supervise pre-obtained drugs, with overdose response, sterile equipment and **low-threshold entry to treatment**: thousands of overdoses reversed with no on-site deaths, less public injecting, more OAT uptake, no rise in local drug crime, under specific enabling legislation. **Drug checking** analyses a sample alongside a brief intervention; ⚑ CanTEST repeatedly finds high-risk adulterants (nitazenes, cathinones sold as MDMA), many clients then discard their drugs, and it doubles as a community early-warning system. **Also**: take-home naloxone, OAT itself, real-time prescription monitoring, BBV testing, ⚑ **HCV antivirals without abstinence restrictions**.

**Legal and ethical framing.** The National Drug Strategy has articulated harm minimisation since 1985, yet possession and supply remain criminal offences in every jurisdiction, with **decriminalisation and diversion varying widely** — cannabis decriminalised in the ACT, cautioning schemes in most states, drug courts in several. **Confidentiality applies as to any patient**, bounded by the usual duties (imminent risk, child protection, fitness to drive). Ethically it rests on **beneficence**, **justice** and **autonomy**; **stigma is itself a health hazard**, which is why the language in §5.1 is clinical, not cosmetic.

## 6.1 Anorexia nervosa

**The short version**
- Restriction → significantly low weight **for that person's expected trajectory**, with intense fear of weight gain and disturbed experience of shape/weight.
- Subtypes over the last 3 months: **restricting** vs **binge-eating/purging**.
- **Highest mortality of any psychiatric disorder** — roughly half medical, half suicide.
- ⚑ **BMI alone must not determine severity, urgency or admission.** Medical instability and *rate* of loss decide.
- Complications are consequences of starvation and reverse with nutrition — **except bone density**.

**The picture.** Usually an adolescent or young adult, under-recognised in males, presenting via a parent, a weight check, amenorrhoea, syncope or incidental bradycardia. Intake is restricted covertly and rationalised as "clean eating" or sport: rigid food rules, body-checking alternating with avoidance, driven exercise through injury. Weight and shape become the sole basis of self-worth, and premorbid perfectionism and rigidity are amplified by starvation — hence cognition cannot be fairly assessed until weight is restored. Egosyntonicity is the trap: the illness is experienced as an achievement, so **collateral history beats the patient's account and you weigh the patient yourself** (gowned, post-void — patients water-load).

| System | Findings | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardiac** | Sinus **bradycardia** (30–50), hypotension, postural drop, **QTc prolongation**, low voltages, reduced LV mass, pericardial effusion, **arrhythmia/sudden death** | Vagal predominance + myocardial atrophy; QT worsens with ↓K⁺/↓Mg²⁺ |
| **Electrolytes** | ↓K⁺, ↓PO₄³⁻, ↓Mg²⁺, ↓Na⁺ (water-loading); alkalosis with vomiting, acidosis with laxatives | Normal until refeeding unmasks total-body depletion |
| **Bone** | Osteopenia/osteoporosis, stress fractures | ↓oestrogen + ↑cortisol + ↓IGF-1. ⚑ **The OCP does not restore BMD**. DXA if amenorrhoeic ≥6–12 mo |
| **Endocrine** | **Hypothalamic amenorrhoea**, **sick euthyroid** (↓T3), ↑cortisol, hypoglycaemia | Do **not** give thyroxine. Growth arrest, pubertal delay, height loss |
| **Haematological** | Pancytopenia — anaemia, **neutropenia**, thrombocytopenia | **Gelatinous marrow transformation**; infection without fever or leucocytosis |
| **GI** | Delayed emptying, bloating, constipation, GORD, **SMA syndrome**, transaminitis | Transaminases rise from starvation *and* refeeding |
| **Other** | Lanugo, carotenoderma, cold intolerance, **hypothermia**, oedema, proximal myopathy | Refeeding oedema is expected — pre-warn |

⚑ **Weight is not the risk marker — physiology is.** A patient at normal or higher BMI who has lost weight rapidly (atypical AN/OSFED) can be **as unstable or more so** than one at low BMI, because adaptation has been faster. Australian guidance is explicit that BMI must not gate treatment or admission; adolescents are assessed on **%median BMI for age/sex (%mBMI)** and their own growth chart.

**Medical admission criteria.** Any one generally warrants medical (not psychiatric) admission. ⚑ Figures **differ between states** — know the shape of the list, quote one set and say whose.

| Domain | Adult — QuEDS (Qld) medical-admission | Adult — NSW Health (2014) | Paediatric/adolescent (RCH) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | **≤40** or **>120** | **<40** or **>110** | **<50** awake |
| Postural HR rise | **>20** | **>10** | **>50** |
| Systolic BP / postural drop | **<80** mmHg / **>20** mmHg | **<90** mmHg / **>10** mmHg | **<80** / **>20** |
| Temperature | **<35.5 °C** (medical admission; **<36.0 °C** psychiatric), cold/blue peripheries | **<35.5 °C** | **<35.5 °C** |
| ECG | **Any arrhythmia**, QTc prolongation, ST/T change | **Any arrhythmia**, QTc prolongation, ST/T change | **QTc >450 ms** |
| K⁺ / Na⁺ | **<3.5** / **<125** | **<3.5** / **<130** | **<3.0** / **<130** |
| PO₄³⁻ / Mg²⁺ | **<0.75** / **<0.7** | **<0.8** / **0.7–1.0** | **<0.9** |
| Glucose | **<3.0** mmol/L | **<3.5** mmol/L | **<3.0** |
| Anthropometry | BMI **<12** (medical), **12–14** (psychiatric) | BMI **<16** — ⚑ *supportive, never sufficient or necessary* | **%mBMI <70%** |
| Rate of loss | **≥1 kg/week** over several weeks | **≥1 kg/week** over several weeks | **>1 kg/wk** or **>15% in 3–6 months** |
| Other | Intake <1000 kcal/day, eGFR <60, **neutrophils <0.7**, uncontrollable purging | Intake <1000 kcal/day, eGFR <60, **neutrophils <2.0**, uncontrollable purging | Syncope, dehydration, failed outpatient care, food refusal, suicidality |

**Workup:** weight/height, lying and standing HR + BP, temperature, **ECG (QTc)**, FBC, EUC, **CMP incl. phosphate and magnesium**, LFT, glucose, TSH, CK.

## 6.2 Refeeding syndrome

**The short version**
- The fluid/electrolyte shift after reintroducing nutrition to a starved patient — the highest-yield topic here.
- Carbohydrate → **insulin surge** → intracellular uptake of **phosphate, potassium, magnesium**, plus sodium/water retention.
- **Hypophosphataemia is the hallmark**; killers are **arrhythmia, cardiac failure, respiratory failure**, plus **Wernicke's** without thiamine.
- Prevent with **thiamine before/with the first feed**, prophylactic phosphate, **daily bloods**, cautious escalation with cardiac and fluid monitoring.
- ⚑ Australian specialist units have moved away from "start low, go slow" — under monitoring, **higher starting energy** shortens admission without excess refeeding syndrome; not for an unsupervised general ward.

**Mechanism.** In starvation, insulin falls and intracellular PO₄³⁻, K⁺ and Mg²⁺ are depleted while **serum levels stay normal** — stores are gone, the serum does not show it. Reintroduce carbohydrate, insulin surges, and all three are driven **into cells** for glycolysis and ATP synthesis; serum crashes at **24–72 h** (risk persists 1–2 weeks). **↓PO₄³⁻** → no ATP → cardiac failure, arrhythmia, respiratory muscle failure, rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis, seizures, delirium. **↓K⁺** → arrhythmia, weakness, ileus. **↓Mg²⁺** → arrhythmia, tetany, **refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia**. **Thiamine** is the pyruvate dehydrogenase cofactor, consumed by a carbohydrate load → **Wernicke's**. **Na⁺/water retention** → oedema and, in an atrophic myocardium, **cardiac failure**.

**Risk:** %mBMI <70 or very low weight; **negligible intake ≥5–10 days**; rapid weight loss; **pre-existing low PO₄³⁻, K⁺ or Mg²⁺** (highest risk); alcohol misuse; purging; sepsis, diabetes, malabsorption, post-bariatric surgery. Prolonged fasting from any cause qualifies.

| Element | Australian practice (QuEDS/NSW) |
|---|---|
| **Thiamine** | **Refeeding in an eating disorder** (this section): QuEDS **300 mg IM/IV 30 min before feeding and daily for the first 3 days, then 100 mg oral daily**; NSW Health instead **100 mg oral bd for the first 3 days**, then oral thereafter — **before or with the first carbohydrate**. ⚑ Different context, different dose: **alcohol-related** thiamine (§5.3) is 300 mg IM/IV daily for 3–5 days then 300 mg oral daily for several weeks in the malnourished, and **≥500 mg parenterally daily for ≥3–5 days** in *established* Wernicke's |
| **Micronutrients** | Daily multivitamin; **zinc sulfate 50 mg daily**; prophylactic **phosphate (Phosphate-Sandoz 500 mg bd)** if high risk |
| **Starting energy** | High risk **0.8–1.0 × BEE ≈ 1000 kcal/day**; extreme risk **0.5 × BEE** (~20 mL/h). Specialist unit: **40 mL/h continuous NG ≈ 6000 kJ/1500 kcal/day** |
| **Escalation** | Extreme risk **200–300 kcal every 2–3 days**. Specialist: day 3–4 → 2000, day 5–6 → 2500, day 7+ → **3000 kcal/day** |
| **Bloods** | **EUC, CMP (PO₄, Mg, Ca), LFT, glucose daily for the first 7–10 days, then second daily until goal energy intake is reached, then twice weekly on weigh days from about week 3** |
| **Cardiac/fluid** | ECG + continuous cardiac monitoring while unstable; strict fluid balance, daily weights (early gain is fluid) |
| **If levels fall** | **Replace, do not stop feeding** — hold the rate steady; correct **magnesium first** if K⁺ is refractory |

**Traps.** Normal admission phosphate does **not** exclude risk — the syndrome is defined by what happens *after* feeding starts. NG feeding is a medical intervention, not a punishment; never give dextrose before thiamine.

## 6.3 Bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, ARFID and OSFED

**The short version**
- **Bulimia nervosa** = recurrent **binges + compensatory behaviours** (vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, fasting, exercise), self-evaluation dominated by shape/weight, at **normal or higher weight**.
- **BED** = binges with distress and **no regular compensation** — the commonest ED, commonly missed.
- A **binge** = objectively large amount in a discrete period **with loss of control** — the loss of control is the core, not the calorie count.
- **Low weight + binge-purging = anorexia nervosa, BP subtype** — weight decides, AN takes precedence.
- Purging signs: **Russell's sign, parotid swelling, dental erosion, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis**.

| | AN (BP subtype) | Bulimia nervosa | BED |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weight | **Low** | Normal or above | Any, often higher |
| Compensation | Present | **Regular** | **Absent/irregular** |
| Dominant risk | Starvation physiology | **Electrolytes** | Metabolic/cardiovascular |
| First-line therapy | FBT/specialist psychological therapy | **CBT-E** (± fluoxetine) | CBT-E / guided self-help |

**Signs of purging.** **Russell's sign** — knuckle calluses from self-induced vomiting; **bilateral painless parotid swelling (sialadenosis)** with raised **salivary** amylase; **perimylolysis** — erosion of the **palatal surfaces of upper incisors**. Also palatal petechiae, oesophagitis, Mallory–Weiss tears, rarely Boerhaave's.

**Biochemistry.** Vomiting loses H⁺ and Cl⁻ → **hypokalaemic hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis**, secondary hyperaldosteronism worsening K⁺ loss. **Laxatives** instead give **normal anion gap metabolic acidosis** with hypokalaemia; **diuretics** mimic vomiting without the oral signs. Hypokalaemia is the proximate cause of sudden death. **Pseudo-Bartter syndrome** — stopping purging causes rebound oedema; warn the patient or they will resume.

**ARFID.** Restriction with nutritional/psychosocial consequence (weight loss, faltering growth, deficiency, tube dependence) but **no body-image disturbance or drive for thinness**. Drivers: sensory sensitivity, low interest in eating, or fear of an aversive consequence (choking, vomiting). Over-represented in autism and anxiety.

**OSFED.** Full severity, incomplete criteria: **atypical anorexia nervosa** (all features, weight normal or above), low-frequency BN or BED, purging disorder, night eating syndrome. ⚑ **Not "mild"** — atypical AN carries comparable mortality.

## 6.4 Management of eating disorders

**The short version**
- **Adolescents: family-based treatment (FBT/Maudsley) is first-line** — parents temporarily take charge of refeeding; the illness is externalised from the child.
- **Adults: CBT-E is first-line** (alternatives MANTRA, SSCM, focal psychodynamic therapy).
- The **GP coordinates and medically monitors**, with a dietitian and mental health clinician.
- ⚑ **Antidepressants are not a treatment for anorexia**; **fluoxetine 60 mg/day has evidence in bulimia**.
- Involuntary treatment is justified when starvation makes refusal of life-saving nutrition itself a symptom.

**Setting.** Mostly outpatient; admit for **medical instability** (§6.1), failed outpatient treatment or acute psychiatric risk. Discharge to an ongoing team — the post-discharge period is high risk.

**FBT.** ~15–20 sessions over 6–12 months. Phase 1: parents take full control of eating and weight restoration, the therapist coaching rather than refeeding. Phase 2: graded handback as weight normalises. Phase 3: adolescent identity and development. Explicitly **non-blaming of parents** — a deliberate break from older family-pathology models.

**CBT-E.** 20 sessions (40 if underweight). Targets the **overvaluation of shape and weight**: regular eating (three meals + snacks, never >4 h apart), self-monitoring, dietary rules, event/mood triggers, body-checking, relapse prevention; broad modules add perfectionism, low self-esteem, mood intolerance.

| Disorder | Pharmacotherapy |
|---|---|
| **Anorexia** | ⚑ **No drug treats the core disorder.** SSRIs ineffective at low weight. Treat comorbid depression/OCD **after weight restoration** and reassess — much resolves with nutrition. **Olanzapine** may modestly assist weight gain/rumination. Avoid QT-prolonging drugs while unstable |
| **Bulimia** | ⚑ **Fluoxetine 60 mg/day** — only agent with robust evidence, **higher than the antidepressant dose**, **adjunct to CBT-E**. Avoid **bupropion** (seizure risk in purging) |
| **BED** | CBT-E/guided self-help; SSRIs modest; lisdexamfetamine **not TGA-approved here** |

**Medicare.** An **Eating Disorder Treatment and Management Plan**, prepared by a medical practitioner, gives **up to 40 rebated psychological and 20 dietetic sessions per 12 months**, with reviews required before exceeding 10, 20 and 30 psychological services — the review before session 21 requires **two** reviews, one by the GP/managing practitioner and one by a **psychiatrist or paediatrician**. Eligibility is broadest for anorexia; other EDs must meet severity and impairment criteria, otherwise a Mental Health Treatment Plan (10 sessions).

**Involuntary treatment.** Anorexia is treated as a mental illness under state legislation, and **refusal of nutrition in severe starvation is generally not competent refusal** — cognition is impaired and the wish to stay underweight is a symptom. Involuntary admission and NG feeding are used for imminent risk to life, as the **least restrictive** option for the shortest period. In adolescents, parental consent may suffice if the young person is not Gillick-competent (§6.8).

## 6.5 Personality disorders, with borderline in depth

**The short version**
- An **enduring, pervasive, inflexible** pattern deviating markedly from cultural expectation, **traceable to adolescence**, **stable across situations**, causing **distress or impairment**.
- **Cluster A** odd/eccentric — paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal; **B** dramatic/erratic — antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic; **C** anxious/fearful — avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive.
- ⚑ The **categorical model is contested** — ICD-11 replaces it with **severity plus trait domains**, keeping only a borderline pattern qualifier.
- **BPD** presents acutely: affective instability, emptiness, identity disturbance, abandonment sensitivity, impulsivity, **self-harm and chronic suicidality**, stress-related paranoia/dissociation.
- **Structured psychological therapy is the treatment; medication is adjunctive at best.**

**Why categories are contested.** Comorbidity between categories is the rule; "PD not otherwise specified" was among the commonest diagnoses; temporal stability and biological validity are poor; and the label attracts stigma used to justify withholding care. ICD-11 therefore codes **severity plus traits** — negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, anankastia; DSM-5 kept the ten categories with a dimensional alternative in Section III. ⚑ Diagnosis is **careful, not forbidden, in under-18s** — early intervention works.

**BPD — the picture.** Onset in adolescence; ~1–2% prevalence, far higher in ED populations. Emotions are **intense, rapidly shifting and slow to settle** — hours, not the days to weeks of a mood episode. Chronic **emptiness**; unstable **sense of self**; **frantic efforts to avoid abandonment**, so a cancelled appointment or a clinician's leave can precipitate crisis. Relationships oscillate between idealisation and devaluation. **Impulsivity** across domains — substances, spending, driving, sex, binge eating. **Recurrent self-harm**, usually to **regulate unbearable affect or end dissociation**, not to die. Under stress, **transient paranoid ideas or dissociation** lasting minutes to hours and resolving with containment — not psychosis.

**Splitting.** Self and others held as wholly good or wholly bad because ambivalence is intolerable, appearing as **team splitting** — one clinician is the only one who understands, another is cruel, roles reversing abruptly. The antidote is structural: **a shared care plan, team communication, one consistent message**, and clinicians who resist the flattery of being the good object.

**Chronic vs acute-on-chronic risk.** BPD carries a **~10% lifetime suicide rate**, so chronic risk is real and must never be dismissed as attention-seeking — but it does not respond to admission. Detect **acute-on-chronic change**: new or escalating method, comorbid **major depressive episode** or substance use, recent loss or discharge, planning with lethal means, loss of a therapeutic relationship. ⚑ Prolonged admission for chronic suicidality is **counterproductive**; **brief purposeful admission (24–72 h)** is the compromise.

**Managing an ED presentation.**
1. **Assess what is treatable** — injury/overdose, then comorbid depression, psychosis, intoxication or withdrawal. Avoid **diagnostic overshadowing**: people with BPD get medically ill and depressed.
2. **Validate before problem-solving.** Naming the distress as understandable does not endorse the behaviour; it makes thinking possible. Most escalation follows feeling dismissed.
3. **Structure and predictability** — quiet space, explicit time frames ("20 minutes now, back at 4"), clear boundaries.
4. **Avoid both errors.** *Dismissal* confirms the abandonment schema and raises risk; *over-medicalisation* (admission, sedation, escalating investigation) reinforces crisis behaviour. Aim for **active, collaborative, time-limited containment**.
5. **Use the existing crisis plan** rather than improvising at 2 am.
6. ⚑ **Medication is not primary therapy** — at most a **short low-dose sedating course for acute crisis (days)**, avoiding benzodiazepines. Treat comorbidity on its merits and **limit quantity supplied**.
7. **Continuity is the intervention.** Discharge with **named follow-up in 1–3 days** (Project Air brief-intervention model), a safety plan, means restriction, and communication to GP and case manager.

**DBT.** Linehan's manualised therapy, best-evidenced for BPD, built on a **biosocial model** (emotional vulnerability meeting a chronically invalidating environment) and on holding **acceptance and change** together. ~12 months, **four components**: (i) **individual therapy** (diary card, chain analysis, target hierarchy: life-threatening > therapy-interfering > quality-of-life-interfering); (ii) **skills group** — **mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness**; (iii) **phone coaching**; (iv) a **therapist consultation team**, managing burnout and countertransference. Alternatives: **MBT, schema-focused therapy, transference-focused psychotherapy, STEPPS**.

**Antisocial PD and the forensic overlap.** Pervasive disregard for others' rights since ~15 — deceitfulness, impulsivity, aggression, reckless disregard for safety, **absence of remorse** — requiring **conduct disorder before 15**, so the thread is conduct disorder → ASPD (§6.7). Over-represented in custodial populations and strongly comorbid with substance use disorders, which drive much of the offending. "Psychopathy" (Hare PCL-R) is a narrower callous-unemotional construct, **not a diagnostic category**. Treat the substance use and comorbidity (the modifiable part) and keep firm transparent boundaries. **Mental illness, not personality disorder alone, is the usual gateway to a forensic pathway.**

## 6.6 Countertransference and the therapeutic frame

**The short version**
- **Countertransference** = the clinician's emotional response to the patient — inevitable, informative, dangerous only when unrecognised.
- Rescue urges, irritation, dread, boredom and hopelessness are **data about the interaction**, not a bad clinician or patient.
- The **frame** — time, place, role, confidentiality, contact rules — is therapeutic in itself.
- **Boundary crossings** are minor context-dependent deviations; **boundary violations** exploit and harm.
- **Supervision** converts countertransference into information rather than acted-out behaviour.

**Why these patients provoke strong reactions.** Distress is communicated **relationally** — the clinician comes to feel the helplessness, rage or abandonment the patient cannot articulate (projective identification). Chronic suicidality provokes fear of blame, repeated presentations fatigue, idealisation flatters and disarms; in eating disorders the analogue is a battle over food that drags clinicians into coercion or collusion. Unexamined, this yields two failure modes — **punitive dismissal** ("manipulative," "not really unwell") or **special exceptions** (extra time, personal contact, private arrangements) — the second reliably preceding the first.

**Boundaries.** The frame is consistent appointment times and length, a professional setting, defined roles, agreed between-session contact, explicit confidentiality limits, no dual relationships. A **boundary crossing** — a small gift, brief self-disclosure, a home visit — may be benign but should be conscious, justifiable and documented. A **boundary violation** serves the clinician and harms the patient: **any sexual contact** (absolutely prohibited at any time; Ahpra treats it as such), financial or business dealings, social/romantic relationships, excessive self-disclosure. Slippery-slope signs: secrecy, scheduling the patient last, contact outside agreed channels, treating them as uniquely special — most reliably, **being unwilling to bring the case to supervision**.

**Supervision.** Regular, protected and non-optional here — individual, group, or a consultation team as in DBT. It makes countertransference usable, tests decisions against a second mind, detects boundary drift early and sustains compassion. Team equivalents: shared care plans, handover of split dynamics, debriefing after incidents.

## 6.7 Child and adolescent psychiatry — common disorders

**The short version**
- Assess in **developmental context** — normal at 4 is pathological at 14; judge by **impairment across more than one setting**, not symptom count.
- **ADHD** and **autism** are **neurodevelopmental** — lifelong, from early childhood, commonly comorbid with each other and with anxiety, frequently diagnosed late.
- **Anxiety is the commonest childhood disorder**, often presenting as **school refusal** or somatic complaints.
- **Childhood depression looks like irritability and behaviour change**, not classical low mood.
- **Multiple informants** — child, parent, school — are mandatory.

**ADHD.** Inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity from early childhood (before 12), **impairing in more than one setting**, corroborated by **more than one reporter**. Inattentive presentation (careless errors, poor sustained attention, disorganisation, forgetfulness) is under-diagnosed, especially in girls, presenting late as underachievement or anxiety; hyperactive-impulsive features decline into inner restlessness. Comorbidity is the norm — ODD/conduct disorder, anxiety, depression, learning disorders, autism, tics, substance use. Differentials: hearing/vision impairment, sleep disorder including OSA, absence seizures, anxiety, trauma, intellectual disability. ⚑ **Diagnosis must not rest on rating scales or observation alone** (AADPA 2022).

*Management (AADPA 2022).* In children **parent/family training is a strong first recommendation**, with school supports and treatment of comorbidity; in adults **CBT-based intervention should be offered**. **Medication is first-line where severity requires rapid symptom reduction**. **First-line: methylphenidate, dexamfetamine or lisdexamfetamine**; **non-stimulants atomoxetine, guanfacine, clonidine** where stimulants are contraindicated, ineffective, or where tics or diversion risk complicate. Screen cardiac and family sudden-death history first; **in children**, monitor **height 6-monthly** and **weight at 3 and 6 months after starting then 6-monthly**; **in all ages**, HR and BP **before and after each dose change and 6-monthly**. ⚑ **Stimulants are Schedule 8, governed by state/territory law** — historically specialist-initiated with GPs continuing under authority, but through 2025–26 several jurisdictions opened GP pathways: **NSW** trained GPs may continue existing scripts from 1 September 2025, with diagnosis/initiation training commencing March 2026; **Queensland** specialist GPs may initiate, modify and continue for **adults** from 1 December 2025; **WA** and **ACT** have GP continuation pathways with initiation phasing in. Victoria, SA, Tasmania and NT remain continuation-only under state authority.

**Autism spectrum disorder.** A **developmental** diagnosis: persistent difficulty in **social communication and reciprocal interaction** (reduced social-emotional reciprocity, atypical nonverbal communication, difficulty forming relationships) **plus restricted, repetitive behaviours/interests** (stereotypies, insistence on sameness, circumscribed interests, sensory hyper- or hypo-reactivity). Often masked until social demands exceed capacity — hence late diagnosis in adolescence, in females and in intellectually able individuals. Assessment combines **developmental history plus structured observation**; standardised tools such as **ADOS-2/ADI-R** are commonly used but are **not mandated**, and a single suitably qualified clinician may diagnose in clear-cut cases, per the **National Guideline for the Assessment and Diagnosis of Autism in Australia, 2nd edition (Autism CRC, 2023)**, with **DSM-5 or ICD-11** as the diagnostic reference. High comorbidity: intellectual disability (~one third), ADHD, anxiety, epilepsy, sleep disorder, ARFID. Management is **developmental and educational** — early intervention, communication and family support, school accommodations, NDIS. **No medication treats core autism**; drugs target comorbidity (risperidone/aripiprazole for severe irritability).

**Anxiety and school refusal.** Separation, generalised, social anxiety and specific phobias are the commonest childhood disorders, often **somatic** — morning abdominal pain, headache or nausea, worst on school days and absent at weekends. **School refusal is a symptom, not a diagnosis.** Distinguish from **truancy**: in refusal the child is **at home, parents know, distressed, wants to attend but cannot**; in truancy absence is concealed and the child undistressed. Screen for **bullying, learning difficulty, undiagnosed ADHD/autism, parental anxiety**. Manage with **early graded return** (duration of absence best predicts chronicity), a written school plan, parent coaching and CBT.

**Conduct and oppositional defiant disorder.** **ODD** — angry/irritable mood, argumentative defiant behaviour and vindictiveness aimed at **authority figures**, without serious violation of others' rights. **Conduct disorder** — repetitive violation of others' rights or major norms across four domains: **aggression to people/animals, destruction of property, deceitfulness or theft, serious rule violations**. Childhood-onset (<10) with **limited prosocial emotions** has the worst prognosis and is the pathway to ASPD (§6.5). Always assess for **abuse, neglect, family violence, parental substance use and untreated ADHD**. Treatment is **parent management training** (Triple P, Incredible Years) plus treating comorbid ADHD; no medication treats conduct disorder itself.

**Tic disorders and Tourette.** Tics are sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic movements or vocalisations, **preceded by a premonitory urge and temporarily suppressible** — the features distinguishing them from other hyperkinetic movements. **Tourette** = **multiple motor tics and ≥1 vocal tic, >1 year**, onset before 18. Onset 5–7 years, peak around puberty, and **most improve substantially by early adulthood**; coprolalia is uncommon (~10%) and not required. Burden is driven by comorbid **ADHD and OCD**. **Psychoeducation first**; treat only for pain, injury or functional impairment — **CBIT/habit reversal first-line**, clonidine or guanfacine then aripiprazole/risperidone second-line.

**Childhood depression and self-harm.** ⚑ Presentation **differs from adults**: **irritability rather than sadness**, somatic complaints, school decline, withdrawal, separation anxiety in younger children; anhedonia and hopelessness emerge in adolescence. Screen for bullying, family violence, substance use, sexuality/gender-identity stressors, bereavement. **Self-harm affects ~10% of adolescents** and is usually affect regulation rather than an attempt to die — but it is the **strongest single predictor of later suicide**. Psychological therapy first for mild–moderate; ⚑ **fluoxetine is the SSRI of choice under 18**, started low, with **review at least fortnightly through the initial phase** and explicit monitoring for behavioural activation and emergent suicidality **over the first 4 weeks**; combine with CBT/IPT-A, with family involvement and means restriction as core.

**Eating disorders in adolescence.** Peak onset mid-adolescence. Assess against **%mBMI and the growth chart**; **growth arrest, pubertal delay and failure of expected weight gain** are the adolescent equivalents of adult weight loss. Reserve is smaller and deterioration faster, so paediatric criteria trigger earlier on **heart rate (<50 awake) and %mBMI/growth**, though they are *not* uniformly stricter than the adult sets — RCH's K⁺ (<3.0, vs the adult <3.5) and postural HR rise (>50, vs the adult >20 QuEDS / >10 NSW) are the **more permissive** figures, tripping later rather than earlier, so ⚑ never carry a paediatric figure across to an adult stem or vice versa (§6.1); **FBT is first-line** (§6.4).

## 6.8 Assessing a young person

**The short version**
- Interview the young person **alone for part of every consultation**, framed as routine practice rather than suspicion.
- Use **HEEADSSS** (**cross-reference Paediatrics §12** for the full structure).
- State confidentiality **and its limits at the start**, before disclosures are made.
- **Gillick competence** governs consent by a mature minor (**Paediatrics §12**) — decision-specific, reassessed each time.
- Involve family wherever possible, but not at the cost of engagement.

**Structure.** Start together for the presenting problem and developmental history, then **see the young person alone** — normalise it ("I see everyone your age on their own for part of the visit") so it does not imply the parent is the problem. Return to the carer to agree a plan, having negotiated what will be shared. Where family is not the right informant, take collateral from school, case worker or GP.

**HEEADSSS** — Home, Education/employment, Eating and exercise, Activities and peers, Drugs and alcohol, Sexuality, Suicide/self-harm/mood, Safety (violence, abuse, online, road). Move least to most sensitive, and ask about suicide directly — it does not plant the idea. Full structure in **Paediatrics §12**.

**Confidentiality and its limits.** State up front that what is discussed stays between you **except** where there is risk of serious harm to them, risk to someone else, or a mandatory reporting obligation (child abuse/neglect — mandatory for medical practitioners in every Australian jurisdiction, thresholds set by state law). If a limit is triggered, **say what you will do and why before doing it**. Breaching confidentiality without warning loses an adolescent from care; concealing a mandatory report is not an option.

**Consent and Gillick competence.** A minor may consent if they have sufficient understanding and intelligence to fully comprehend what is proposed — assessed **for the specific decision**, so competence for a contraceptive prescription does not imply competence to refuse NG feeding in anorexia. **Refusal** by a mature minor carries less weight than consent and may be overridden by parents, a court, or mental health legislation. **Cross-reference Paediatrics §12.**

**Involving family.** Carer engagement is a treatment component, not a courtesy — FBT, parent management training and family psychoeducation all work through the family. Assess carer capacity and stress, and where the young person objects, negotiate partial disclosure rather than accepting a veto. Where family is unsafe, substitute school, extended family or a case manager.

# Psychiatry Notes — Section 7: Treatments, Law and Services
*Sources: RANZCP ECT guidelines (2019), Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, NSW Mental Health Act 2007, Victorian Mental Health and Wellbeing Act 2022, MBS Online, PBS, ACSQHC/AIHW, National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement (2022). Scope: the psychotherapies, the drug reference, ECT and neurostimulation, the law authorising involuntary care, restrictive practice, confidentiality and the service system.*

## 7.1 The psychotherapies

**The short version**
- Three registers: **insight-oriented** (psychodynamic — makes the unconscious conscious), **short-term focussed** (CBT/IPT — changes a defined maintaining mechanism now), **supportive** (holds function, does not destabilise).
- **CBT has the broadest evidence base** — the default answer unless the stem points elsewhere.
- Match therapy to **mechanism**: avoidance → exposure; role transition/grief → IPT; emotional dysregulation and self-harm → DBT; ambivalence → MI.

**CBT.** Model: **event → automatic thought → emotion, physiology, behaviour**; the behaviour (avoidance, safety behaviour) prevents disconfirmation and maintains the belief. Layers: automatic thoughts → intermediate rules → **core beliefs (schemas)**; distortions include catastrophising, all-or-nothing thinking and mind-reading. Course: shared formulation → **thought records** (situation, emotion, automatic thought, evidence for/against, balanced alternative, re-rate) → **behavioural experiments** → **behavioural activation** → **exposure** → relapse-prevention blueprint. Structured and agenda-set; **homework is the active ingredient**; **typically 8–20 sessions**.

- **Behavioural activation** — withdrawal → lost reinforcement → worse mood, reversed by **scheduling activity by value and mastery, not mood**. Effective standalone; deliverable by non-specialists.
- **Exposure** — graded, repeated, **without safety behaviours** (which preserve the belief that escape averted catastrophe). In vivo, imaginal, **interoceptive** (panic), **ERP** for OCD. ⚑ Inhibitory-learning framing has displaced "stay until anxiety halves" — the goal is **violated expectancy**.

**IPT.** Time-limited; targets the **interpersonal context**, not cognitions, through one or two of four foci: **grief, role transition, role dispute, interpersonal deficits**.

**Psychodynamic psychotherapy.** Current distress repeats **unconscious conflict and early relational patterns**. Tools: **transference** (feelings for earlier figures displaced onto the therapist), **countertransference** (the therapist's reaction, used as data), **defences** — mature (humour, sublimation), neurotic (repression, displacement, intellectualisation), immature (**splitting, projection, projective identification, denial, acting out** — the first and third mark borderline organisation) — and **interpretation** producing **insight**. Needs ego strength; **relatively contraindicated in acute psychosis, mania or crisis**.

**DBT.** For **BPD with chronic self-harm**. Individual therapy + **skills group** (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness) + phone coaching + consultation team. Dialectic = **acceptance and change**; targets ranked life-threatening → therapy-interfering → quality-of-life-interfering.

**ACT.** Targets **psychological flexibility**, not thought content: acceptance, **defusion**, present-moment contact, self-as-context, **values**, committed action.

**Supportive therapy.** Not uncovering — ventilation, reassurance, advice, psychoeducation, problem-solving, environmental intervention. ⚑ It **strengthens defences rather than dismantling them** — the pivot away from psychodynamic work.

**Motivational interviewing.** Guiding style for **ambivalence**; evokes **change talk**, avoids the righting reflex. Technique in **§5**. Exam point: MI does not treat depression — it is the **precursor** that gets someone to accept a treatment.

**Family therapy.** **Family-based (Maudsley) treatment for adolescent anorexia** — first-line, parents control refeeding (§6); **family psychoeducation in schizophrenia**, cutting relapse by lowering **expressed emotion** (criticism, hostility, over-involvement) (§4).

| Therapy | Targets | Duration | Evidence | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CBT** | Maintaining cognitions and behaviours | 8–20 sessions | Strongest, broadest | Concrete, does homework |
| **Behavioural activation** | Withdrawal, lost reinforcement | 8–16 | Non-inferior to full CBT in depression | Non-specialist delivery |
| **IPT** | Grief, transition, dispute, deficits | 12–16 | Strong — depression, bulimia | Clear interpersonal trigger |
| **Psychodynamic** | Conflict, defences, transference | 16–30 to years | Moderate; BPD (MBT) | Ego strength; **not** acute psychosis |

## 7.2 Psychopharmacology overview

**The short version**
- **SSRI first-line**; efficacy across classes is similar, so **the distinguishing hazard chooses the drug**.
- **Serotonin toxicity is fast, hyperreflexic, clonic; NMS is slow, rigid, hyporeflexic; anticholinergic toxicity is dry.**
- Interactions that kill: **MAOI with serotonergics or tyramine**; **lithium with NSAIDs/ACEi/thiazides/dehydration**; tramadol or St John's wort on an SSRI.
- Antidepressants **do not cause dependence** — but stopping abruptly causes a **discontinuation syndrome**.

| Class | Mechanism | Key adverse effects | Distinguishing hazard |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SSRI** (sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, citalopram, fluvoxamine) | 5-HT reuptake block | GI upset, headache, sleep change, **sexual dysfunction 40–60%**, early anxiety spike | **Hyponatraemia** in elderly; **bleeding** with NSAID/anticoagulant; **citalopram QT**; fluoxetine long t½ (5-week MAOI washout); paroxetine worst discontinuation; fluvoxamine ↑ clozapine |
| **SNRI** (venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine, duloxetine) | 5-HT + NA reuptake | As SSRI + sweating, tachycardia | **Venlafaxine: dose-related ↑BP >150–225 mg, severe discontinuation, high overdose toxicity** |
| **TCA** (amitriptyline, nortriptyline, clomipramine) | Reuptake + antimuscarinic, antihistaminic, α1 block | Dry mouth, constipation, retention, sedation, postural drop | **Lethal in overdose — Na⁺-channel block → broad QRS, VT, seizures**; bicarbonate is the antidote |
| **MAOI** (phenelzine, tranylcypromine); **RIMA** (moclobemide) | Blocks MAO-A/B | Postural hypotension, insomnia, weight gain | **Hypertensive crisis with tyramine**; **serotonin toxicity** with any serotonergic. Moclobemide reversible → far safer |
| **Mirtazapine** | α2 antagonist + 5-HT2/3 block | **Sedation — paradoxically worse at low dose** (relative H1 dominance); **weight gain and increased appetite at all doses** | Rare agranulocytosis; chosen for insomnia + weight loss in the frail elderly |
| **Agomelatine** | MT1/MT2 agonist, 5-HT2C antagonist | Headache; weight- and sexually neutral | ⚑ **Hepatotoxicity — LFTs at baseline, ~3, 6, 12, 24 weeks and each dose rise** |
| **Vortioxetine** | 5-HT reuptake + multimodal receptor action | Dose-related nausea; less sexual dysfunction | Signal for cognitive symptoms; PBS-restricted |

**Mood stabilisers** — **§2**. Recall: lithium level **12 h post-dose**; lithium alone has an anti-suicide effect; valproate effectively barred in childbearing potential; lamotrigine **halved** with valproate, any rash → stop.

**Antipsychotics** — **§4**. Typicals — D2 block, EPSE, hyperprolactinaemia; atypicals — D2 + 5-HT2A, dominated by **metabolic** harm; **clozapine** for treatment resistance (agranulocytosis, myocarditis in the first 4 weeks, seizures, mandatory monitoring). **Metabolic monitoring — see §1.7 for the full monitoring table.** Australian schedule (Adult Positive Cardiometabolic Health Resource, Mindgardens/Equally Well 2023): **weight and waist weekly for the first 6–8 weeks** after starting or changing an antipsychotic, and **all parameters — weight/waist, BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids — at baseline, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months, then 6-monthly**.

**Anxiolytics and hypnotics.** **Benzodiazepines** — GABA-A positive modulation; effective short-term but cause **tolerance, dependence, withdrawal seizures, falls, cognitive impairment, and respiratory depression with opioids/alcohol**. Prescribe for **days to weeks with an exit plan**; avoid in PTSD and substance dependence. Long t½ (diazepam) for tapering; **oxazepam** (no active metabolites) in the elderly and hepatic impairment. **Z-drugs** — same dependence risk plus a ⚑ TGA warning on **complex sleep behaviours**. CBT-I first-line for insomnia; **propranolol** for autonomic performance anxiety only.

| Feature | Serotonin toxicity | NMS | Anticholinergic toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | **Hours (<24 h)** | **Days (1–3)** | Hours |
| **Neuromuscular** | **Hyperreflexia, clonus, legs > arms** | **Lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia** | Normal |
| **Pupils** | **Mydriasis** | Normal | **Mydriasis** |
| **Bowel sounds** | **Increased**, diarrhoea | Normal/reduced | **Absent — ileus, retention** |
| **Skin** | Diaphoretic | Diaphoretic | **Dry, flushed, hot** |
| **Temperature** | Moderate ↑ (usually <38.5 °C) | **↑ — diagnostic threshold >38.0 °C on at least 2 occasions**; frequently >38.5 °C and may exceed 40 °C | ↑ |
| **CK** | Normal–mild ↑ | **Markedly ↑**; rhabdomyolysis → AKI | Normal |
| **Management** | Cease, **benzodiazepines**, cooling, fluids; **cyproheptadine** if moderate–severe | Cease antipsychotic, ICU-level support, cooling, fluids; **dantrolene ± bromocriptine** if severe | Cease, supportive care, benzodiazepines; **physostigmine** only on toxicology advice |

Hook: **serotonin = hyper and fast; NMS = rigid, hot, slow, high CK; anticholinergic = dry.** Also on the differential: malignant hyperthermia (suxamethonium/volatiles, masseter spasm, ↑EtCO₂) and sympathomimetic toxicity (sweaty, no rigidity). Triggers: serotonergic added or interacting; dopamine blocker started (or agonist withdrawn); antimuscarinic load.

**Discontinuation syndromes.** Onset **1–4 days** after stopping or a large dose cut (later with fluoxetine): flu-like malaise, dizziness, nausea, insomnia, vivid dreams, **"brain zaps."** Worst with **paroxetine and venlafaxine**; minimal with fluoxetine. ⚑ **Duration is not the discriminator from relapse** — it is brief (days to a few weeks) only after **short-term treatment of 6–12 weeks**; after longer treatment withdrawal can be **severe and protracted, lasting months** (Aust Prescr 2026;49:99–103). Use the **onset window** instead: discontinuation begins **within 1–4 days** of the dose change, is physical/neurological, and settles within hours of reinstatement; relapse takes weeks and reproduces the illness. Taper **gradually, scaled to treatment duration and half-life — 2–4 weeks may suffice after a short course, but after long-term use taper over months**, using **hyperbolic (proportional) dose reductions** for paroxetine, venlafaxine and duloxetine. ⚑ Not dependence — no craving, no dose escalation. Benzodiazepine withdrawal *is* dependence, with seizure risk.

**Hyponatraemia/SIADH.** SSRIs and venlafaxine. Risk: **age >65, female, low body weight, thiazide, low baseline Na⁺, carbamazepine**. Usually within **2–4 weeks**: nausea, lethargy, falls, confusion, seizures — easily blamed on the depression or on dementia. **Check Na⁺ at baseline and 2–4 weeks in older patients.** Mild and stable — continue and monitor; moderate–severe — cease, fluid restrict, **correct slowly (osmotic demyelination)**.

**QT prolongation.** Highest risk: **citalopram and escitalopram** (dose-dependent), **haloperidol** (especially IV), **droperidol**, ziprasidone, amisulpride, pimozide, TCAs, methadone. ⚑ TGA caps: **citalopram 40 mg/day, 20 mg if >65, hepatic impairment or CYP2C19 poor metaboliser; escitalopram 20 mg/day, 10 mg if >65.** ECG before and after starting with cardiac disease, electrolyte disturbance or other QT drugs; correct **K⁺ and Mg²⁺**.

**Interactions that matter.** **MAOI + tyramine** (aged cheese, cured meat, fermented soy, tap beer, yeast extract) → hypertensive crisis. **MAOI + any serotonergic** (SSRI, SNRI, TCA, triptan, tramadol, pethidine, linezolid, St John's wort) → serotonin toxicity; washout **2 weeks either way, 5 weeks after fluoxetine**. **Tramadol** — serotonergic *and* proconvulsant. **St John's wort** — serotonergic **and** a CYP3A4/P-gp inducer failing the OCP, warfarin and antiretrovirals. **Lithium** — levels rise with **NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs, thiazides, dehydration, low salt intake, AKI**. **Clozapine** — ↑ with fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin and **smoking cessation**.

## 7.3 ECT — practicalities, consent and the law

**The short version**
- A modified generalised seizure under **general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation**; **2–3 per week, 6–12 per course** (indications in §2).
- **Right unilateral ultrabrief** = fewest cognitive effects; **bitemporal** = fastest and most effective — speed versus memory.
- Dosing is relative to **seizure threshold** (RUL ≈6×, bitemporal ≈1.5×); adequacy is **EEG seizure quality (≥~25 s with post-ictal suppression)**, not a good-looking convulsion.
- Consent is the examinable part: **capacity-specific, informed, written, revocable**; without capacity a **tribunal** must authorise.

**How it is given.** Pre-anaesthetic and cognitive baseline workup; IV **propofol or thiopentone** at lowest effective dose (both raise threshold), **suxamethonium**, bite block, pre-oxygenation and bag-mask ventilation; EEG and motor seizure recorded; monitored recovery. Threshold is titrated at the first treatment and **rises through the course**. **Benzodiazepines and anticonvulsant mood stabilisers raise the threshold and blunt efficacy** — reduce or cease where safe; **lithium** risks prolonged seizure and post-ECT delirium (withhold on treatment days); antipsychotics are compatible. Seizure >120 s is terminated pharmacologically.

**Relative contraindications:** raised ICP or space-occupying lesion, recent MI or stroke, unstable aneurysm/AVM, phaeochromocytoma, retinal detachment, prohibitive anaesthetic risk. **Pregnancy is not one.**

**Side effects by time course.** Immediate — anaesthetic risk, headache, myalgia, nausea; **post-ictal confusion over 15–60 min**. Early — **anterograde amnesia**, usually recovering within **2–4 weeks** of the course ending. Persistent — **retrograde autobiographical amnesia around the treatment period**, permanent in a minority, more likely with bitemporal placement. Mortality is that of a brief GA — approximately **2.1 deaths per 100,000 treatments (~1 in 50,000)**.

**Consent and involuntary ECT.** ⚑ **State-based — name the jurisdiction.** Capacity is presumed and assessed **for this decision**.
- **NSW (MHA 2007).** An **ECT consent inquiry** confirms a voluntary patient has capacity and has consented in writing. An **ECT administration inquiry** covers those who cannot consent, involuntary patients and under-16s: the Mental Health Review Tribunal must find the person lacks capacity and that ECT is **reasonable and proper** and **necessary or desirable for their safety or welfare**, on written certification by **two medical practitioners, at least one a psychiatrist**. It authorises a **specified number, usually up to 12**.
- **Victoria (MHWA 2022).** An adult with capacity may consent in writing. ⚑ **No substitute decision-maker — guardian, parent or family — may consent to ECT for an adult.** Where capacity is absent, the authorised psychiatrist applies to the **Mental Health Tribunal**, which must find **no less restrictive way to treat** and weigh the person's views and preferences and any **advance statement of preferences**. It specifies **up to 12 treatments within a maximum 6 months**. **All under-18s need a Tribunal determination even if consenting.** Urgent hearings exist where ECT is needed to save life.
- ⚑ ***PBU & NJE v Mental Health Tribunal* [2018] VSC 564** — a capable person may refuse ECT even if the refusal seems unwise; **lack of insight is not automatically incapacity.**

**Myths to correct.** Not given awake or unmodified; no structural brain damage on imaging; **not a last resort** (§2); not wholesale memory erasure, though retrograde loss must be disclosed; most Australian ECT is given **with consent**. Relapse without maintenance is high, so **ECT always hands over to continuation pharmacotherapy**.

## 7.4 Other neurostimulation

**The short version**
- **rTMS**: awake, no anaesthetic, no induced seizure, no cognitive impairment; **MBS-funded since 1 November 2021**.
- ⚑ MBS rules are strict: **18+, ≥2 antidepressant classes adequately trialled, prescribed and mapped by a trained psychiatrist**; **35 initial + 15 retreatment sessions — a lifetime cap of 50, once only**; any prior rTMS (public or private) disqualifies.
- **Esketamine** is TGA-registered for treatment-resistant depression and ⚑ **PBS-listed from 1 May 2025**, supervised with post-dose observation.

**rTMS.** Focal alternating magnetic field over the **left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**; weekday sessions over **4–6 weeks**, shortened by theta-burst protocols. Patient awake, drives home. Scalp discomfort and headache are common; the serious risk is **seizure (<1 in 10,000 sessions)**. Contraindicated with **ferromagnetic implants near the coil**, cochlear implants or deep brain stimulators. No systemic or cognitive effects — its advantage over ECT — but weaker in severe or psychotic depression. Items **14216/14217** (prescription/mapping, initial delivery) and **14219/14220** (retreatment).

**Ketamine/esketamine.** NMDA antagonists with downstream glutamatergic/AMPA effects. **Intranasal esketamine** is given with an oral antidepressant, self-administered **under supervision in an approved setting with ≥2 hours' observation** for sedation, dissociation and BP rise; no driving that day; twice-weekly induction then tapering. **Racemic ketamine** (IV/SC) is off-label and specialist-only. Onset is **rapid (hours–days), including on suicidal ideation, but wanes without repeat dosing** — a bridge, not maintenance. Concerns: dissociation, hypertension, cystitis, abuse liability, thin long-term data. Not a substitute for ECT in psychotic depression or catatonia.

## 7.5 Mental health legislation

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Each state and territory has its own Act — name the state before quoting any threshold or time limit.**
- The universal test: **mental illness/disorder present; risk of serious harm or serious deterioration; no less restrictive option reasonably available.** Diagnosis alone never suffices; risk alone never suffices.
- These Acts authorise treatment of **the mental illness only** — physical treatment runs on consent and guardianship law.
- ⚑ Both Acts require a **statement of rights** to be given and explained, and guarantee representation and free communication.
- ⚑ A **statutory patient-initiated right to a second psychiatric opinion** exists in **Victoria** (and the ACT, Qld and WA) but **not in NSW**, where a second opinion is a matter of clinical courtesy rather than legislation.

**Never grounds by themselves:** political, religious or philosophical belief; sexual preference or gender identity; immoral or illegal conduct; substance use alone; intellectual disability alone; economic or social status.

**NSW — Mental Health Act 2007.** A **mentally ill person** has a condition seriously impairing mental functioning with characteristic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, serious thought disorder, severe mood disturbance) *and* needs care to prevent **serious harm** — physical, financial, reputational or self-neglect. A **mentally disordered person** need not have a diagnosable illness but behaves so irrationally that temporary care is needed to prevent **serious physical harm**; ⚑ that detention is capped at **3 days excluding weekends and public holidays**, reviewed 24-hourly, **maximum 3 per month**.

Pathway: a medical practitioner or accredited person completes a **mental health certificate (Schedule 1)** after examination; police and ambulance may also bring a person in. An **authorised medical officer must examine within 12 hours** of arrival, and continued detention needs **two examinations by two doctors, at least one a psychiatrist**. The person then goes before a **mental health inquiry conducted by the Mental Health Review Tribunal** as soon as practicable — the Tribunal, not the treating team, decides. An **involuntary patient order** made at inquiry runs **up to 3 months**; review is **at least 3-monthly for the first 12 months**, then at least 6-monthly. **CTOs** run **up to 12 months**, the Tribunal being satisfied it is the **least restrictive alternative**, that a workable treatment plan exists, and (where there is a history) that treatment has been refused with consequent deterioration. Breach permits the person to be taken to a facility for treatment; ⚑ a CTO **does not authorise forcible injection at home** — enforcement runs through recall.

**Victoria — Mental Health and Wellbeing Act 2022** (replaced the 2014 Act; commenced 1 September 2023, implementing Royal Commission recommendations). All orders require mental illness, need for **immediate treatment** to prevent serious deterioration or harm, and no less restrictive means:

| Order | Made by | Maximum duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Assessment order** (community or inpatient) | Medical practitioner or authorised mental health practitioner, having examined within the previous 24 h | Community **24 h**; inpatient **24 h from arrival or 72 h from making, whichever is earlier**; extendable **twice by 24 h** |
| **Temporary treatment order** | **Authorised psychiatrist** — ⚑ not the person who made the assessment order | **28 days**; Tribunal must hear before expiry |
| **Treatment order** (community or inpatient) | **Mental Health Tribunal only** | **6 months** adult; **3 months** under 18 |

⚑ The divergence students get wrong: **in Victoria only the Tribunal makes the ongoing treatment order**, whereas in NSW the initial order is made at a Tribunal inquiry flowing from clinical certification. Victorian orders default to **community** unless the person cannot be treated there. The Act is explicitly **supported decision-making**: **views and preferences** and any **advance statement of preferences** considered, a **nominated support person** recognised, and **opt-out non-legal advocacy (IMHA)** notified when an order is made.

**Interface with guardianship and consent law.** ⚑ A mental health order authorises treatment **of the mental illness** — not a laparotomy or dialysis. For physical treatment in someone lacking capacity use consent and substitute-decision law: advance care directive, enduring guardian/medical treatment decision maker, the "person responsible" hierarchy, or the guardianship tribunal (**NCAT** in NSW, **VCAT** in Victoria); emergency treatment is permitted where delay risks life. Sterilisation and psychosurgery always need tribunal approval. **Being an involuntary patient does not itself mean incapacity for other decisions.**

## 7.6 Seclusion, restraint and acute behavioural disturbance

**The short version**
- **Least restrictive practice always** — a last resort after de-escalation genuinely fails, for the shortest time possible.
- **De-escalate first**: environment, space, one communicator, offer of oral medication, food, a phone call.
- **Oral before parenteral** — **olanzapine wafer 5–10 mg** or **diazepam 5–10 mg** are standard Australian first-line options.
- ⚑ **Do not give a parenteral benzodiazepine within 1 hour of IM olanzapine** — excessive sedation, cardiorespiratory depression and, rarely, death. The rule runs **one way**: if a parenteral benzodiazepine has already been given, IM olanzapine may follow only after careful clinical evaluation with close monitoring.

**De-escalation.** Reduce stimulation, remove the audience, two exits and no entrapment, one nominated communicator, calm voice, acknowledge the grievance, offer choices, set limits without threats. Always exclude organic causes: hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, head injury, delirium, intoxication or withdrawal, sepsis, pain, retention.

**Drugs (adults).** ⚑ Real variation between jurisdictions and settings — quote the local protocol.
- **Oral (preferred):** **olanzapine wafer 5–10 mg** (max 30 mg/24 h), **diazepam 5–10 mg**, or **lorazepam 1–2 mg** (max 8 mg/24 h), repeatable ~2-hourly.
- **Parenteral:** ⚑ **droperidol 10 mg IM/IV**, repeatable once after 15 min (commonly capped at 20 mg/24 h), is the dominant Australian **emergency department** first-line (DORM trials); **IM olanzapine 5–10 mg** is the main alternative. ⚑ **Parenteral benzodiazepines (e.g. IM midazolam 5–10 mg), alone or added to droperidol, are *not* recommended in adults** — increased adverse events and more repeat sedation (Qld Health, *Management of patients with acute severe behavioural disturbance*; same position stated in §5.7). Midazolam survives in some local ED protocols as a fallback, but it is not the guideline first- or second-line. ⚑ Several **inpatient mental health** guidelines (e.g. Queensland Health MHAOD) instead use **IM lorazepam 1–2 mg first-line with IM olanzapine second-line and explicitly do not recommend droperidol or midazolam** — an examinable ED-versus-unit divergence.
- **Older, frail or delirious:** halve doses, avoid benzodiazepines (paradoxical worsening), treat the cause. **Avoid haloperidol in stimulant toxicity or hyperthermia**; QT caution with droperidol and haloperidol.

**Monitoring after sedation.** Continuous visual observation, frequency escalating with depth: awake — observations 15-minutely for an hour, then half-hourly; drowsy but rousable — half-hourly vitals; difficult to rouse — **5–10-minutely**; unresponsive — **call the MET**. Record RR, continuous SpO₂, BP, HR, temperature and conscious level until ambulant; watch for **dystonia and akathisia**.

**Governance.** **Seclusion** = confinement alone in a room the person cannot leave; **physical restraint** = staff using hands or body; **mechanical restraint** = devices; **chemical restraint** = medication given primarily to control behaviour rather than treat illness. All need legal authority, documented justification, near-continuous observation, attention to hydration, toileting and privacy, regular review, carer notification, reporting and a **debrief with the consumer**. ⚑ Prone restraint is discouraged nationally (positional asphyxia). Reducing and where possible **eliminating** restrictive practice is national policy under the **National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement (in effect March 2022)**, carrying forward the safety priorities of the Fifth National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Plan (2017–2022), and under the **NSQHS Standards**: ⚑ the AIHW-reported seclusion rate has **more than halved in a decade — ~14 events per 1,000 bed days in 2009–10 to ~6 in 2024–25**.

## 7.7 Confidentiality and its limits

**The short version**
- Confidentiality is strong but **not absolute** — it yields to serious risk, statutory mandate and court order.
- Exceptions: **risk of serious harm to an identifiable other**, **mandatory reporting of children**, statutory notifications, subpoena, treatment-team sharing.
- ⚑ Mental health Acts create a **statutory carve-out** — designated carers must be told certain things without case-by-case consent.

**Risk to others.** A credible threat against an **identifiable person** may justify warning that person, notifying police, intensifying treatment or admission. Australia has not adopted *Tarasoff* wholesale, but privacy law permits disclosure to lessen a **serious and imminent threat to life, health or safety**. Disclose the **minimum necessary** and document the reasoning. Analogous duties: **fitness to drive**, notifiable diseases, and **mandatory notification to Ahpra** for an impaired practitioner placing the public at substantial risk.

**Children.** ⚑ Medical practitioners are **mandatory reporters** in every jurisdiction. NSW — report **risk of significant harm** to the Child Protection Helpline (*Children and Young Persons (Care and Protection) Act 1998*). Victoria — the *Children, Youth and Families Act 2005*, plus Crimes Act offences of **failing to disclose** and **failing to protect**. The trigger is **reasonable suspicion, not proof**; good-faith reports attract protection from liability. Exam point: a parent's illness (puerperal psychosis with infanticidal ideation) triggers consideration of the **children's** safety regardless of the parent's consent.

**Family and carers.** ⚑ Under the **NSW Act** a patient may nominate up to **two designated carers**, and an authorised medical officer may identify a **principal care provider**; they must be notified of **detention** (⚑ the commonly quoted **"within 24 hours"** timeframe is **UNCONFIRMED** — it could not be verified against s78 of the Act, the NSW Health carers page or the Schedule 3 statement of rights; the *events* requiring notification are confirmed, the *deadline* is not), an upcoming inquiry, absconding, transfer, discharge, reclassification to voluntary, ECT applications and Tribunal matters. A designated carer may be excluded by the patient — but not a parent/guardian of someone under 18, and not where exclusion creates serious risk. Victoria recognises a **nominated support person**. Even without consent you may always listen, give **general** information about illness, treatment and services, discuss crisis and discharge planning in general terms, and refer to carer supports. Carers' involvement in assessment, care and discharge planning is recognised in **state carers legislation** — the *Carers (Recognition) Act 2010* (NSW) (NSW Carers Charter: carers are partners in care) and the *Carers Recognition Act 2012* (Vic) — and, operatively, in the carer-notification and consultation provisions of each mental health Act. ⚑ The Commonwealth *Carer Recognition Act 2010* binds **Australian Public Service agencies and Commonwealth-funded providers, not state health services**, and creates no enforceable individual right.

## 7.8 Services and access

**The short version**
- Most mental health care happens in **general practice**, not psychiatry.
- A **GP mental health treatment plan** unlocks **Better Access**: **10 individual + 10 group** subsidised sessions per **calendar year** (⚑ item structure changed 1 November 2025).
- **headspace** = **12–25 years**, self-referral, free or low cost.
- **Barriers** are examinable in themselves: stigma, cost, distance, culture, language, workforce.

**Team.** **Psychiatrist** — diagnosis, complex pharmacology, ECT, legal instruments. **Psychologist** — assessment, psychometrics, structured therapy. **Mental health nurse** — monitoring, depot clinics, physical health; often the **case manager**, who usually holds the relationship. **Social worker** — housing, income, child protection, discharge planning. **Occupational therapist** — function, vocational and cognitive rehabilitation, sensory modulation. Plus **peer/lived-experience workers**, pharmacist, Aboriginal mental health worker, interpreter. The **GP** is the coordinating hub.

**Settings.** **Inpatient** — acute units, high-dependency areas, specialist units (mother–baby, older persons, CAMHS, eating disorders, forensic). **Community teams** — case management, depot clinics, CTO delivery, assertive outreach. **Crisis teams** — 24-hour triage and home-based treatment as an admission alternative. **Consultation–liaison psychiatry** — delirium, capacity assessment, self-harm review, somatic symptom disorders. **PECC/PECU** — short-stay units co-located with EDs (~48–72 h). **NGO/psychosocial** — housing, day programmes, NDIS. **Digital/telephone** — Lifeline 13 11 14, Beyond Blue, 13YARN, online CBT.

**Primary care and funding.** A **GP mental health treatment plan** requires assessment with an outcome measure (K10), agreed goals, treatment and referral, a crisis plan and scheduled **review**. It opens **Better Access** — Medicare-subsidised therapy by psychologists and eligible social workers and occupational therapists, capped at **10 individual and 10 group services per calendar year**, referred in blocks (commonly up to 6, then GP review, then the balance). ⚑ **From 1 November 2025 the MHTP review items (2712 and equivalents) and ongoing mental health consultation items (2713 and equivalents) were removed** — GPs now use **time-tiered general attendance items** to review, refer and provide ongoing mental health care, letting physical and mental health be addressed in one consultation. **Dedicated MHTP preparation items remain**, and are now tied to the patient's MyMedicare-registered practice or usual medical practitioner. **Session caps unchanged.** Also PHN-commissioned stepped care and psychiatrist item **291**.

**Populations.** **headspace** — **12–25**, self-referral, four streams (mental health, physical/sexual health, AOD, vocational/education), with early-psychosis and eheadspace arms. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander** care runs through ACCHOs and social and emotional wellbeing frameworks (§1). **CALD** patients need **professional interpreters — never family, never children**. ⚑ **Rurally**, psychiatrist and psychologist supply falls steeply while suicide rates rise with remoteness: longer waits, more GP and ED presentations, long-distance involuntary transfers by police or air, no local specialist units. Mitigations — **telepsychiatry** (Medicare telehealth now permanent), outreach, GP upskilling, the RFDS, workforce incentives.

**Barriers to care.** **Stigma** — public, self- and structural, including from health professionals. **Cost** — gap fees, the session cap, medication, lost income. **Distance and transport. Waiting lists. Cultural** — mistrust from historic and continuing harms, differing explanatory models, fear of child removal. **Language** — interpreters under-used. **The illness itself** — impaired insight, avolition, paranoia and hopelessness are structural barriers. **Systemic** — workforce shortage, fragmentation between Commonwealth-funded primary care and state-funded specialist services, and the **"missing middle"**: too unwell for Better Access, not unwell enough for state services.

**Consumers and carers with lived experience.** Built into the system rather than consulted about it: **peer workers** in teams; consumer and carer consultants in governance; **advance statements of preferences** and nominated support persons in Victorian law; **official/community visitors** with statutory access to units; lived-experience representation on tribunals and curricula. The **recovery model** (hope, identity, meaning, personal responsibility, connectedness) is the stated framework of the **National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement** and the National Mental Health Strategy. Carers' own burden, financial strain and grief are a legitimate target of assessment.

## 8.1 Syndrome lookup — stem to diagnosis

| Stem cue | Think | Why it's not the near-miss |

| Links between consecutive ideas absent or opaque to the listener | Loosening of associations — schizophrenia (§1.2) | Flight of ideas keeps discernible links (puns, clang, distraction) — mania |

| Episodic headache, sweating and palpitations with paroxysmal hypertension, pallor not flushing | Phaeochromocytoma → plasma free metanephrines (§3.2) | Rare — do not screen every panic patient |

| Syncope at venepuncture | Blood-injection-injury phobia — applied tension (§3.3) | Biphasic vasovagal response; relaxation is the wrong treatment |
| Intrusive taboo thoughts, repugnant and resisted, with mental rituals | OCD (§3.4) | Ego-dystonic with no intent — the opposite of a violent or paraphilic drive |
| Perfectionism and rigidity seen as correct and virtuous; the distress is in others | OCPD (§3.4) | Ego-syntonic pervasive trait, not discrete obsessions with linked compulsions |
| Preoccupation with a slight defect, mirror-checking, seeking cosmetic procedures | Body dysmorphic disorder (§3.4) | Procedures typically worsen it — CBT + SSRI |

| ≥2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations with mood symptoms absent | Schizoaffective disorder (§4.2) | In psychotic depression or mania the psychosis sits **only inside** mood episodes |
| ≥1 month of delusions, no hallucinations, disorganisation or negatives, function preserved outside the theme | Delusional disorder (§4.2) | Schizophrenia brings negative symptoms and global functional decline |
| Inner restlessness with pacing, unable to sit, weeks into an antipsychotic | Akathisia (§4.5) | Anticholinergics do not help it; it is linked to suicidality, not to psychotic agitation |
| Choreoathetoid lip-smacking and tongue protrusion after years of antipsychotic | Tardive dyskinesia (§4.5) | Stop the anticholinergic — the opposite of acute dystonia management |
| Lead-pipe rigidity, fever, autonomic instability and CK in the thousands over 3 days | NMS (§4.7) | Serotonin toxicity is hours, clonic and hyperreflexic |

| Week 3 of clozapine: tachycardia out of proportion, dyspnoea, unexplained CRP rise | Clozapine myocarditis (§4.6) | Peaks in the first 3–4 weeks — not benign clozapine tachycardia |
| Hallucinations at 24 h with intact orientation and a clear sensorium | Alcoholic hallucinosis — escalate benzodiazepines (§5.3) | DT clouds consciousness and disorients; hallucinosis does not |
| Dense anterograde amnesia with confabulation, immediate recall preserved | Korsakoff syndrome (§5.3) | The chronic, largely irreversible sequel of missed Wernicke |
| Mydriasis, lacrimation, yawning, piloerection, cramps, diarrhoea, craving | Opioid withdrawal (§5.5) | Miserable but rarely lethal — unlike alcohol, benzodiazepine or GHB withdrawal |
| Agitated, mydriatic, hyperthermic and drenched in sweat after a binge | Sympathomimetic toxidrome — benzodiazepines first (§5.7) | Wet skin separates it from anticholinergic toxicity |
| Paranoid with formication, clearing over 10 days of abstinence | Stimulant psychosis (§5.7) | Third-person auditory hallucinations and negative symptoms point to schizophrenia |
| Years of daily cannabis, cyclical vomiting relieved only by hot showers | Cannabinoid hyperemesis (§5.8) | Antiemetics fail; cessation is the cure |
| Festival collapse with seizure and Na⁺ 118 | MDMA hyponatraemia (§5.8) | SIADH plus excess free water — not simple heat stroke |
| Abrupt coma with rapid spontaneous recovery, bradycardia, myoclonus | GHB/GBL (§5.8) | No antidote; withdrawal in dependent users is severe and benzodiazepine-refractory |
| Photophobia, hyperacusis, metallic taste and paraesthesiae days after stopping a hypnotic | Benzodiazepine withdrawal (§5.6) | Perceptual hypersensitivity is characteristic and it can progress to seizures and delirium |
| BMI 19 but 15 kg lost in 3 months, HR 44, postural SBP drop 25 mmHg | Medically unstable atypical AN/OSFED — admit (§6.1) | Normal BMI does not exclude instability; BMI must not gate admission |
| Knuckle callus, painless parotid swelling, erosion of palatal incisor surfaces | Self-induced vomiting (§6.3) | Russell's sign, sialadenosis with raised *salivary* amylase, perimylolysis |
| Mood shifts over minutes to hours, abandonment sensitivity, self-harm to end dissociation | BPD (§6.5) | Bipolar mood episodes run days to weeks with sleep, energy and goal-directed change |
| Onset before 12, impairment in more than one setting, more than one informant | ADHD (§6.7) | Never diagnosed on rating scales or observation alone |
| Morning abdominal pain on school days only; child at home, parents aware, distressed | School refusal (§6.7) | Truancy is concealed, undistressed and elsewhere with peers |
| Movements preceded by a premonitory urge and temporarily suppressible | Tic disorder / Tourette (§6.7) | The urge and suppressibility separate tics from other hyperkinetic movements |
| 10-year-old with irritability, somatic complaints and school decline | Childhood depression (§6.7) | Presents as irritability and behaviour change, not classical low mood |

## 8.2 Signature findings

- **Thought alienation** — insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting, made acts/feelings/impulses; ⚑ no longer diagnostically specific for schizophrenia (§1.2).
- **Incongruent affect** (laughing while describing a bereavement) — schizophrenia-spectrum flag (§1.2).
- **Lead-pipe rigidity with CK in the thousands** — NMS. **Inducible or ocular clonus with hyperreflexia** — serotonin toxicity (§4.7).
- **Oculogyric crisis, trismus, opisthotonus; laryngeal dystonia = airway emergency** — acute dystonia (§4.5).
- **Post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome** up to 3 h after olanzapine pamoate LAI (§4.5).
- **Waxy flexibility, catalepsy, negativism, echolalia/echopraxia, automatic obedience, ambitendency** — catatonia (§4.8).
- **Ebstein anomaly** — first-trimester lithium; **neural tube defects** — valproate and carbamazepine (§2.6).
- **"Electric shock" brain zaps** with dizziness and flu-like malaise days after a dose cut — antidepressant discontinuation (§2.3, §7.2).
- **HLA-B*15:02** and carbamazepine SJS/TEN — testing *considered* in Han-Chinese patients in Australia (§2.6).
- **Macrocytosis without anaemia, GGT rise, AST:ALT >2:1** — alcohol (§5.2).
- **Gelatinous marrow transformation**, with infection occurring without fever or leucocytosis — anorexia (§6.1).
- **Formication ("bugs under the skin"), bruxism, "meth mouth", mydriasis, skin excoriation** — methamphetamine (§4.4, §5.7).
- **Russell's sign; bilateral painless parotid sialadenosis with raised salivary amylase; perimylolysis of the palatal surfaces of the upper incisors; palatal petechiae** — purging (§6.3).
- **Pseudo-Bartter rebound oedema** on stopping purging; **refeeding oedema** is expected — pre-warn (§6.1, §6.3).
- **Lanugo, carotenoderma, hypothermia, SMA syndrome, sick euthyroid (↓T3)** — starvation; all reverse with nutrition **except bone density**, and the OCP does not restore it (§6.1).
- **Hypophosphataemia at 24–72 h of feeding** — the hallmark of refeeding syndrome (§6.2).
- **Hypokalaemic hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis** (vomiting) versus **normal anion gap metabolic acidosis with hypokalaemia** (laxatives) (§6.3).
- **Kayser–Fleischer rings** — Wilson disease; **ovarian teratoma** — anti-NMDAR encephalitis (§4.4).
- **Tubular (non-expanding) visual field; give-way ratchety weakness; tremor entrainment** — FND rule-in signs (§3.7).
- **Splitting and team splitting** — BPD; the antidote is structural (one shared care plan, one message) (§6.5).
- **High expressed emotion** — criticism, hostility, emotional over-involvement; predicts relapse in schizophrenia (§4.9).
- **Nocturnal sialorrhoea in 30–80% on clozapine**; tachycardia is benign only once myocarditis is excluded (§4.6).
- **Subacute combined degeneration** in a nitrous oxide user (B12 inactivation) (§5.8).
- **Hoover's sign, tremor entrainment and Hoover-type inconsistency are positive findings** — la belle indifférence and secondary gain are unreliable and must not be used (§3.7).

## 8.3 Confusable pairs

| Pair | The discriminator |
|---|---|
| Delirium vs dementia vs depression | Delirium — acute, **fluctuating attention and consciousness**, disorientation, reversed sleep–wake, visual hallucinations (§1.5, §4.4). Dementia — chronic, clear consciousness. Depression — "memory problems" are impaired concentration on a background of pervasive low mood and anhedonia (§2.1). ⚑ Delirium detail is deferred to Geriatrics §1 |
| NMS vs serotonin syndrome vs malignant catatonia vs anticholinergic toxicity | NMS — dopamine blocker started/increased (or agonist withdrawn), **days (1–3)**, generalised lead-pipe rigidity, normal-to-reduced reflexes, **CK very high**. Serotonin — serotonergic added, **hours (<24 h)**, **hyperreflexia and clonus, legs > arms**, mydriasis, hyperactive bowel sounds and diarrhoea, CK normal/mild. Malignant catatonia — catatonic background, waxy flexibility, posturing, negativism; **lorazepam then ECT**, antipsychotics may worsen it. Anticholinergic — **dry, flushed, hot, absent bowel sounds, retention**, no rigidity (§4.7, §7.2) |
| Akathisia vs agitation vs anxiety | Akathisia — **subjective inner restlessness** with pacing and inability to sit, timed to antipsychotic start or dose rise (usually <4 weeks); **anticholinergics do not help** (reduce dose/switch, propranolol, mirtazapine). Psychotic agitation responds to the antipsychotic; anxiety has cognitive content and no drug-timing relationship (§4.5) |
| Bipolar vs unipolar depression | Any past **mania or hypomania** — ask directly, take collateral. Bipolar depression: earlier onset, more episodes, atypical/psychotic/postpartum features, prior antidepressant switch. Consequence: antidepressant monotherapy risks a manic switch and rapid cycling (§2.1, §2.5) |
| Schizophrenia vs schizoaffective vs mood disorder with psychotic features | Schizoaffective needs **≥2 weeks of psychosis with mood symptoms absent**, with mood episodes present for most of total illness duration. In psychotic depression or mania the psychosis occurs **only within** mood episodes and is usually mood-congruent. Schizophrenia: ≥6 months, negatives and functional decline dominate (§4.2) |
| BPD vs bipolar II | **Minutes to hours**, interpersonally reactive, baseline emptiness, self-harm to regulate affect (BPD) vs **days to weeks** of sustained change with reduced sleep need and increased goal-directed activity (bipolar II) (§6.5) |
| Postnatal blues vs postnatal depression vs puerperal psychosis | Blues — day 3–5, gone by day 10–14, mood reactive, function preserved, ~50–80%. PND — anhedonia, guilt, impaired bonding, months if untreated, ~1 in 7. Psychosis — **first 2 weeks**, rapid onset with marked fluctuation and confusion, infant-focused delusions, 1–2/1000, emergency (§2.7) |
| Alcohol withdrawal vs delirium tremens vs Wernicke's | Uncomplicated withdrawal 6–24 h with a **clear sensorium**; hallucinosis 12–48 h with **intact orientation**; DT 48–72 h with **clouded consciousness and disorientation**; Wernicke is **confusion + ophthalmoplegia + ataxia** from thiamine deficiency and needs parenteral thiamine, not more benzodiazepine (§5.3) |
| Panic vs GAD vs social anxiety | Panic — **discrete crescendo attacks peaking in minutes**, fear of the attack and the body. GAD — **continuous topic-mobile worry ≥6 months** with muscle tension and initial insomnia. Social anxiety — symptoms **only where evaluation is possible**, absent when alone (§3.1–3.3) |
| PTSD vs acute stress disorder vs adjustment disorder | **ASD 3 days–1 month; PTSD beyond 1 month** (delayed expression if full criteria first met ≥6 months out); adjustment disorder needs **no criterion A trauma**, begins within 1 month (ICD-11) / 3 months (DSM-5-TR) of an identifiable stressor and resolves within ~6 months of it ending (§2.8, §3.5) |
| Anorexia vs bulimia vs ARFID | **Weight decides between AN and BN** — low weight with binge–purge is AN, BP subtype. BN sits at normal or higher weight with regular compensation and electrolyte-dominant risk. ARFID has **no body-image disturbance or drive for thinness** (§6.3) |
| FND vs factitious vs malingering | FND — **positive rule-in signs** of internal inconsistency or incompatibility; intent is deliberately **not assessed**. Factitious — conscious falsification with **no obvious external incentive**, and motive need not be proven. Malingering — conscious with an **obvious external incentive**, and not a psychiatric disorder (§3.7) |
| The toxidromes | Sympathomimetic — **wet**, mydriatic, hyperthermic, agitated. Anticholinergic — **dry**, flushed, absent bowel sounds, retention. Opioid — **pinpoint pupils, bradypnoea, sedation**. Serotonergic — clonus, hyperreflexia, diarrhoea. NMS — rigid, hot, slow onset, very high CK (§5.7, §7.2) |
| ADHD vs autism vs conduct disorder | ADHD — inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity from before 12, impairing in >1 setting, corroborated by >1 reporter. Autism — **social communication and reciprocity difficulty plus restricted repetitive behaviours**; a social *skill* deficit, not fear or inattention. Conduct disorder — repetitive **violation of others' rights** across aggression, property destruction, deceit/theft and serious rule-breaking; ODD is defiance towards authority without rights violation (§3.3, §6.7) |
| Catatonia vs stupor | Catatonia is a **psychomotor syndrome diagnosed on ≥3 signs** — mutism, negativism, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, posturing, echophenomena — with response to a **lorazepam challenge**; undifferentiated stupor demands organic exclusion (EEG separates catatonia from non-convulsive status) (§4.8) |
| OCD vs OCPD | **Ego-dystonic**, resisted and distressing, with discrete obsessions and linked compulsions vs **ego-syntonic** pervasive lifelong perfectionism, orderliness and rigidity where the distress is in those around them (§3.4) |
| Illusion vs hallucination vs pseudohallucination | Misperception of a **real** stimulus vs a percept with **no** stimulus in external space with the full force of a true percept vs an experience in inner subjective space and/or recognised as unreal (§1.2) |
| Discontinuation syndrome vs relapse | **Onset within 1–4 days of the dose change**, physical/neurological, settling within hours of reinstatement — relapse takes weeks and reproduces the illness. ⚑ Duration is **not** the discriminator (§2.3, §7.2) |
| Complex PTSD vs BPD | CPTSD — **stable** negative self-concept, less prominent frantic abandonment-avoidance, re-experiencing required, following prolonged inescapable interpersonal trauma (§3.5) |
| Stimulant psychosis vs schizophrenia | Abrupt onset locked to a binge, **tactile and visual** hallucinations, negative symptoms uncommon, resolving in days to ~1 month with abstinence — the diagnosis is retrospective (§5.7) |
| Physiological dependence vs addiction | Tolerance and withdrawal occur with prescribed opioids and benzodiazepines; a use disorder needs **impaired control, salience and use despite harm** (§5.1) |
| Boundary crossing vs boundary violation | A crossing is a minor, context-dependent deviation that should be conscious, justifiable and documented; a **violation serves the clinician and harms the patient** — any sexual contact, financial dealings, dual relationships (§6.6) |

## 8.4 Must-not-miss

| Emergency | Immediate action as stated in the sections |
|---|---|
| **NMS** | Stop all antipsychotics and other dopamine blockers (including metoclopramide, prochlorperazine); medical/ICU transfer; aggressive IV fluids against rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI, active cooling, electrolyte correction, VTE prophylaxis, serial renal function and CK; **benzodiazepine (lorazepam) first pharmacologically**; dantrolene and/or bromocriptine if severe or refractory; ECT if refractory or if malignant catatonia cannot be excluded (§4.7) |
| **Serotonin toxicity** | Cease the drug, supportive care, **benzodiazepines**, cooling and fluids; **cyproheptadine** if moderate–severe (§4.7, §7.2) |
| **Malignant catatonia** | **Lorazepam then ECT — first-line without delay**; withhold or minimise antipsychotics, which can precipitate or worsen it; VTE prophylaxis, hydration, pressure-area and nutritional care from day one (§4.7, §4.8) |
| **Acute laryngeal dystonia** | **Benztropine 1–2 mg IM/IV** (or promethazine), repeat if needed — relief within minutes is diagnostic; **secure the airway**; then reduce dose or switch (§4.5, §4.7) |
| **Clozapine agranulocytosis** | Red result (ANC <1.5 or WCC <3.0) → **cease immediately**, daily FBC, haematology, watch for infection; **ANC <0.5 = agranulocytosis** — treat as febrile neutropenia with cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, consider G-CSF (§4.6) |
| **Fever or sore throat on clozapine** | **Urgent FBC, whenever the last one was taken** (§4.6) |
| **Clozapine myocarditis** | Rising **troponin >2× ULN and/or CRP >100 mg/L** → **stop clozapine, echocardiogram, cardiology** (§4.6) |
| **Clozapine ileus/toxic megacolon** | Treat abdominal pain, distension, vomiting or absent bowel sounds as **ileus/toxic megacolon until excluded — imaging and surgical review** (§4.6) |
| **Lithium toxicity** | Stop lithium, **IV normal saline**, levels 4–6 hourly with UEC and ECG; **haemodialysis if >4 mmol/L**, or >2.5 with significant symptoms or renal failure; levels rebound after dialysis; ⚑ activated charcoal does not bind lithium (§2.6) |
| **Lamotrigine rash** | **Stop immediately**, especially with mucosal involvement, purpura or fever (§2.6) |
| **Severe alcohol withdrawal / DT** | **Diazepam loading 10–20 mg orally every 1–2 h until 60–80 mg given or lightly sedated**; review if still agitated after 80 mg or 4 h; **>120 mg only after specialist review**; refractory DT → ICU with parenteral benzodiazepine ± phenobarbitone; rehydrate, replace Mg/K/PO₄, hunt occult infection (§5.3) |
| **Wernicke encephalopathy** | **Treat empirically on suspicion** — ≥500 mg/day thiamine parenterally for ≥3–5 days; **give thiamine before (or with) any glucose load**, and never withhold glucose from a hypoglycaemic patient (§5.3) |
| **Opioid overdose** | Naloxone **titrated to respiration, not arousal** — 100 µg IV increments (community 400 µg IM/SC or 1.8 mg intranasal); remove any fentanyl or buprenorphine patch; observe **≥2 h** after the last dose in short-acting overdose and **admit for prolonged observation (a day or more) after methadone or controlled-release opioids** (§5.5) |
| **Refeeding syndrome** | **Thiamine before or with the first carbohydrate**, prophylactic phosphate if high risk, daily bloods, cautious escalation with cardiac and fluid monitoring; if levels fall, **replace and hold the feed rate steady — do not stop feeding**; correct **magnesium first** if K⁺ is refractory (§6.2) |
| **Medically unstable anorexia nervosa** | Any single admission criterion generally warrants **medical (not psychiatric) admission** — BMI must not gate it; involuntary admission and NG feeding where refusal of life-saving nutrition is itself a symptom (§6.1, §6.4) |
| **Puerperal psychosis** | **Same-day assessment and almost always admission, ideally to a mother–baby unit**; exclude organic causes (sepsis, eclampsia, thyroiditis) first; antipsychotic ± mood stabiliser, benzodiazepine, **ECT if severe, catatonic or refusing food and fluid**; assess risk to mother **and** infant (§2.7) |
| **Anti-NMDAR encephalitis** | Confirm on **CSF antibodies — serum can be falsely negative**; seek an ovarian teratoma; MRI, EEG and LP where the picture points that way (§1.5, §4.4) |
| **Suicidality after an attempt** | Collaborative **written safety plan** (never a no-suicide contract), means restriction, limited quantities dispensed, SSRIs over TCAs where overdose risk is real; **contact within 7 days of discharge as an actual appointment**, same-day discharge summary to the GP, assertive follow-up (§2.9) |
| **Risk to dependent children** | Mandatory in every adult assessment; Australian clinicians are **mandatory reporters on reasonable suspicion, not proof**; a parent's illness triggers consideration of the children's safety **regardless of the parent's consent** (§1.4, §7.7) |
| **Stimulant hyperthermia** | **Cool aggressively if >39 °C — antipyretics do not work**; benzodiazepines first-line and before antihypertensives; ⚑ avoid unopposed beta-blockade in cocaine toxicity (§5.7) |
| **QTc >500 ms or a rise >60 ms** | Stop or switch the QT-prolonging agent; **correct K⁺ and Mg²⁺** and check interacting drugs first (§1.5, §4.5, §7.2) |
| **Over-sedation after acute behavioural disturbance** | Escalating observation — difficult to rouse: **5–10-minutely**; unresponsive: **call the MET**; record RR, continuous SpO₂, BP, HR, temperature and conscious level until ambulant (§7.6) |

## Gaps

What a written finals paper could reasonably test that sections 1–7 do not cover:

- **Delirium and dementia proper.** §1.5 names delirium as the most important mimic but explicitly defers diagnosis, causes, 4AT/CAM and management to Geriatrics §1. No BPSD pathway beyond "antipsychotics are not first-line", and no cut-off scores for MMSE, MoCA or RUDAS despite all three being named.
- **The four-part capacity test.** Capacity is load-bearing throughout (ECT consent, Gillick, guardianship) but understand / retain / weigh / communicate is never set out.
- **Mental health legislation outside NSW and Victoria.** Qld, WA, SA, Tas, ACT and NT thresholds, orders and time limits are absent (only the second-opinion right is mentioned for other jurisdictions).
- **Forensic psychiatry.** No fitness to stand trial, mental impairment defence, forensic orders or prison mental health pathway — only the ASPD/custodial overlap in §6.5.
- **Overdose management detail.** TCA overdose is named as bicarbonate-responsive with broad QRS, but there is no paracetamol, mixed-overdose or lithium-specific decontamination content and no ECG criteria for TCA toxicity.
- **Sleep disorders.** CBT-I is named as first-line for insomnia; nothing on melatonin, Z-drug dosing, OSA workup or circadian disorders.
- **Older-persons psychiatry.** Late-onset psychosis, late-life depression specifics, elder abuse, and prescribing in frailty beyond "lowest dose, no anticholinergics".
- **Intellectual disability psychiatry**, including diagnostic overshadowing in that population.
- **Gambling and other behavioural addictions.**
- **Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, menopause-related mood change**, and psychiatric aspects of specific physical illness (e.g. post-stroke depression).
- **Gender dysphoria and LGBTIQ+ specific care** beyond LGBTIQ+ appearing as a suicide risk demographic.
- **Adult ADHD pharmacology** — agents are named but with no doses, titration schedules or formulation differences.
- **Antipsychotic dose equivalence and oral-to-LAI conversion** — chlorpromazine equivalents appear only in the TRS definition.
- **Fitness to drive** — appears only as a confidentiality/duty exception, with no standards or reporting thresholds.
- **Perinatal disorders beyond mood** — no perinatal anxiety, OCD or tokophobia; antipsychotic choice in breastfeeding is only "olanzapine and quetiapine have the most safety data".
- **Childhood bereavement and developmental trauma**, and paediatric acute sedation dosing (all ABD doses given are adult).
- **Neurosyphilis, HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder and CNS infection management** once identified as the cause of a psychiatric presentation.
