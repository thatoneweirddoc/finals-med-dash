# Geriatrics Notes — Section 1: Delirium, Dementia and Depression

*Sources: ACSQHC Delirium Clinical Care Standard (2021), Clinical Practice Guidelines and Principles of Care for People with Dementia (NHMRC-approved, 2016; ChEI/memantine update 2024), Therapeutic Guidelines (Psychotropic/Geriatric), Australian Prescriber, DLB Consortium 4th Consensus (2017), Dementia Australia, TGA, PBS, Austroads Assessing Fitness to Drive (2022), Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission restrictive practices rules. Scope: the three Ds — discrimination, delirium diagnosis/management, dementia subtypes/assessment/management, late-life depression — at written-finals depth for Australian practice.*

---

## 1.1 The three Ds — the discriminator table

**The short version**

- Delirium = **acute, fluctuating, inattentive, clouded consciousness**. Dementia = chronic, progressive, clear sensorium. Depression = subacute, mood-congruent, effort-dependent cognition.
- **Inattention is the cardinal feature of delirium** — if attention is intact, it is very unlikely to be delirium.
- Delirium is *superimposed on* dementia in a large proportion of cases; dementia is the single biggest risk factor for delirium. A stem with baseline dementia and 2 days of worsening = delirium until proven otherwise.
- **Attitude to deficits** is the fastest bedside discriminator between dementia and depression: dementia conceals/confabulates, depression complains.
- Any "acute confusion" stem in a written paper is a delirium stem unless the timeline is months.

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression (pseudodementia) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours–days, datable | Months–years, insidious (stepwise if vascular) | Weeks–months; often datable to a loss/illness |
| Course | **Fluctuates**, worse late afternoon/night (sundowning) | Slowly progressive, stable through the day | Diurnal variation — classically worse in the morning |
| Attention | **Impaired — the defining feature** | Preserved until late | Poor concentration but attention testable if engaged |
| Conscious level | **Altered** — drowsy or hypervigilant | Clear until terminal stages | Clear |
| Orientation | Impaired, fluctuating | Impaired, progressive | Usually intact |
| Memory | Registration + recent memory poor (attention-driven) | Recent >> remote; encoding failure, no benefit from cueing | Inconsistent, patchy; improves with cueing/prompting |
| Psychomotor | Hypo- or hyperactive, may alternate | Normal until late | Retardation or agitation |
| Hallucinations/delusions | Common — **visual**, transient, fleeting persecutory delusions | Less common (except **DLB — recurrent formed visual hallucinations**); delusions of theft in AD | Uncommon; if present, mood-congruent (nihilistic, guilt, poverty) |
| Sleep–wake cycle | **Disrupted/reversed** — hallmark | Fragmented late in disease | Early-morning waking; initial insomnia |
| Speech/thought | **Disorganised**, rambling, incoherent | Word-finding difficulty, empty content | Slow, monosyllabic, latency of response |
| Reversibility | Usually reversible (but ↑mortality, may not fully resolve) | Irreversible (bar the treatable mimics) | Reversible with treatment |
| Attitude to deficits | Unaware, distressed by perceptual disturbance | **Conceals, confabulates, minimises** ("my memory is fine") | **Highlights and complains**; "I don't know" answers, poor effort |
| Collateral | "He changed overnight" | "It's been about 2 years" | "He stopped enjoying things first" |

⚑ Do not treat these as mutually exclusive. The three coexist constantly; a depressed patient with dementia who develops delirium is the commonest real ward scenario, and exam stems that give a *baseline* plus an *acute change* are testing whether you separate the two layers.

## 1.2 Delirium — recognition, screening and causes

**The short version**

- Diagnosis is clinical: **acute onset + fluctuating course + inattention + (disorganised thinking or altered conscious level)**. That combination is the CAM algorithm in plain English.
- **Hypoactive delirium is the commonest subtype, is the one most often missed, and carries the worse prognosis.** The quiet, drowsy, withdrawn patient is not "settled" — they are delirious.
- Screen with the **4AT** (score **≥4 → possible delirium**; **1–3 → possible cognitive impairment**; 0 → neither). CAM is the alternative but requires training.
- Delirium is a **medical emergency and a marker of frailty**: it predicts falls, functional decline, longer stay, institutionalisation, new dementia diagnosis and death.
- It is usually **multifactorial** — find and fix *all* the contributors, not one.

**Clinical features.** Acute change in mental status over hours–days, fluctuating within the day; impaired attention (cannot sustain, focus or shift); disturbed cognition (orientation, memory, language, perception); altered arousal from hypervigilance to stupor. Perceptual disturbance is typically **visual** and frightening. Add emotional lability, reversed sleep–wake cycle and disorganised, tangential speech.

**Subtypes.**

| Subtype | Picture | Exam trap |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperactive | Agitated, restless, wandering, calling out, pulling lines, hallucinating | Over-diagnosed — this is the *minority* |
| **Hypoactive** | Withdrawn, drowsy, slow, sparse speech, poor oral intake, "pleasantly confused" | **Commonest, most missed, worst outcomes**; mislabelled as depression, fatigue or "just tired" |
| Mixed | Alternates between the two across the day | Fluctuation itself is the diagnostic clue |

**Screening tools.**

| Tool | Structure | Cut-off / use |
|---|---|---|
| **4AT** | Alertness; AMT4 (age, DOB, place, year); Attention (months backwards); Acute change/fluctuation. Score 0–12 | **≥4 possible delirium; 1–3 possible cognitive impairment**. No training, ~2 min, works in drowsy/uncooperative patients |
| **CAM** | (1) acute onset/fluctuating course **AND** (2) inattention, **PLUS** (3) disorganised thinking **OR** (4) altered conscious level | Positive = **1 + 2 + (3 or 4)**. CAM-ICU for intubated patients |
| Bedside attention | Months backwards, days backwards, serial 7s, digit span | Months backwards is the best single screen |

⚑ **Australian Delirium Clinical Care Standard (ACSQHC, revised 2021)** — 8 quality statements: (1) early identification of risk, (2) multicomponent prevention interventions, (3) patient/carer information and support, (4) prompt assessment and diagnosis with a validated tool, (5) identify and treat the underlying causes, (6) prevent complications of care (falls, pressure injury, functional decline, malnutrition), (7) **avoid antipsychotic medicines**, (8) safe transition from hospital with a documented plan. It specifies **cognitive screening within 24 hours of presentation** for patients aged **65+ — or 45+ for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people** — and for anyone with known cognitive impairment, using culturally appropriate tools.

**Causes — work the list, don't guess.** Think of predisposing (baseline vulnerability) vs precipitating (the acute hit); the frailer the brain, the smaller the insult needed.

| Domain | Specifics |
|---|---|
| Infection | UTI, pneumonia, cellulitis, biliary sepsis, meningitis/encephalitis |
| **Drugs** | Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, promethazine), **benzodiazepines**, opioids (esp. pethidine), steroids, dopaminergics, antiepileptics, digoxin, lithium; polypharmacy, any recent change |
| **Withdrawal** | **Alcohol, benzodiazepines**, nicotine — ask explicitly; often day 2–4 of admission |
| Retention/constipation | **Urinary retention and faecal impaction** — classic missed reversible causes; PR and bladder scan |
| Pain | Undertreated pain, esp. #NOF and post-op |
| Metabolic | Na⁺, glucose, Ca²⁺, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, thyroid, thiamine (Wernicke's), B12 |
| Hypoxia/perfusion | Pneumonia, PE, **ACS (often silent)**, arrhythmia, cardiac failure, anaemia, shock |
| Intracranial | Stroke, **subdural haematoma** (fall + anticoagulant), seizure/post-ictal, tumour |
| Environment | Ward transfers, ICU, sensory deprivation, sleep disruption, restraints, **IDC**, immobility, dehydration |

**Workup.** Collateral history first — baseline and timeline. Full drug review incl. recent changes, PRNs and agents stopped on admission. Exam: vitals with sats and temperature, hydration, focal neurology, chest, abdomen, **bladder scan and PR**, pressure areas. Bedside BSL, ECG, urinalysis (⚑ a positive dipstick alone does **not** diagnose UTI in an older person — asymptomatic bacteriuria is common; treat the patient, not the stick). Bloods: FBE, UEC, CMP, LFT, CRP, TFT, B12/folate, cultures if febrile. CXR. **CT brain if** focal neurology, head injury/fall, anticoagulation, reduced conscious level, or no cause found. LP/EEG only if indicated.

## 1.3 Delirium management — the highest-yield item in this section

**The short version**

- **Treat the cause + non-pharmacological management = first line, always.** Nothing else outranks this.
- **Benzodiazepines are NOT first-line and are themselves a cause of delirium.** The only routine indication is **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal delirium**.
- Antipsychotics are **not recommended** to treat delirium per the Clinical Care Standard; consider only for severe distress or immediate risk of harm when non-drug measures have failed — **lowest dose, shortest time, documented, reviewed daily**.
- Antipsychotics are **contraindicated in DLB and Parkinson's disease** (neuroleptic sensitivity) — use a benzodiazepine there instead if sedation is unavoidable.
- **Physical restraint is avoided** — it worsens agitation, prolongs delirium and causes injury.

⚑ **Past-exam trap: "an elderly patient with acute confusion is given diazepam."** This is wrong on three counts — it is not first-line, benzodiazepines *precipitate and prolong* delirium in older people, and long-acting agents accumulate. The correct answer is to identify and treat the precipitant while instituting non-pharmacological measures. Diazepam is only the right answer if the stem gives you **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal** (and even then, in hepatic impairment or marked frailty, shorter-acting **oxazepam/lorazepam** are preferred as they lack active metabolites).

**Non-pharmacological management (first-line, all patients).**

| Domain | Actions |
|---|---|
| Orientation | Clock, calendar, natural light, repeated reorientation, consistent staff, avoid ward moves |
| Sensory | **Glasses on, hearing aids in and working** — the cheapest effective intervention |
| People | Family/carers to stay; familiar objects from home; interpreter if needed |
| Sleep | Reduce night noise/light, no routine overnight obs or meds where safe, no daytime napping |
| Mobility | Early repeated mobilisation, physio; **remove IDC, lines, telemetry** ASAP |
| Nutrition | Oral intake, dentures in, feeding assistance, monitor bowels |
| Medication | Deprescribe anticholinergics/sedatives; review every drug against the delirium list |
| Basics | Analgesia (regular paracetamol), treat constipation and retention, oxygenation, glycaemic control |
| Communication | Calm, short sentences, one instruction at a time; de-escalate, don't confront |

**Pharmacological management — only if.** Severe distress, or behaviour posing immediate danger, where non-drug strategies have failed. Start low: **haloperidol 0.5 mg** (avoid in prolonged QT and parkinsonian syndromes), **risperidone 0.25–0.5 mg** or **olanzapine 2.5 mg** — single dose, reassess; no regular orders or open-ended PRNs. Review daily, cease early, document the indication, discuss with family, and **do not continue on discharge** — inadvertent continuation is a recognised medication-safety failure. Avoid phenothiazines (anticholinergic burden).

⚑ **Absolute caution: DLB and Parkinson's disease.** Around half of DLB patients show severe neuroleptic sensitivity — irreversible parkinsonism, rigidity, reduced consciousness, autonomic instability, increased mortality. Typicals are contraindicated; atypicals only with extreme caution and consent. If sedation is essential, a low-dose short-acting benzodiazepine is safer. Antipsychotics also increase stroke and death risk in dementia generally.

**Prevention.** Multicomponent (HELP-style) bundles cut incidence by roughly a third: orientation and therapeutic activity, early mobilisation, sleep protocols, vision/hearing correction, hydration, avoiding catheters and restraints, medication review. Prophylactic antipsychotics are **not** recommended.

**Follow-up.** Resolution may take weeks–months. Reassure family it is common, usually reversible, not "going mad". Re-assess cognition at 4–6 weeks — delirium markedly increases subsequent dementia risk. Document the episode and precipitant in the discharge summary.

## 1.4 Dementia — subtypes and how stems distinguish them

**The short version**

- **AD** = insidious **episodic memory** loss first. **VaD** = **stepwise**, vascular risk factors, focal signs. **DLB** = **fluctuation + visual hallucinations + parkinsonism + RBD**. **FTD** = **young, personality/language first, memory relatively spared**.
- Diagnosis needs **cognitive decline in ≥1 domain + loss of function** (that functional decline is what separates dementia from MCI).
- **DLB vs PDD = the one-year rule** — dementia before or within 1 year of parkinsonism → DLB; dementia >1 year after established motor PD → PDD.
- Mixed AD/vascular pathology is the commonest picture at autopsy — "either/or" is a simplification.
- Always screen for **reversible contributors** before labelling.

| Subtype | Proportion | Early hallmark | Stem cues | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alzheimer's disease** | ~60–70% | Anterograde **episodic memory** — repeats questions, misplaces items | Insidious over years; preserved social façade; delusions of theft; later apraxia, agnosia, language; hippocampal/medial temporal atrophy | ChEIs indicated |
| **Vascular** | ~15–20% | Executive dysfunction, slowed processing; memory may be relatively spared | **Stepwise** decline or post-stroke; HTN/DM/AF/smoking; **focal signs**, gait apraxia, early urinary symptoms, pseudobulbar affect; white-matter change/infarcts on imaging | Manage vascular risk; ChEIs not PBS-subsidised |
| **Dementia with Lewy bodies** | ~5–10% | **Fluctuating cognition** with marked variation in attention/alertness | **Recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations** (people, animals), **spontaneous parkinsonism**, **REM sleep behaviour disorder** (acts out dreams, often precedes by years), recurrent unexplained falls/syncope, autonomic dysfunction, severe **neuroleptic sensitivity** | Probable DLB = dementia + ≥2 core features (or 1 core + indicative biomarker). ChEIs often work well |
| **Parkinson's disease dementia** | — | Dementia arising in established PD | Motor PD ≥1 year before cognitive decline | Same Lewy pathology; ⚑ **one-year rule** is the separator |
| **Frontotemporal dementia** | Commonest dementia **<65 yrs** alongside AD | Behavioural variant: **disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive/ritualistic behaviour, hyperorality/dietary change**, executive dysfunction with **relatively preserved memory and visuospatial function**. Language variants: progressive non-fluent aphasia / semantic dementia | Younger patient (50s–60s), referred for "personality change", social/legal transgression, family history; frontotemporal atrophy | ⚑ **ChEIs are not indicated and may worsen behaviour**; SSRIs sometimes used for behaviour |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** | Uncommon but examinable | **Triad: gait apraxia ("magnetic", wide-based, shuffling) + urinary incontinence + cognitive impairment** | Gait disturbance usually **first and most prominent**; ventriculomegaly out of proportion to atrophy | Potentially treatable — **CSF tap test** (large-volume LP with pre/post gait timing) predicts response to **ventriculoperitoneal shunt**. "Wet, wacky, wobbly" — but wobbly comes first |

**Reversible/contributing causes to screen for every time:** hypothyroidism, **B12 and folate deficiency**, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, **depression**, **delirium**, **medications** (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids), alcohol and thiamine deficiency, **subdural haematoma**, NPH, obstructive sleep apnoea, uncorrected hearing and vision loss, neurosyphilis/HIV where risk factors exist.

## 1.5 Cognitive assessment and diagnosis

**The short version**

- **Collateral history is essential** — the diagnosis is made on trajectory and function, not on a screening score.
- **MMSE** (/30, ~<24 abnormal) is education- and language-biased and weak on executive function. **MoCA** (/30, <26 abnormal; +1 point if ≤12 years education) is more sensitive to MCI and executive dysfunction.
- ⚑ **RUDAS** (/30, **cut-off 23 — a score below 23 suggests cognitive impairment**) was developed in Australia and is the tool of choice in **culturally and linguistically diverse** patients — designed so items are minimally affected by education and culture and to survive translation intact.
- ⚑ **KICA-Cog** (/39; **≤33 remote, ≤35 urban/regional**) for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, screened from age **45**.
- **MCI = objective cognitive decline WITHOUT loss of independent function.** Once function goes, it is dementia.

| Tool | Score | Cut-off | Best for | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MMSE | /30 | <24 (approx.; adjust for education) | Familiar, tracks severity, used for PBS documentation | Education/language bias; ceiling effect; poor for executive/frontal and for MCI |
| MoCA | /30 | <26 | MCI, executive dysfunction, vascular and Lewy body patterns | Longer (~10 min); still education-sensitive; requires training/certification |
| **RUDAS** | /30 | **≤22** | ⚑ CALD populations, low literacy, interpreter-mediated assessment; 6 items — memory, body orientation, praxis, drawing, judgement, language | Less familiar to staff; not a substitute for full assessment |
| **KICA-Cog** | /39 | **≤33 remote; ≤35 urban/regional** | ⚑ Older Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people; KICA-Carer gives the informant component | Requires specific materials and cultural competence |
| Clock drawing | — | Qualitative | Rapid executive/visuospatial screen | Not standalone |
| IQCODE / informant tools | — | — | Collateral quantification of change from baseline | Depends on informant reliability |

**Diagnostic pathway.** History (onset, trajectory, domains, function — ADLs/IADLs: finances, medications, driving, cooking, appointments) → **collateral** → cognitive screen with the appropriate tool → depression screen → exam incl. gait, focal neurology, parkinsonism, cardiovascular. **Bloods:** FBE, UEC, CMP, LFT, TSH, **B12 and folate**, glucose/HbA1c, lipids; syphilis/HIV if indicated. **Imaging:** non-contrast **CT brain** minimum, to exclude SDH, tumour, NPH, infarcts; **MRI** if subtype matters or onset is young/atypical. FDG-PET, DaT SPECT (suspected DLB) and CSF/amyloid biomarkers are memory-clinic tools, not finals material.

**MCI**: measurable decline noticed by patient/informant, independence preserved. Manage with vascular risk reduction, exercise, hearing/vision correction, medication review, review at 6–12 months; some convert to dementia, some revert.

## 1.6 Dementia management

**The short version**

- Non-pharmacological, psychosocial and carer-directed care is the foundation; drugs are adjuncts with modest effect.
- **ChEIs (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine)** — PBS-subsidised for **mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease** with specialist confirmation; also helpful in **DLB/PDD** (often the best responders). **Not for FTD.** Side effects: **bradycardia/syncope/heart block, GI upset, cramps, vivid dreams, weight loss**.
- **Memantine** (NMDA antagonist) — PBS for **moderately severe AD**; reduce dose in renal impairment.
- **BPSD: look for the unmet need first — pain, infection, constipation, retention, delirium, boredom, fear, over/under-stimulation.** Drugs come after that search fails.
- ⚑ **Risperidone** is the only PBS-listed antipsychotic for BPSD (psychosis or persistent aggression in moderate–severe AD unresponsive to non-drug measures), and PBS restricts it to **up to 12 weeks** with review.

**Non-pharmacological.** Structured routine, orientation aids, meaningful activity, exercise, music/reminiscence therapy, optimised vision and hearing, environmental modification, carer education. Address vascular risk, alcohol, smoking, OSA; deprescribe anticholinergics.

**Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine.**

| Drug | Use | Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Donepezil / galantamine / rivastigmine | Mild–moderate AD (PBS); DLB and PDD (good evidence, esp. fluctuation and hallucinations); rivastigmine patch if GI intolerance | **Bradycardia, syncope, AV block**; nausea/vomiting/diarrhoea/anorexia; caution with peptic ulcer or NSAIDs; ↑falls via syncope. No efficacy difference between the three |
| Memantine | Moderately severe AD (PBS) | Better tolerated; dizziness, headache, confusion; **dose-reduce in renal impairment** |
| ⚑ Not indicated | **FTD** — ChEIs may worsen behaviour; not PBS-subsidised for vascular dementia | — |

**BPSD — the exam algorithm.** (1) **Describe** the behaviour — what, when, with whom, triggers. (2) **Exclude a medical cause**: **pain**, infection, constipation, urinary retention, dehydration, **delirium**, new drugs, sensory deprivation. (3) **Environmental/psychosocial**: overstimulation, unfamiliar staff, toileting need, hunger, boredom, fear, poor communication. (4) **Non-drug strategies**, trialled and documented. (5) Only then medication — with **informed consent**, a defined target symptom, a review date and a withdrawal plan. A trial of regular paracetamol is underused and often effective for agitation.

⚑ **Residential aged care (post-Royal Commission).** Antipsychotics used to control behaviour are **chemical restraint** — a restrictive practice. Required: **last resort** to prevent harm, alternatives trialled and **documented**; **informed consent** from the resident or a **Restrictive Practices Substitute Decision-Maker**; least restrictive form, shortest duration; recorded in the behaviour support plan; regular review. The five restrictive practices: **chemical, environmental, mechanical, physical, seclusion**. Antipsychotic use is a national aged-care quality indicator.

**Safety, driving, supports.** ⚑ Under **Austroads (2022)** a person with dementia **cannot hold an unconditional licence**; a **conditional licence** with (usually annual) review may be considered on the treating doctor's assessment of insight, judgement, attention, reaction time, visuospatial function and memory, with an **on-road OT driving assessment** in borderline cases; commercial licensing needs specialist assessment **plus** mandatory practical assessment. The **patient** must notify the licensing authority; advise and document this, and report directly if they keep driving unsafely (mandatory in NT and SA). Also: home safety (stove, wandering, falls), **advance care planning and enduring guardianship/POA while capacity remains**, financial abuse risk, **carer strain** (respite, carer payments, National Dementia Helpline), and **My Aged Care** referral for CHSP/HCP, then ACAT and residential care. Dementia is a terminal illness — take a palliative approach in advanced disease.

## 1.7 Depression in older people

**The short version**

- Older people report **less sadness** and more **somatic symptoms, anxiety, irritability, and cognitive complaints** — depression hides behind physical presentations.
- **Pseudodementia**: depression presenting as cognitive impairment. Clues — shorter history, patient *complains* of memory, "I don't know" answers, poor effort, preserved cueing benefit, mood precedes cognition.
- Use the **Geriatric Depression Scale** (GDS-15: **0–4 normal, 5–8 mild, 9–11 moderate, 12–15 severe**); it omits somatic items deliberately.
- ⚑ **Older men have the highest suicide rate of any group in Australia** — ask directly, always.
- **SSRIs first-line** (sertraline/escitalopram) — watch **hyponatraemia/SIADH, falls and fractures, bleeding with NSAIDs/antiplatelets/anticoagulants**, and **QT with citalopram**. **Avoid TCAs.**

**Presentation.** Somatic preoccupation, unexplained pain, weight loss, fatigue, early-morning waking, anxiety, agitation, withdrawal, apathy, hopelessness, self-neglect, "failure to thrive". Anhedonia is more reliable than reported sadness. Screen for bereavement, chronic pain, disability, sensory loss, isolation, alcohol and drug causes (corticosteroids, beta-blockers, opioids, benzodiazepines); exclude hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, occult malignancy.

**Pseudodementia vs dementia.**

| | Depression (pseudodementia) | Dementia |
|---|---|---|
| History length | Weeks–months, datable | Years |
| Complaint | Patient complains loudly | Family complains; patient minimises |
| Effort on testing | Poor — "I don't know" | Tries hard, gives wrong answers, confabulates |
| Cueing | Improves recall | Little benefit |
| Mood vs cognition | Mood change **precedes** cognitive change | Cognitive change first; mood follows |
| Diurnal pattern | Worse in the morning | Worse in the evening (sundowning) |
| Response to treatment | Cognition improves with antidepressant | No |

⚑ Depression and dementia frequently coexist, and depression in later life is itself a **risk factor for, and sometimes a prodrome of, dementia**. If unclear, treat the depression and **reassess cognition after remission** — don't diagnose dementia during an untreated depressive episode.

**Treatment.**

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Non-drug | Behavioural activation, CBT/IPT, exercise, social prescribing, treat pain and sensory loss, address isolation |
| First-line drug | **SSRI — sertraline or escitalopram**. **Start low, go slow** but titrate to an adequate dose; allow 4–6 weeks; continue ≥12 months after remission |
| Alternatives | Mirtazapine (insomnia + weight loss; sedating, ↑appetite); venlafaxine (monitor BP); avoid paroxetine (anticholinergic, short half-life) |
| ⚑ Avoid | **TCAs** — anticholinergic (confusion, retention, constipation), postural hypotension and falls, cardiac conduction effects, lethal in overdose |
| ⚑ Citalopram | **Dose-related QT prolongation** — max **20 mg/day if >65 years** (TGA); avoid with other QT-prolonging drugs or electrolyte disturbance |
| ⚑ SSRI harms | **Hyponatraemia/SIADH** — check Na⁺ at baseline and a few weeks after starting; worst with **thiazides/indapamide**. **Falls and fracture**. **Bleeding** with NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants (PPI or avoid). Serotonin syndrome with tramadol/triptans |
| Severe/refractory | Psychiatric referral; **ECT** effective and well tolerated, esp. psychotic depression, food refusal, high suicide risk |
| Risk | Ask about ideation, plan, means (firearms, stockpiled medicines), hopelessness. Older men, bereavement, physical illness, alcohol and isolation raise risk; fewer warnings and more lethal methods — lower threshold for admission |

## Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Cardinal feature of delirium | Inattention |
| Delirium subtype that is commonest, most missed, worst prognosis | Hypoactive |
| 4AT score indicating possible delirium | ≥4 (1–3 = possible cognitive impairment) |
| CAM positive requires | Acute onset/fluctuation **and** inattention, **plus** disorganised thinking **or** altered conscious level |
| First-line management of delirium | Treat the cause + non-pharmacological measures |
| Only routine indication for a benzodiazepine in delirium | Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal |
| Dementia in which antipsychotics are contraindicated | Dementia with Lewy bodies (and Parkinson's disease) — neuroleptic sensitivity |
| Fluctuating cognition + formed visual hallucinations + parkinsonism + REM sleep behaviour disorder | Dementia with Lewy bodies |
| Rule separating DLB from Parkinson's disease dementia | One-year rule — dementia before/within 1 year of parkinsonism = DLB |
| Dementia with disinhibition, apathy, hyperorality and relatively preserved memory in a 58-year-old | Behavioural-variant frontotemporal dementia |
| Stepwise decline with focal neurology and vascular risk factors | Vascular dementia |
| Gait apraxia + urinary incontinence + cognitive impairment, ventriculomegaly | Normal pressure hydrocephalus (CSF tap test → VP shunt) |
| Cognitive tool of choice in a CALD patient with low education, and its cut-off | RUDAS, ≤22/30 |
| Cognitive tool for older Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients | KICA-Cog (≤33/39 remote, ≤35 urban/regional); screen from age 45 |
| GDS-15 score suggesting moderate–severe depression | ≥10 (5–9 mild) |
| Distinguishes MCI from dementia | Preserved independent function in MCI |
| Only PBS-listed antipsychotic for BPSD, and duration | Risperidone, up to 12 weeks with review |
| Key ChEI adverse effects to screen for before prescribing | Bradycardia/AV block, syncope, GI upset |
| Antidepressant class to avoid in older people | Tricyclics |
| Maximum citalopram dose over age 65 | 20 mg/day (QT prolongation) |
| Electrolyte to check after starting an SSRI in an older patient | Sodium (hyponatraemia/SIADH) |
| Licence status permitted with a dementia diagnosis | Conditional licence only, with review — never unconditional |
