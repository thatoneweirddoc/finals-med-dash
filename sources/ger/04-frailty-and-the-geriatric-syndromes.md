# Geriatrics Notes — Section 4: Frailty and the Geriatric Syndromes

*Sources: Clinical Frailty Scale v2.0 (Rockwood 2020); Fried phenotype; Cochrane CGA review (Ellis 2017); ACSQHC Comprehensive Care Standard and HAC list; NSW ACI Pressure Injury Toolkit; Australian Prescriber; Continence Foundation of Australia; Therapeutic Guidelines (Australian); My Aged Care / Support at Home; ACQSC SIRS; AIFS Elder Abuse Prevalence Study 2021; AGS feeding-tube statement; NICE CG32. Scope: frailty, CGA and the Australian aged care pathway, function, continence, pressure injury, malnutrition, sensory loss, immobility, elder abuse.*

---

## 4.1 Frailty

**The short version**

- Reduced multisystem reserve → a small stressor (UTI, new drug, a night in ED) causes disproportionate functional loss.
- **Fried phenotype**: weight loss, exhaustion, weakness (grip), slowness (gait), low activity. **0 robust, 1–2 pre-frail, ≥3 frail.**
- **Rockwood cumulative deficit model**: frailty index = deficits present ÷ deficits counted (30–70 items). Continuous but impractical by hand; the **Clinical Frailty Scale** (9 points, judgement-based, baseline function) is its bedside form.
- Predicts mortality, delirium, falls, surgical morbidity and institutionalisation **better than age or comorbidity count**.
- **Dynamic and partly reversible** — the examinable punchline, not a nihilistic label.

Fried thresholds: >5% unintentional weight loss over a year; tiredness most of the time; grip, gait speed and weekly activity each in the lowest quintile for sex. Biologically coherent — sarcopenia, neuroendocrine dysregulation and inflammation in a self-reinforcing cycle — but it needs a dynamometer and a measured walk, ignores cognition, and cannot be scored in a bed-bound patient.

**Clinical Frailty Scale** ⚑ — nine levels: very fit → fit → managing well → living with very mild frailty → mild → moderate → severe → very severe frailty → terminally ill. Grading synthesises mobility, energy, IADL then ADL dependence.

- ⚑ **v2.0 (2020) relabelled the levels** ("Vulnerable" → "Living with Very Mild Frailty"; 5–8 → "Living with… frailty"). Numbers unchanged; old wording persists on hospital forms.
- ⚑ Score the **baseline ~2 weeks before the acute presentation** — scoring the illness is the commonest error.
- ⚑ Not validated <65 yrs, **not for stable long-term disability** (frailty ≠ disability), and **not a sole triage rule**. CFS ≥5 commonly triggers geriatrics referral.

**Why it matters, and what reverses it.** Frailty drives the peri-operative risk discussion (it independently predicts post-op delirium, complications and loss of independence) and **changes treatment targets** — loosen HbA1c and BP targets, deprescribe. Reversibility: progressive **resistance exercise** (best evidence, works into the 90s); **protein and energy with exercise** (alone you add fat, not muscle); **deprescribing** anticholinergics, sedatives, hypotensives; treating depression, anaemia, pain, poor dentition and sensory impairment.

## 4.2 Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment

**The short version**

- Multidimensional **interdisciplinary diagnostic process** producing a coordinated plan — assessment *plus* delivery, not a form. Domains: **medical, functional, cognitive/psychological, social and environmental.**
- Inpatient CGA → **more likely alive and in own home at 3–12 months** (RR ~1.06) and **~20% fewer residential care admissions** (RR ~0.80); mortality alone barely moves.
- **Ward-based** CGA beats a roving consult service that only advises. Australian settings: GEM, orthogeriatrics, ACAT, My Aged Care, Support at Home.

| Domain | Content |
|---|---|
| Medical | • Problem list, comorbidity • Medication review incl. OTC • Nutrition • Continence • Pain • Sensory function • Dentition |
| Functional | • ADLs/IADLs • Gait, balance, falls • Transfers • Aids and equipment |
| Cognitive/psychological | • Cognition and delirium status • Mood • Alcohol • BPSD |
| Social/environmental | • Carer capacity and strain • Home layout, stairs, bathroom • Finances, driving • Advance care planning and substitute decision-maker |


**Who does what**: geriatrician — diagnosis, deprescribing, prognosis, goals of care; nursing — continence, skin, delirium screening, day-to-day function; physio — mobility, balance, exercise; OT — ADL/IADL performance, home hazards, equipment; speech pathology — swallow; dietitian — malnutrition; pharmacist — anticholinergic burden; social work — carer support, accommodation, elder abuse. The **family meeting** converts parallel assessments into one plan.

**Australian pathway**: **GEM** subacute beds; **orthogeriatric** co-management of hip fracture (less delirium, shorter stay, better mortality); **My Aged Care** as single entry point → **ACAT/ACAS** assessment → approval; Transition Care post-hospital; **residential aged care** (ACAT approval plus means assessment).

- ⚑ **Support at Home replaced Home Care Packages and Short-Term Restorative Care from 1 Nov 2025**, with **8 ongoing classification levels**. "HCP levels 1–4" is out of date; entry-level help remains via CHSP.

- ⚑ CGA content is embedded in **NSQHS Standard 5 (Comprehensive Care)**, which mandates screening for falls, pressure injury, delirium, malnutrition and self-harm — the geriatric syndromes are an accreditation obligation, not optional.

## 4.3 Functional assessment

**The short version**

- **ADLs** (Katz): bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding. ⚑ The **DEATH** mnemonic (Dressing, Eating, Ambulating, Toileting, Hygiene) is a *different* list — it has ambulating and hygiene but no continence or transferring, so do not use it to recall Katz.
- **IADLs** (Lawton, 7–8 items): shopping, finances, medications, transport, cooking, housework, telephone. The **SHAFT** mnemonic (Shopping, Housekeeping, Accounting, Food preparation, Transport/Telephone) covers five of them.
- **IADLs are lost first** — cognitively demanding, so the **more sensitive early marker**. ADL loss is later and more advanced.
- Barthel = 10 weighted ADL items, max 100. Katz = 6 ADLs scored 0/1, hierarchical loss (bathing usually first). A documented **premorbid baseline** is what makes CFS scoring, rehab goals and discharge planning possible.

Ask about **performance, not capability**, and corroborate with a collateral informant — patients over-report. Probes: who shops and how do they get there; who manages the tablets; who pays the bills; can they shower unaided; falls; still driving. Timed Up and Go and gait speed add objectivity. Barthel has a **ceiling effect** — a perfect score says nothing about IADLs.

**Why it changes decisions**: it separates acute decline from long-standing dependence, sets rehab goals, and determines whether "home with a package" is realistic. Unexplained IADL decline should prompt cognitive assessment, mood screening and a medication review, not a referral for home help.

## 4.4 Urinary and faecal incontinence

**The short version**

- Types: **stress, urge, overflow, functional, mixed** — separable on history.
- **Screen reversible causes first**: infection, constipation, delirium, drugs, restricted mobility, atrophic vaginitis, polyuria.
- Assessment: bladder diary, examination (abdomen, PR, pelvic/prostate), urinalysis/MSU, **post-void residual**. Ladder: **behaviour → pelvic floor training and bladder retraining → drugs → containment/surgery.**
- ⚑ **Avoid oxybutynin in the frail or cognitively impaired** — high anticholinergic load, crosses the BBB.
- ⚑ **Overflow diarrhoea from impaction** is the classic trap — liquid stool past a hard plug, mistreated with loperamide.



| Type | History | Mechanism / setting |
|---|---|---|
| Stress | Leak on cough/sneeze/lift; small volumes; no urge | Pelvic floor or sphincter deficiency; parity, post-menopause, post-prostatectomy |
| Urge | Sudden undeferrable urge, larger volumes, nocturia | Detrusor overactivity; idiopathic or neurological (stroke, PD, MS, dementia) |
| Overflow | Dribbling, hesitancy, poor stream, palpable bladder, high PVR | Obstruction (BPH, stricture, constipation) or underactive detrusor (diabetic neuropathy, anticholinergics) |
| Functional | Normal tract; can't reach the toilet in time | Mobility, dexterity, vision, cognition, restraints |
| Mixed | Features of more than one | Common in older women — treat the worse component first |

**Reversible contributors**: symptomatic UTI (not asymptomatic bacteriuria); constipation; delirium; **drugs — diuretics, alpha-blockers, sedatives, opioids, anticholinergics, CCBs (oedema → nocturnal diuresis), SGLT2 inhibitors, caffeine, alcohol**; restricted mobility; atrophic vaginitis; polyuria from hyperglycaemia or hypercalcaemia.

**Assessment**: a *bladder diary* over ~3 days separates polyuria from frequency, and stress from urge. *Post-void residual* by bladder scan after voiding — cut-offs vary locally, but **PVR must be measured before drug treatment of urge incontinence**, since an antimuscarinic in an obstructed bladder precipitates retention.

**Ladder**

1. **Continence-promoting behaviour** — treat constipation, morning-dose diuretics, sensible fluids, less caffeine and alcohol, accessible toilet, raised seat, night commode, lighting, easy clothing. In dementia, **prompted/timed voiding** is the mainstay.
2. **Pelvic floor muscle training** — first-line for stress and mixed incontinence in women (supervised beats a leaflet); effective post-prostatectomy. **Bladder retraining** with progressive deferment is first-line for urge.
3. **Drugs** — antimuscarinics (solifenacin, darifenacin, tolterodine) or the beta-3 agonist mirabegron. ⚑ Prefer low CNS-penetration agents or mirabegron in the old and cognitively impaired; **avoid oxybutynin**, as cumulative anticholinergic exposure is linked to cognitive impairment and dementia risk. **Topical vaginal oestrogen** helps atrophic urgency.
4. **Containment** (pads, pull-ups, condom drainage) — for dignity and skin protection, but after assessment, not instead of it. ⚑ **Indwelling catheters are not a continence treatment**: reserve for retention, sacral pressure injury needing a dry field, or palliative comfort. Surgery: sling/colposuspension, prostate surgery.

**Faecal incontinence**: impaction with overflow; sphincter or pudendal damage; neurological disease; diarrhoeal states; rectal prolapse; functional. PR for tone, squeeze, loading, mass, prolapse, perianal sensation. Manage with disimpaction, then a **planned bowel regimen** (toileting after meals to use the gastrocolic reflex, consistency modified with fibre or loperamide) and barrier skin care.

**Constipation**: drugs (opioids, anticholinergics, iron, CCBs, antacids), immobility, dehydration, low fibre, hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, diabetes, Parkinson's, cord lesions, painful anorectal conditions — and **always exclude colorectal cancer** if new, with bleeding, weight loss or anaemia. Australian ladder: non-drug measures → **bulk-forming (psyllium)**, unsuitable if immobile or poorly hydrated → **osmotic: macrogol first-line** → **stimulant (senna, bisacodyl)**, often with an osmotic → docusate is weak alone → **rectal measures** for impaction. ⚑ **Opioids need a prophylactic aperient prescribed with the opioid** — tolerance does not develop.

## 4.5 Pressure injuries

**The short version**

- Injury to skin and underlying tissue from **sustained pressure, shear and friction** over a bony prominence, amplified by moisture and poor perfusion.
- Risk = immobility + malnutrition + moisture + impaired sensation + poor perfusion + shear + frailty. Screen on admission with a validated tool (Braden, Waterlow, Norton) **plus head-to-toe skin inspection**.
- Stages 1–4 by depth, plus **unstageable** and **deep tissue injury**. Prevention: repositioning, support surfaces, skin and moisture care, nutrition, heel offloading, early mobilisation.
- ⚑ Pressure injury is **HAC 1 on the ACSQHC hospital-acquired complications list** and a named harm in the Comprehensive Care Standard.

| Stage | Essence (described, not reproduced) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Skin intact; localised **non-blanchable** redness over a bony prominence. In darkly pigmented skin look for temperature and induration rather than colour. |
| 2 | **Partial-thickness** dermal loss — shallow wound with a viable pink-red base, or a serum-filled blister. No slough or fat visible. Not for skin tears or moisture damage. |
| 3 | **Full-thickness** loss with subcutaneous fat visible; bone, tendon and muscle **not** exposed. Depth varies by site — shallow at the ear, deep at the sacrum. |
| 4 | Full-thickness loss **exposing bone, tendon, muscle or joint**. Osteomyelitis risk. |
| Unstageable | Full-thickness loss with the base **obscured by slough or eschar**; stageable once visible. **Stable dry adherent eschar on an ischaemic heel is left alone.** |
| Deep tissue injury | **Intact or blistered skin** with persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon or purple discolouration. Damage begins at the bone–muscle interface and may declare rapidly. |

**Sites**: sacrum, heels, occiput, scapulae (supine); greater trochanter, lateral malleolus, ear (side-lying); ischial tuberosities (sitting — chair-bound patients are high risk). **Device-related** injuries take the device's shape — look under masks, probes, collars, casts.

**Prevention**: documented individualised repositioning, 30° tilt not 90° side-lying, slide sheets, head of bed low to limit shear; high-specification foam mattress minimum (alternating-pressure if higher risk) plus a chair cushion; **float heels**; daily skin inspection; barrier creams for incontinence-associated dermatitis; **do not massage reddened prominences**; nutrition; early mobilisation.

**Management**: treat the cause — offload completely, optimise nutrition and perfusion, manage moisture and pain. Match dressing to wound: debride necrotic or sloughy wounds (autolytic hydrogel, enzymatic, or sharp/surgical if extensive or septic); hydrogel or hydrocolloid if dry; foam or alginate/hydrofibre for heavy exudate. Systemic antibiotics only for clinical infection — **do not treat colonisation**. Revisit the plan if no improvement in 2 weeks.

## 4.6 Malnutrition and unintentional weight loss

**The short version**

- Under-recognised: Australian studies find tens of percent of older inpatients and aged care residents malnourished or at risk.
- Screen with **MST** (weight loss + appetite; **≥2 = at risk**) or **MNA/MNA-SF**; a positive screen triggers **dietitian assessment**, not a supplement drink alone.
- Structured "**D**" causes: dentition, dysphagia, dysgeusia, depression, dementia, drugs, disease, diarrhoea, disability, dollars, dislike of the food. Consequences: sarcopenia, falls, impaired healing, infection, delirium, mortality.
- ⚑ **PEG in advanced dementia does not prolong survival, prevent aspiration, or heal or prevent pressure injuries** — careful hand feeding preferred.
- ⚑ Malnutrition is a **hospital-acquired complication in its own right** on the ACSQHC HAC list (as is pressure injury) and is a mandated Comprehensive Care Standard screen.

**Assessment.** MNA-SF adds mobility, acute illness/stress, neuropsychological problems and BMI or calf circumference to intake and weight loss. Full assessment: weight trajectory and % loss, BMI (interpret cautiously — oedema, amputation, vertebral height loss), diet history, grip strength, muscle and fat stores. ⚑ **Albumin measures inflammation, not nutrition** — a normal albumin does not exclude malnutrition.

**Causes by category**: medical (malignancy, heart failure, COPD, CKD, hyperthyroidism, malabsorption, pain); oral (dentures, xerostomia, candidiasis); neurological (post-stroke dysphagia, Parkinson's, feeding apraxia); psychiatric (**depression — leading and treatable**); drugs (digoxin, metformin, SSRIs, opioids); social (can't shop or cook, poverty, isolation).

**Management**: treat the cause; **food first** — fortification (cream, butter, milk powder, oils), small frequent energy-dense meals, preferred food, protected mealtimes, feeding assistance, and **stopping unnecessary therapeutic diets** (a low-salt "diabetic" diet in a malnourished frail resident does net harm); **oral supplements between meals**, not instead of them; dietitian referral for every positive screen. Enteral feeding suits **time-limited reversible** situations (acute stroke, head and neck cancer).

**PEG in advanced dementia** ⚑ — no survival benefit; aspiration continues (oropharyngeal secretions and refluxed feed); no pressure injury or functional benefit. Against real burdens: site infection and leakage, agitation, restraints, diarrhoea, loss of the pleasure and human contact of eating. **Careful hand feeding** is preferred. Difficulty eating in advanced dementia **marks the terminal phase** — the response is a goals-of-care conversation, not a procedure.

**The exam stem — dependent 75-year-old with Alzheimer's in a nursing home, refusing food for two days.** Two days is an **acute change**: a symptom to diagnose, not a nutrition problem to bypass.

1. **Reversible cause first** — delirium and its precipitants (UTI, pneumonia, dehydration, drug change, retention); **pain** (arthritis, unwitnessed fracture, pressure injury); **oral/dental** problems (candidiasis, ulcers, abscess, ill-fitting dentures); **depression**; **new medications**; **impaction**; new dysphagia.
2. **Assess** — collateral history of baseline intake, observe a meal, obs, examine the **mouth**, chest, abdomen, PR, skin; bloods for infection, electrolytes, calcium, glucose; medication chart review.
3. **Treat what you find**, plus feeding assistance, texture modification, preferred foods, oral care and hydration.
4. **Do not jump to NG or PEG.** If nothing reversible is found and this is end-stage dementia, the answer is goals of care with the substitute decision-maker, comfort feeding and palliative care.

**Refeeding syndrome**: refeeding a chronically underfed patient drives insulin-mediated intracellular shift of phosphate, potassium and magnesium. Risk: very low BMI, large weight loss, negligible intake for days, low electrolytes before feeding, alcohol. Features: **hypophosphataemia** (hallmark), hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, thiamine deficiency, fluid overload → arrhythmias, cardiac and respiratory failure, Wernicke's. Prevention: **thiamine before and during early feeding**, start at reduced energy, replace electrolytes daily.

## 4.7 Sensory impairment

**The short version**

- Vision and hearing loss are common, under-detected, and routinely mistaken for cognitive decline.
- Consequences: **falls, delirium, isolation, depression, apparent dementia, medication errors, poor rehab participation.**
- Hearing loss is an established **modifiable dementia risk factor**. The interventions are cheap and unglamorous — glasses on, hearing aids in, wax out, light on.

**Vision**: presbyopia, cataract, AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy. Ask about reading, TV, faces and driving; check acuity **with their usual glasses**. Cataract surgery reduces falls. ⚑ **Multifocal/bifocal lenses increase falls risk** on stairs and kerbs — single-vision distance glasses for outdoor walking is a genuine falls intervention.

**Hearing**: presbycusis (bilateral, high-frequency, worse in noise), **cerumen impaction — check first**, ototoxicity (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, cisplatin). Face the patient, cut background noise, speak lower-pitched rather than louder, use a ward amplifier, refer to audiology, maintain aids — one left in the bedside drawer helps no one. **Dual sensory impairment compounds risk**, and sensory deprivation is a target of delirium prevention.

## 4.8 Immobility and hospital-associated deconditioning

**The short version**

- Bed rest is a **treatment with side effects**, and the dose–response is steep in older people.
- Ten days of strict bed rest in **healthy** older volunteers cost roughly **1 kg of leg lean mass (~1.5 kg whole-body)** — more than young adults lose in 28 days.
- Cascade: immobility → sarcopenia and weakness → falls and dependence → longer stay, institutionalisation, death.
- **Early mobilisation is a clinical intervention**, not a courtesy — it belongs on the ward-round problem list.

| System | Complication of immobility |
|---|---|
| Musculoskeletal | • Muscle atrophy and strength loss • Contractures • Disuse osteoporosis |
| Cardiovascular | • **VTE — DVT and PE** • Orthostatic hypotension • Reduced cardiac reserve |
| Respiratory | • Atelectasis and **hospital-acquired pneumonia** • Poor secretion clearance • Aspiration if fed supine |
| Skin / GI / GU | • Pressure injuries • Constipation and impaction • Retention, incontinence, CAUTI |
| Neuro / metabolic | • Delirium • Depression, apathy, learned dependence • Insulin resistance, negative nitrogen balance, anorexia |

**What to do**: mobilise on day one unless genuinely contraindicated; sit out for meals; walk to the toilet rather than use a bottle or pan; **remove tethers** (IV lines, catheters, telemetry, oxygen tubing) early — every attachment is a mobility restraint. Early physiotherapy with a documented mobility plan. Treat pain, delirium and postural hypotension: they are the reasons people won't move. Footwear and aids on the ward; avoid restraints; VTE prophylaxis. ⚑ Australian programmes such as Eat Walk Engage sit within the Comprehensive Care Standard framework.

## 4.9 Elder abuse — recognition and response

**The short version**

- A single or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, **within a relationship of expected trust**, causing harm or distress to an older person.
- Forms: **psychological, financial, neglect, physical, sexual** — often more than one at once.
- ⚑ AIFS National Elder Abuse Prevalence Study (2021): **~15% of community-dwelling Australians 65+ reported abuse in the past year; psychological commonest (~12%)**, then neglect (~3%), financial (~2%), physical (~2%), sexual (~1%). **Adult children are the largest perpetrator group** and dominate financial abuse. Texts calling financial abuse commonest describe **helpline caseloads**, not prevalence.
- Ask **alone, directly and routinely** when risk features are present. **1800 ELDERHelp — 1800 353 374**; aged care providers also report under **SIRS**.

| Form | Presentation clues |
|---|---|
| Psychological | • Fearfulness, withdrawal, deference to a companion who answers for them • Threats of being "put in a home" • Isolation |
| Financial | • Can't pay for care despite apparent means • Sudden change of will or power of attorney • Missing possessions • A relative controlling the money who resists services (they cost money and bring witnesses) |
| Neglect | • Pressure injuries, dehydration, malnutrition, poor hygiene • Untreated problems, medications not given |
| Physical | • Injury inconsistent with the explanation • Bruises in unusual sites (inner arms, trunk, neck, ears) • Injuries of different ages • Delayed presentation |
| Sexual | • Genital/anal injury, bleeding, STI • New distress with personal care |

**Risk factors** — victim: cognitive impairment, dependence, isolation, advanced age, prior family violence. Perpetrator: carer stress, financial dependence on the older person, substance misuse, shared housing, inheritance entitlement. Systemic: understaffed residential care, ageism.

**How to ask**: see them **alone**, on a plausible pretext; normalise ("we ask everyone about safety at home") then be specific — do you feel safe at home; does anyone use your money without asking; has anyone hurt or threatened you. Professional interpreter, **never a family member**.

**Capacity and undue influence.** Distinguish a **capable person making an unwise choice** (their right) from one produced by **impaired capacity or undue influence**. Capacity is decision- and time-specific and presumed until shown otherwise; the threshold rises with the gravity of the transaction. Undue influence is about **process** — isolation from independent advice, dependency on the beneficiary, secrecy and urgency, a decision inconsistent with known values. Check whether an enduring power of attorney exists and whether the attorney acts within scope; involve the public advocate or state tribunal (NCAT, VCAT).

**Response pathway**: treat injuries, assess capacity and risk, admit if there is no safe discharge → social work, geriatrician, GP (carer stress may be the driver; respite may be the intervention) → **1800 ELDERHelp (1800 353 374)** for state/territory helplines → **police** for assault, fraud or theft → **guardianship tribunal** if capacity impaired → **SIRS**: approved aged care providers report eight incident types (unreasonable force; sexual misconduct; psychological or emotional abuse; unexpected death; staff theft or financial coercion; neglect; inappropriate restrictive practices; unexplained absence) to the ACQSC — **Priority 1 within 24 h, Priority 2 within 30 days**. ⚑ Clinician mandatory reporting is **not uniform across Australian jurisdictions**, but professional duties, SIRS obligations and criminal law still apply.
