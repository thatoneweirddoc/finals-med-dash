## Falls, Mobility and Bone Health


### Geriatrics Master Notes — Falls, Mobility and Bone Health

*Sources: ACSQHC Hip Fracture Clinical Care Standard (2023) and Preventing Falls guidelines (2025); RACGP/Healthy Bones Australia osteoporosis guideline (2024) and HBA position statement (2025); World Falls Guidelines (2022); Cochrane exercise-for-falls review (2019); MBS, PBS, Therapeutic Guidelines, AMH, ANZ Hip Fracture Registry. Scope: falls assessment and prevention, mobility, osteoporosis diagnosis and drug therapy, hip and fragility fracture care, sarcopenia.*

#### 2.1 Falls — the multifactorial model

**The short version**
- A fall = unintentional coming to rest on the ground, not from overwhelming external force, syncope or seizure — but the definition is imperfect and unexplained falls often *are* syncope.
- ~30% of community-dwellers >65 and ~50% of those >80 fall each year; higher in RACFs and hospital.
- Falls are almost never mono-causal — risk rises steeply with the *number* of risk factors.
- Strongest predictors: previous fall, gait/balance impairment, muscle weakness, polypharmacy (especially psychotropics), visual and cognitive impairment.
- Consequences drive the exam answer: fracture, fear of falling, deconditioning, loss of independence, institutionalisation, excess mortality after hip fracture.

Name all three buckets:

| Bucket | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | • Gait/balance disorder, sarcopenia, lower-limb weakness<br>• Neuropathy, parkinsonism, prior CVA, vestibular disease/BPPV<br>• Visual impairment — cataract, AMD, glaucoma, multifocals<br>• Cognitive impairment, delirium<br>• Orthostatic/postprandial hypotension, arrhythmia, aortic stenosis<br>• Foot pathology, OA, chronic pain, urinary urgency<br>• Vitamin D deficiency, anaemia, hypoglycaemia, alcohol |
| **Extrinsic** | • Rugs, cords, clutter, poor lighting, no stair rails<br>• Wet floors, uneven paths, unfamiliar ward<br>• Worn/backless footwear; wrong walking aid; low toilet seat |
| **Situational** | • Rushing to phone or toilet, nocturia in the dark<br>• Carrying objects, dual-tasking, climbing on chairs<br>• Alcohol; risk-taking beyond capacity |

**Consequences worth naming**
- ~5% of falls cause fracture — hip, wrist (Colles), proximal humerus, pubic rami, vertebral. Subdural haematoma is the other serious outcome; suspect it if anticoagulated or new cognitive change.
- **Fear of falling** affects up to half of fallers, including after non-injurious falls: activity restriction → deconditioning → more falls. A treatable target.
- **Long lie** (>1 h down, typically living alone) → dehydration, hypothermia, **rhabdomyolysis with AKI**, **pressure injury**, aspiration. Independently predicts death and institutionalisation. Always ask "could you get up?" — it changes the discharge plan (alarm, home care, floor-rise training).
- Hip fracture: ~a quarter die within 12 months, many never regain pre-fracture function, and a substantial proportion admitted from home move into residential care.

#### 2.2 The falls history and examination

**The short version**
- Take the history as *before / during / after* — this is what separates a mechanical fall from syncope.
- Always seek a **witness account**; the patient's own account of unwitnessed LOC is unreliable.
- Screening triad (World Falls Guidelines): any fall in the past 12 months, gait/balance difficulty, fear of falling.
- Examination minimum: **lying and standing BP**, gait and balance (**TUG**), cardiovascular, neurological, MSK/feet, vision, cognition. Investigations targeted, not routine; ECG in essentially all.

**History — the structure to write down**

| Phase | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Before** | • Doing what? Standing up, turning, reaching up, coughing, micturating?<br>• Warning — dizziness, palpitations, chest pain, aura, focal symptoms?<br>• Time of day, footwear, lighting, aid used or left behind |
| **During** | • Lost consciousness? Witness account of colour, tone, jerking, eyes open/shut<br>• Hands put out? (preserved protective reflexes → mechanical trip) |
| **After** | • Could you get up? How long down? Who found you?<br>• Immediate recovery vs prolonged confusion (post-ictal), incontinence, tongue bite, injuries |

Then the modifiable-factor sweep: **medications** (incl. OTC, PRN sedatives, recent changes), **alcohol**, **footwear**, **vision** (multifocals, cataract), **home hazards** (stairs, bathroom, night route to toilet), **continence**, **cognition and mood**, **function/ADLs**, supports, and whether this is one of a cluster.

**Examination**

- **Lying and standing BP.** ⚑ Supine after ≥5 min rest, then at **1 and 3 minutes standing** — a single immediate reading misses delayed OH. **Orthostatic hypotension** = fall in **systolic ≥20 mmHg** or **diastolic ≥10 mmHg** within **3 minutes** of standing (or head-up tilt); in known supine hypertension, **systolic fall ≥30 mmHg**. Note symptoms and pulse response — flat pulse suggests autonomic failure or beta-blockade, a rise suggests volume depletion.
- **Gait and balance.** **Timed Up and Go (TUG)**: rise from a standard armchair, walk 3 m, turn, return, sit. **≥12 s** flags impaired mobility. ⚑ TUG is a poor stand-alone predictor of future falls; use it to describe and track mobility, not to rule falls in or out. Watch *how*: pushing off the chair (proximal weakness), festination, freezing at the turn, wide base, retropulsion.
- **Five-times sit-to-stand** without arms — lower-limb power proxy. **Berg Balance Scale** (14 items) is the fuller physio measure. Gait speed <0.8 m/s marks slowness (also a sarcopenia/frailty criterion).
- **Neurological**: tone/power/reflexes, cerebellar signs, proprioception and vibration (neuropathy, B12), visual fields, Romberg, parkinsonian features, cognition.
- **Cardiovascular**: rate, rhythm, murmurs (**ESM to carotids with slow-rising pulse → aortic stenosis**).
- **MSK and feet**: joint range, leg length, quadriceps bulk — and *take the shoes and socks off*: long nails, ulcers, deformity, sensation, and the footwear itself.
- **Vision**: acuity with usual glasses; multifocals worn outdoors.

#### 2.3 Drugs that cause falls

**The short version**
- Psychotropics (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, antipsychotics, antidepressants) and cardiovascular agents dominate.
- Risk is dose-related and cumulative — the **number** of falls-risk-increasing drugs matters as much as which.
- **Deprescribing is one of the few interventions with real evidence**, and it is free — always include it.
- ⚑ **Anticoagulants do not cause falls** — they change the consequences. "High falls risk" alone is not a reason to withhold anticoagulation in AF; stroke reduction almost always outweighs bleeding risk.

| Class | Mechanism of fall risk |
|---|---|
| Antihypertensives (ACEI/ARB, CCB, beta-blocker) | Hypotension, orthostatic drop, bradycardia; risk peaks after initiation/uptitration |
| Diuretics | Volume depletion, orthostasis, urgency/nocturia → rushed toilet trips, hyponatraemia |
| Alpha-blockers (prazosin, tamsulosin) and nitrates | First-dose/postural hypotension; venodilation → preload drop → syncope |
| Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs | Sedation, next-day hangover, ataxia, impaired postural reflexes; long-acting worst |
| Antipsychotics | Sedation, orthostasis (alpha-blockade), drug-induced parkinsonism, gait freezing |
| Antidepressants — TCAs *and* SSRIs/SNRIs | TCAs anticholinergic/sedating/orthostatic. ⚑ SSRIs are **not** falls-safe — ↑falls and fracture, plus hyponatraemia (SIADH) |
| Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, promethazine, benztropine) | Delirium, blurred vision, sedation; cumulative **anticholinergic burden** |
| Opioids | Sedation, delirium, hypotension, ataxia; worst at initiation and dose increase |
| Antiepileptics (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, gabapentinoids) | Sedation, ataxia, nystagmus, hyponatraemia; ↓BMD long-term |
| Hypoglycaemics (insulin, sulfonylureas) | Hypoglycaemia → collapse; over-tight HbA1c targets in the frail are a classic trap |
| Alcohol | Sedation, ataxia; interacts with all of the above, under-reported |

Use a structured tool (STOPP/START, Beers), but the exam-safe answer is: *review every drug against its current indication, stop what has none, wean sedatives, recheck postural BP after each change.*

#### 2.4 Falls vs syncope vs seizure

**The short version**
- ⚑ **An unexplained fall in an older person may be syncope the patient does not recall** — retrograde amnesia for LOC is common. Treat "found on floor, no memory" as syncope until proven otherwise.
- The discriminator is the *narrative*: trigger, prodrome, duration, recovery, witness account.
- **ECG in everyone**; echocardiogram if murmur, structural disease, exertional symptoms or abnormal ECG.
- Red flags for cardiac syncope: no prodrome, exertional or supine onset, palpitations, family history of sudden death.

| Feature | Mechanical fall | Syncope | Seizure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Trip, slip, turn, reaching | Standing, pain, micturition, cough, exertion — or nil | Often nil; sleep deprivation, alcohol withdrawal |
| Prodrome | Nil ("legs gave way") | Lightheaded, greying vision, nausea, sweating; **absent in arrhythmia** | Aura — epigastric rising, déjà vu, olfactory |
| Consciousness / duration | Preserved | Lost, seconds | Lost, 1–3 min |
| Appearance | — | Pale, clammy, limp; brief myoclonic jerks possible | Cyanosed, rigid then rhythmic clonic, eyes open |
| Tongue bite / incontinence | No / no | No (rarely tip) / occasionally | **Lateral** bite is specific / common but non-specific |
| Recovery | Immediate | Oriented within seconds–minutes | **Post-ictal confusion for minutes–hours**, headache, Todd's paresis |
| Injury pattern | Hands/knees/forehead (protective) | Facial injury (no protective reflex) | Posterior shoulder dislocation, vertebral fracture |

**Cardiac causes not to miss**
- **Arrhythmia** — sick sinus, complete heart block, paroxysmal AF with pauses, VT. Abrupt, no prodrome, rapid full recovery ("Stokes–Adams"). Resting ECG may be normal → Holter, patch monitor, then **implantable loop recorder** for infrequent unexplained syncope.
- **Aortic stenosis** — exertional syncope, angina, dyspnoea; slow-rising pulse, ESM to carotids, soft A2 → **echocardiogram**. Also HOCM, severe LV dysfunction, PE, tamponade, dissection.
- **Carotid sinus hypersensitivity** — syncope/falls on head turning, tight collars, shaving. Carotid sinus massage with continuous ECG and BP: **asystole ≥3 s** (cardioinhibitory) and/or **systolic fall ≥50 mmHg** (vasodepressor); mixed forms occur. ⚑ Do **not** massage if carotid bruit, or stroke/TIA/MI within 3 months. Dual-chamber pacing only for the **cardioinhibitory** type with recurrent syncope.

Baseline workup: ECG, postural BP, FBC, U&E, Ca, glucose, TSH, 25(OH)D, B12; CT head if head strike on anticoagulant, focal signs or new cognitive change.

#### 2.5 Falls prevention — what actually works

**The short version**
- **Exercise is the best-evidenced intervention** — must challenge balance, be ongoing, adequate dose (~3 h/week).
- **Multifactorial assessment + tailored intervention** in high-risk people (previous injurious fall, gait/balance problem, multiple risk factors).
- Also: medication review, first-eye cataract surgery, vitamin D **in the deficient**, podiatry/footwear, OT home hazard assessment, pacing in cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome.
- ⚑ Blanket vitamin D does **not** prevent falls; bed rails, restraints and generic risk scores do not prevent hospital falls.

**Exercise — the detail that gains marks.** Cochrane (Sherrington 2019): exercise cuts the *rate* of falls by ~**23%**; balance/functional training ~**24%**; multiple types combined (balance + resistance) ~**34%**; Tai Chi ~**19%**. Walking, dance or resistance alone — uncertain. Prescription: **progressively challenging balance work, ≥3 h/week, indefinitely** (benefit lost when it stops), via physio or exercise physiologist (e.g. Otago programme).

| Intervention | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Balance-challenging exercise, adequate dose | Strong — the cornerstone |
| Multifactorial assessment + tailored intervention in high-risk | Supported; depends on interventions actually being delivered |
| Medication review / psychotropic withdrawal | Supported; the effect is in *stopping* |
| Home hazard assessment by OT | Effective in **high-risk** only (recent fall, poor vision, recent discharge) |
| First-eye cataract surgery | Reduces falls; expedite |
| Vitamin D | Only if 25(OH)D <50 nmol/L |
| Podiatry, foot care, supportive footwear | Modest benefit, low harm |
| Pacing | Only cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome / documented bradyarrhythmia |
| Single-vision lenses outdoors | Helps active multifocal wearers |
| Hip protectors | Reduce hip fracture in high-risk RACF residents; adherence limits |
| Education alone; vitamin D in the replete or by bolus | Ineffective; bolus dosing potentially harmful |
| Bed rails, restraints, chair alarms, risk scores | Not effective; rails and restraints cause harm |

**Hospital inpatient falls.** ⚑ Australian guidance now de-emphasises numerical **falls risk scores** — treat every older inpatient as at risk and act on *modifiable* factors: delirium screening, orientation, glasses/hearing aids and call bell in reach, toileting plan, sedative review, proper footwear, correct bed height, aid at the bedside, uncluttered space, overnight lighting, supervised mobilisation. Post-fall: assess for injury (occult head injury if anticoagulated), neuro obs, identify the cause, revise the plan.

**Bed rails and restraints are not the answer** — full-length rails turn a low fall into a fall from height and cause entrapment; restraint (physical or chemical) increases delirium, deconditioning, pressure injury and mortality, and is a restricted practice under aged-care legislation. If someone is climbing out of bed, find and treat the reason (pain, retention, delirium, toileting).

#### 2.6 Osteoporosis — diagnosis

**The short version**
- Osteoporosis = reduced bone mass + microarchitectural deterioration → fragility and fracture.
- **DXA T-score** (lumbar spine, femoral neck/total hip; vs young adult peak): **normal ≥ −1.0**, **osteopenia −1.0 to −2.5**, **osteoporosis ≤ −2.5**.
- ⚑ **Z-score** (age-matched) for premenopausal women, men <50, children; ≤ −2.0 = below expected range for age → hunt secondary causes.
- ⚑ **A minimal-trauma fracture in an adult >50 is diagnostic of osteoporosis regardless of T-score** — treat, don't wait for the number.
- ⚑ **Medicare funds screening DXA from age 70**; before that it is case-finding on risk factors or prior fracture.

**Minimal-trauma fracture** = fracture from a fall from standing height or less, or no identifiable trauma. Skull, face, hands, feet and pathological fractures excluded; vertebral and hip sites carry the highest subsequent risk.

**Who to assess:** all adults **≥70** (MBS items 12320/12322; rescreen ~5-yearly if T ≥ −1.5, ~2-yearly if −1.5 to −2.5); anyone **>50 with a minimal-trauma fracture**; any age with a major risk factor — **glucocorticoids ≥7.5 mg prednisolone daily for ≥3 months**, hypogonadism (incl. androgen deprivation, aromatase inhibitors), primary hyperparathyroidism, coeliac/malabsorption, CKD or liver disease, RA, thyrotoxicosis, anorexia nervosa, transplantation, long-term antiepileptics.

**Baseline bloods for secondary causes:** **FBC, ESR/CRP, U&E/eGFR, LFT, calcium, phosphate, 25(OH)D, PTH, TSH**. Then as indicated: testosterone (men), coeliac serology, SPEP and free light chains (myeloma), 24-h urinary calcium, cortisol screen. Review the drug chart (glucocorticoids, PPIs, SSRIs, antiepileptics, androgen/oestrogen deprivation).

**Risk calculators.** **FRAX** — 10-year probability of major osteoporotic and hip fracture, ± BMD (Australian version). **Garvan** — Australian, uniquely includes **number of prior falls**; 5- and 10-year risk. Use in the osteopenic range: treat if T ≤ −2.5, **or** T −1.5 to −2.5 with **FRAX major osteoporotic ≥20% or hip ≥3%**. ⚑ Neither handles glucocorticoid dose well and FRAX ignores falls — judgement overrides.

⚑ **Vertebral fractures are often asymptomatic.** Height loss >3 cm, new thoracic kyphosis, or an incidental "wedge" on CXR/CT should trigger lateral thoracolumbar imaging or DXA vertebral fracture assessment.

#### 2.7 Osteoporosis management

**The short version**
- Non-drug foundation: calcium (diet first), vitamin D if deficient, weight-bearing + resistance + balance exercise, smoking cessation, alcohol limits, falls prevention.
- First line: **oral bisphosphonate**; **IV zoledronic acid** if intolerant/non-adherent; **denosumab**, especially in renal impairment.
- ⚑ **Denosumab must never simply be stopped** — cessation causes rapid bone loss and **rebound vertebral fractures**; always follow with a bisphosphonate.
- Anabolics (teriparatide, romosozumab) for very high risk — PBS-restricted, **must be followed by an antiresorptive**.
- PBS funds treatment after any **minimal-trauma fracture**, and for people **≥70 with T ≤ −2.5**.

**Non-pharmacological**

| Element | Australian recommendation |
|---|---|
| Calcium | **1000 mg/day** adults; **1300 mg/day** women >50 and men >70. **Diet first**; if inadequate, supplement **500–600 mg/day** only |
| Vitamin D | Target 25(OH)D **>50 nmol/L**; if low, **800–1000 IU/day**. ⚑ Avoid high-dose annual/bolus regimens (↑falls) |
| Exercise | **Weight-bearing impact + progressive resistance + challenging balance**, supervised and **ongoing** |
| Lifestyle | Smoking cessation; alcohol ≤2 standard drinks/day; BMI >20; protein ~1.0–1.5 g/kg/day |

**Bisphosphonates**
- **Alendronate 70 mg weekly PO**; **risedronate 35 mg weekly** (enteric-coated form may be taken with food); **zoledronic acid 5 mg IV over ≥15 min every 12 months**.
- ⚑ **Oral administration rules** — on waking, fasting, with a **full glass of plain water**, then **upright and nil by mouth ≥30 min**. Non-adherence explains lost efficacy and oesophagitis.
- Cautions: **eGFR <35 mL/min** (zoledronic acid; avoid orals below ~30), oesophageal stricture, inability to sit upright, hypocalcaemia — **replete calcium and vitamin D first**.
- Adverse effects: oesophagitis/dyspepsia; **acute-phase reaction** after the first zoledronic acid infusion (flu-like myalgia/fever 1–3 days, ~30%); hypocalcaemia; rarely uveitis.
- **Osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ)** — exposed necrotic jaw bone >8 weeks. **Rare at osteoporosis doses** (~1 in 10,000–100,000 patient-years; far commoner at oncology doses). Risk ↑ with >4 years' use, invasive dental work, smoking, diabetes, steroids. **Dental review and complete invasive work before starting**; do not interrupt therapy for a clean or filling.
- **Atypical femoral fracture** — transverse subtrochanteric/shaft fracture with cortical beaking, preceded by weeks of **prodromal thigh or groin pain**. Rare (~1 per 10,000 patient-years, rising after 5 years). New thigh pain on long-term antiresorptive → **image both femurs**.
- **Drug holiday** — ⚑ the Australian guideline (RACGP/Healthy Bones Australia 2024) says **reconsider the need to continue after 5–10 years**; the commonly taught 5 years oral / 3 years IV sits inside that. If no recent fracture and T now > −2.5, a 2–3 year break with review is reasonable; continue if T ≤ −2.5, prior hip/vertebral fracture or ongoing glucocorticoids. ⚑ Applies to **bisphosphonates only**.

**Denosumab**
- RANKL monoclonal antibody, **60 mg SC every 6 months**. No renal dose adjustment (usable when eGFR precludes bisphosphonates) but **hypocalcaemia risk is high in advanced CKD** — check calcium, replete vitamin D, recheck after dosing.
- ⚑⚑ **High-yield: denosumab is not retained in bone. Delay or cessation causes a rebound surge in resorption, rapid loss of all accrued BMD, and multiple painful vertebral fractures — <3%, concentrated in those treated >3 years.** Keep the **6-monthly interval rigorously** — the Australian guideline says flatly that denosumab **should not be interrupted**, so treat a delayed dose as urgent rather than as having a grace period. If it must stop, **transition to bisphosphonate therapy for a minimum of 12 months**, starting when the next denosumab dose was due. Never let it lapse through admission, GP change or dental work.

**Anabolic agents**
- **Teriparatide** (PTH 1-34) 20 microgram SC daily, **max 18 months lifetime**. PBS very-high-risk criterion is narrower than often quoted: **T ≤ −3.0 PLUS at least two minimal-trauma fractures, one of them after ≥12 months of continuous antiresorptive therapy**. ⚑ Must be **followed immediately by an antiresorptive**.
- **Romosozumab** 210 mg SC monthly for **12 months**, then antiresorptive. PBS-restricted to very high risk. ⚑ **Avoid within 12 months of MI or stroke** (excess CV events, ARCH).
- Also: raloxifene (vertebral only, ↑VTE), MHT (early postmenopause), testosterone if hypogonadal.

**PBS in one line:** subsidised after any established **minimal-trauma fracture**, and for people **aged ≥70 with T ≤ −2.5** (≤ −3.0 for some agents), plus corticosteroid indications — thresholds differ by drug.

**Duration and review:** generally long-term. Review adherence, calcium/vitamin D and falls annually; DXA at 1–2 years then ~2-yearly. A fracture **on** treatment means check adherence and technique, re-screen for secondary causes, then escalate — not "stop the drug".


#### 2.8 Hip fracture and fragility fracture care

**The short version**
- Classic presentation: **shortened and externally rotated leg** (unopposed iliopsoas pull on the lesser trochanter — it is not characteristically abducted), groin/hip pain, unable to weight-bear.
- **Intracapsular vs extracapsular** determines the operation — the retinacular blood supply to the head runs up the neck.
- ACSQHC standard: analgesia at presentation, **surgery within 48 h**, orthogeriatric co-management, **mobilisation day 1 without weight-bearing restriction**, delirium prevention, **bone health + falls assessment before discharge**.
- ⚑ Only ~a third of Australian hip fracture patients leave hospital on bone-protection medication — say you started it.
- **Fracture liaison services** capture the fragility fracture patient; wrist and vertebral fractures are the missed sentinel events.

**Imaging.** AP pelvis + lateral hip. Normal X-ray but unable to weight-bear does **not** exclude fracture — MRI (best) or CT.

| Type | Blood supply | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| **Intracapsular** (subcapital/transcervical), undisplaced | Retinacular vessels may be intact | Internal fixation (cannulated screws/DHS) |
| **Intracapsular**, displaced | Retinacular vessels disrupted → **AVN and non-union** | **Hemiarthroplasty**; **THR** if previously independent, mobile, cognitively intact |
| **Extracapsular — intertrochanteric** | Preserved, heals well | **Dynamic hip screw** |
| **Extracapsular — subtrochanteric/reverse oblique** | Preserved but mechanically unstable | **Intramedullary nail** |

**Clinical Care Standard — the seven principles, and your management plan**
1. **Care at presentation** — prompt assessment, imaging, admission under an agreed pathway; preoperative cognitive assessment (4AT).
2. **Pain management** — assess at presentation and regularly; multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia including a **regional nerve block (fascia iliaca or femoral)**. Avoid NSAIDs. Good analgesia prevents delirium.
3. **Orthogeriatric model of care** from admission, not a rescue consult.
4. **Timely surgery** — within **48 hours** absent contraindication; delay only for reversible medical problems, not routine echocardiograms or "medical clearance".
5. **Mobilisation day 1 post-op, no weight-bearing restrictions**, daily thereafter; early nutrition, remove IDC early.
6. **Minimising further fracture** — falls **and** bone health assessment before discharge with a written plan; start calcium/vitamin D and an antiresorptive (zoledronic acid is convenient post-operatively once replete).
7. **Transition from hospital** — individualised plan, rehabilitation, communication to GP and carer.

**Delirium prevention** — the hospital's highest-risk group. Analgesia, avoid benzodiazepines and anticholinergics, oxygenation, hydration, treat constipation and retention, early catheter removal, orientation, sensory aids, early mobilisation; screen with 4AT.

**Fracture liaison services (FLS)** — a coordinator systematically identifies every patient >50 with a fragility fracture, investigates, initiates treatment and falls assessment, and hands over to the GP. ⚑ **Wrist (Colles), proximal humerus and vertebral fractures are the sentinel events routinely ignored** — a Colles fracture in a 62-year-old woman is an osteoporosis diagnosis waiting to be made.

#### 2.9 Sarcopenia, deconditioning and immobility

**The short version**
- **Sarcopenia** = loss of skeletal muscle **mass plus strength/function**; low **grip strength** screens, low muscle mass confirms, **gait speed <0.8 m/s** marks severity.
- Primary (age-related) vs secondary (inactivity, low protein intake, inflammatory disease, cancer, organ failure, glucocorticoids).
- Consequences: falls, fractures, functional decline, longer admissions, delirium, institutionalisation, mortality.
- Hospital **deconditioning** is fast — measurable muscle loss within days of bed rest, worse in the old. "Bed rest" carries harms: pressure injury, VTE, constipation, atelectasis, orthostatic intolerance, delirium.
- ⚑ **Treatment is progressive resistance exercise plus adequate protein** (~1.0–1.5 g/kg/day) — no effective drug exists. Also treat vitamin D deficiency, review catabolic drugs, address dysphagia, dentition and social causes of poor intake.
- Inpatient answer: mobilise from day one, dressed and out of bed for meals, avoid tethering lines, dietitian review, physio-led resistance training continued post-discharge.
