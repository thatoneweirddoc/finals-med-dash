## Physiology of Pregnancy and the Menstrual Cycle


### O&G Master Notes — Physiology of Pregnancy and the Menstrual Cycle

*Australian finals level. Anchored to RANZCOG statements, Queensland Clinical Guidelines, Australian Pregnancy Care Guidelines, and standard physiology reference (StatPearls, GLOWM, Deranged Physiology). Numbers are consensus ranges — where sources genuinely differ this is flagged. Scope: normal physiology and the clinical traps it creates, not pathology (covered in later sections).*

#### 1.1 The HPO axis

GnRH is released in **pulses** from the arcuate nucleus. The pulse *frequency* — not the amount — determines which gonadotrophin dominates: **slow pulses favour FSH, fast pulses favour LH**. Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH exposure downregulates pituitary receptors and shuts the axis down entirely — this is the mechanism behind GnRH agonists in endometriosis and fibroids, and explains the initial "flare" (1–2 weeks of stimulation) before suppression.

Ovarian steroidogenesis is the **two-cell, two-gonadotrophin** system: theca cells respond to LH and make androgens (androstenedione, testosterone); granulosa cells respond to FSH and **aromatise** those androgens to oestradiol. Granulosa cells also make **inhibin B** (follicular phase, suppresses FSH) and **inhibin A** (luteal phase), and **AMH** (from small antral follicles — hence AMH as an ovarian reserve marker, cycle-independent).

Feedback is dose- and duration-dependent, which is the single most counterintuitive point in the cycle: **oestradiol is normally inhibitory, but sustained high oestradiol (roughly >700–1100 pmol/L for ≥36–48 h) flips to positive feedback** and triggers the LH surge. Progesterone is inhibitory throughout (basis of combined and progestogen-only contraception).

**The short version**
- GnRH pulse frequency selects FSH vs LH; continuous GnRH = shutdown.
- Theca makes androgen (LH-driven); granulosa aromatises it to oestradiol (FSH-driven).
- Low/moderate E2 = negative feedback; sustained high E2 = positive feedback = LH surge.

#### 1.2 The menstrual cycle

| Phase | Days (28-d cycle) | Dominant hormone | Ovary | Endometrium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Menstrual | 1–4 | all low | follicular recruitment begins | shedding of functionalis |
| Follicular / proliferative | 1–13 (**variable length**) | oestradiol (rising) | cohort recruited → dominant follicle selected ~day 7 | glands straight, stroma compact, mitoses; regenerates to 8–10 mm |
| Ovulation | ~14 | LH surge | oocyte released ~36 h after surge onset | peak thickness, triple-line on USS |
| Luteal / secretory | 15–28 (**fixed 14 ± 2 d**) | progesterone | corpus luteum | tortuous glands, subnuclear vacuoles (d16–17), stromal oedema → predecidualisation |

**The short version**
- **Follicular phase length is variable; the luteal phase is fixed at ~14 days.** All cycle-length variation is follicular. This is why a "day 21 progesterone" must actually be timed **7 days before the expected period**, not literally day 21 — in a 35-day cycle you test on day 28.
- Ovulation is **~36 h after LH surge onset, ~10–12 h after LH peak** — the basis of LH urine kits and IVF trigger timing.
- Dominant follicle selection: rising E2 suppresses FSH; only the follicle with the most FSH receptors survives the falling FSH. Give exogenous FSH and you override selection — hence multifollicular development in ovulation induction.
- Mid-luteal progesterone **>30 nmol/L** ≈ ovulation occurred.

Corpus luteum lifespan is ~14 days unless **rescued by hCG** from the implanting trophoblast (hCG binds the LH receptor). No hCG → luteolysis → progesterone withdrawal → spiral artery vasoconstriction, prostaglandin and MMP release, functionalis breakdown → menses.

**Clue → answer.** Thin, clear, stretchy cervical mucus with spinnbarkeit and ferning = **peri-ovulatory (oestrogen)**. Thick, scant, tacky mucus = **luteal (progesterone)** — and the mechanism the POP exploits. A **0.3–0.5 °C sustained basal temperature rise** = progesterone thermogenesis, i.e. ovulation has already happened (retrospective, useless for timing intercourse prospectively).

#### 1.3 Fertilisation, implantation and placentation

Fertilisation occurs in the **ampulla** of the tube. Morula by day 3, blastocyst day 5, hatching from zona then **implantation days 6–10 post-ovulation**, typically upper posterior uterine wall. Syncytiotrophoblast erodes decidua and secretes **hCG**, detectable in serum ~day 8–10 post-ovulation (i.e. before a missed period).

hCG kinetics matter for early pregnancy problems: in a viable intrauterine pregnancy hCG **rises by at least ~50–66% over 48 h**, peaks at **8–10 weeks (~100,000 IU/L)**, then falls to a plateau by ~20 weeks. Suboptimal rise → ectopic or failing pregnancy; a fall of <15% in 48 h is also abnormal.

**Trophoblast invasion occurs in two waves** — decidual spiral artery segments at ~8–10 weeks, myometrial segments at ~16–18 weeks. Invasion strips the spiral arteries of smooth muscle and endothelium, converting high-resistance, vasoactive vessels into flaccid, high-capacitance conduits delivering ~500–750 mL/min at term. **Failure of the second wave is the root lesion of pre-eclampsia and placental FGR**, and is why uterine artery Doppler notching/high PI at 20–24 weeks predicts both.

**Luteoplacental shift:** the corpus luteum supplies progesterone until the placenta takes over at **7–9 weeks**. Removing a corpus luteum (e.g. ovarian cystectomy) before ~8 weeks therefore requires progesterone support; after 10 weeks it does not.

**hPL** (human placental lactogen) rises steadily through pregnancy and is powerfully **anti-insulin and lipolytic** — the reason gestational diabetes is a *second half* of pregnancy disease, and the reason insulin requirements in pre-existing T1DM climb steeply from ~20 weeks while hypoglycaemia risk is highest in the first trimester.

#### 1.4 Cardiovascular adaptation

| Parameter | Change | Timing / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiac output | **↑ 30–50%** (≈5 → 7 L/min) | rises from 5 weeks, plateau by ~24 weeks |
| Stroke volume | ↑ ~25–30% | dominant early contributor |
| Heart rate | ↑ 10–20 bpm | dominant late contributor |
| SVR | **↓ 25–40%** | progesterone, NO, prostacyclin, relaxin |
| Blood pressure | ↓, **nadir 20–24 weeks**, back to baseline by term | |
| Blood volume | ↑ ~40–50% (~1.2–1.5 L) | |
| Immediate postpartum CO | **further ↑ 60–80%** | autotransfusion from contracting uterus + relief of caval compression |

**The short version**
- The BP nadir at 20–24 weeks **masks chronic hypertension**: a booking BP of 118/72 taken at 22 weeks in a woman with pre-existing hypertension looks normal. Always seek a pre-pregnancy or <20-week reading.
- **Aortocaval compression from ~20 weeks** — supine hypotension. Left lateral tilt 15–30° for resuscitation, transfer and CTG.
- The most dangerous haemodynamic moment for a woman with severe mitral stenosis or pulmonary hypertension is **immediately after delivery**, not during labour.

Physiological (do not over-investigate): ejection systolic flow murmur (up to 90%), S3, ectopics, laterally displaced apex, mild peripheral oedema, ECG left axis deviation, T-wave inversion in III/V1–V3, small Q in III. **A diastolic murmur is never physiological.**

#### 1.5 Respiratory adaptation

Progesterone directly stimulates the central respiratory centre → **tidal volume ↑ 30–50%** with essentially **unchanged respiratory rate** → minute ventilation ↑ 30–50%. Mechanically, the diaphragm rises ~4 cm and the subcostal angle widens.

| Parameter | Change |
|---|---|
| Tidal volume | ↑ 30–50% |
| Minute ventilation | ↑ 30–50% |
| Respiratory rate | unchanged |
| FRC, RV, ERV | **↓ ~20–25%** |
| VC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC | **unchanged** |
| O₂ consumption | ↑ 20–30% |
| PaCO₂ | **↓ to ~28–32 mmHg** |
| HCO₃⁻ | ↓ to ~18–22 mmol/L (renal compensation) |
| pH | 7.40–7.47 (mild compensated respiratory alkalosis) |

**The short version**
- **A PaCO₂ of 40 mmHg in a breathless pregnant asthmatic is not reassuring — it is impending respiratory failure.** Same logic as a "normal" bicarbonate in DKA.
- **↑ O₂ consumption + ↓ FRC = desaturation within seconds of apnoea** — the reason pregnant intubation is a high-risk airway and pre-oxygenation is non-negotiable.
- **Spirometry does not change in pregnancy.** An abnormal FEV1/FVC is never "just pregnancy."
- Physiological dyspnoea affects 60–70%, starts in the first/second trimester and is not progressive on exertion. Red flags: orthopnoea, PND, sudden onset, hypoxia, chest pain.

#### 1.6 Haematological adaptation

**Plasma volume ↑ 40–50% but red cell mass only ↑ 20–30%** → dilutional ("physiological") anaemia. The critical exam point is timing: **plasma expansion accelerates from the second trimester and the Hb nadir is 28–32 weeks — NOT 12 weeks.** A woman with Hb 98 g/L at 11 weeks does not have physiological anaemia; she has pathology (usually iron deficiency, sometimes haemoglobinopathy) and needs a ferritin and a film.

| Threshold | Value |
|---|---|
| Anaemia, 1st trimester | Hb **<110 g/L** |
| Anaemia, 2nd trimester | Hb **<105 g/L** |
| Anaemia, 3rd trimester | **sources differ: <105 (SA Health) vs <110 g/L (KEMH/WA)** — see note |
| Anaemia, postpartum | Hb **<100 g/L** |
| Iron deficiency in pregnancy | ferritin **<30 µg/L** (vs <15 non-pregnant) |
| Total iron requirement over pregnancy | ~1000 mg |

⚑ **Source disagreement:** third-trimester Hb cut-off. SA Health applies <105 g/L to both second and third trimesters; KEMH (WA) reverts to <110 g/L in the third trimester (matching the CDC pattern of 110/105/110). Either is defensible in a written answer — state <110 first trimester, <105 second, and note the third-trimester figure varies 105–110 by jurisdiction. The ferritin <30 µg/L cut-off is uniform: **ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, so the non-pregnant <15 threshold under-diagnoses in pregnancy.**

Other haematological shifts:
- **Gestational thrombocytopenia** — 5–10% of pregnancies, third trimester, usually **>70–80 ×10⁹/L**, asymptomatic, resolves postpartum. **Discriminator:** platelets <70, onset before the third trimester, or any other cytopenia/organ dysfunction → think ITP, pre-eclampsia/HELLP, TTP, APS.
- **Hypercoagulability** — fibrinogen roughly doubles (4–6 g/L), factors VII, VIII, IX, X, XII and vWF rise, **protein S falls**, acquired activated protein C resistance develops, and fibrinolysis is suppressed (PAI-1, plus placental PAI-2). Net VTE risk ↑ **4–6×**, and is **highest postpartum**, peaking in the first week and elevated for 6 weeks.
- **D-dimer rises physiologically** → useless as a rule-out in pregnancy. Suspected PE goes straight to imaging (CXR then V/Q or CTPA), not to a D-dimer.
- **WCC rises** (up to ~15 ×10⁹/L antenatally, 25–30 ×10⁹/L in labour) and **ESR rises** (fibrinogen). Use **CRP** for infection.

#### 1.7 Renal adaptation

- **RPF ↑ 60–80%, GFR ↑ 40–50%**, both established by the end of the first trimester.
- **Creatinine falls to ~35–70 µmol/L**; urea falls. ⚑ **A creatinine of 90 µmol/L in pregnancy is significant renal impairment** even though most lab reference ranges call it normal — pregnancy-specific ranges are essential.
- Urate falls early, then rises with gestation; used as an adjunct marker in pre-eclampsia.
- **Physiological hydronephrosis**, right > left in 80–90% (progesterone-mediated ureteric relaxation plus dextrorotation of the uterus compressing the right ureter over the iliac vessels). Right loin pain in pregnancy is therefore common and non-specific.
- **Glycosuria is common** (reduced tubular reabsorption threshold) and **must not be used to screen for GDM**.
- Proteinuria up to 300 mg/24 h is normal; **PCR ≥30 mg/mmol** = significant.
- Osmostat resets down ~10 mOsm/kg → serum Na runs ~4–5 mmol/L lower.
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** affects 2–7%; untreated, **20–30% progress to pyelonephritis**, with associated preterm birth and low birthweight. This is why urine MCS is a booking test and why treatment is mandatory in pregnancy but not in non-pregnant adults.

#### 1.8 GI, hepatic and other adaptation

Progesterone reduces lower oesophageal sphincter tone and slows gastric emptying and gut transit; the enlarging uterus adds mechanical displacement. Result: **reflux in up to 80% by the third trimester**, constipation, and — critically for anaesthesia — a **"full stomach" assumption from ~16–20 weeks**, mandating rapid sequence induction and antacid prophylaxis.

Liver biochemistry traps:
- **ALP rises 2–4×** because of the **placental isoenzyme**. A raised ALP in pregnancy means nothing on its own.
- **ALT, AST, GGT and bilirubin should remain within (or below) non-pregnant limits.** Any rise is pathological — think obstetric cholestasis, pre-eclampsia/HELLP, AFLP, viral hepatitis.
- **Albumin falls** by dilution → **total calcium falls but ionised calcium is unchanged**. Correct before calling hypocalcaemia.
- Gallbladder contractility falls and bile becomes more cholesterol-saturated → gallstones.

Thyroid: **hCG is a weak TSH-receptor agonist** (shared α-subunit), so **TSH dips in the first trimester**, sometimes below the non-pregnant lower limit. Oestrogen raises TBG so **total T4/T3 rise while free T4 is roughly unchanged**. Iodine requirement rises (hence 150 µg/day supplementation). **Clue → answer:** hyperemesis + suppressed TSH + raised fT4, no goitre, no eye signs, negative TRAb, settling by 20 weeks = **gestational transient thyrotoxicosis**, not Graves — treat supportively, not with carbimazole.

Other: cortisol and CBG rise (Cushing's is hard to diagnose in pregnancy); prolactin rises 10–20×; relaxin and progesterone cause ligamentous laxity, lumbar lordosis and symphysis pubis dysfunction; a **Th2 shift with reduced cell-mediated immunity** makes listeria, TB, varicella, influenza and malaria more severe (RA often remits, SLE often flares).
