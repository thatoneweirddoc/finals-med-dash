## O&G Pharmacology Summary


### O&G Master Notes — O&G Pharmacology Summary

*Australian sources: Australian Medicines Handbook, Therapeutic Guidelines, TGA Prescribing Medicines in Pregnancy database, SOMANZ 2023, Queensland Clinical Guidelines, RANZCOG, Australasian Menopause Society. All pregnancy safety categories quoted are **TGA (Australian) categories**, not US FDA letters — the two systems assign different letters to the same drug.*

#### 14.1 The TGA pregnancy categorisation system

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Taken by large numbers of pregnant women without observed increase in malformations or harmful fetal effects |
| **B1** | Limited human data, no increased harm observed; **animal studies show no fetal damage** |
| **B2** | Limited human data, no increased harm observed; **animal data inadequate or lacking**, available data show no harm |
| **B3** | Limited human data, no increased harm observed; **animal studies show increased fetal damage of uncertain human significance** |
| **C** | Pharmacological effects causing **reversible** harmful effects on the fetus/neonate **without** malformation |
| **D** | Has caused, is suspected of causing, or may cause **increased fetal malformation or irreversible damage** — may still be used where benefit outweighs risk |
| **X** | Risk of **permanent damage so high the drug must not be used in pregnancy or where pregnancy is possible** |

**Critical caveat, and a favourite MCQ trap: the system is not a hierarchy.** The B subcategories reflect *how much animal data exists*, not degree of safety — a B3 drug is not "safer" than a C or D drug. The clearest illustration: **lamotrigine is TGA Category D** despite being a preferred antiseizure medicine in pregnancy. Categories also ignore trimester of exposure. Use the AMH monograph, not the letter alone.

**The short version**
- A = extensive reassuring human data. D = known/suspected teratogen but sometimes justified. X = never.
- **B1/B2/B3 differ by animal data, not by safety** — B is not automatically safer than C.
- **Absolute contraindications to remember: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, warfarin (1st trimester), valproate, isotretinoin (X), thalidomide (X), misoprostol in ongoing pregnancy (X), live vaccines.**

#### 14.2 Uterotonics

| Drug | Class / mechanism | Dose (Queensland Clinical Guidelines) | Key contraindications and adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oxytocin** (Syntocinon) | Nonapeptide, oxytocin receptor agonist | **Prophylaxis**: 10 IU IM, or 10 IU IV slowly over 3–5 min; **caesarean** 3–5 IU IV over 1–2 min. **Treatment**: 5 IU IV over 1–2 min, may repeat once after 5 min (**max 10 IU IV bolus**), then infusion 30 IU in 500 mL, run at **5–10 IU/hour** | Rapid IV bolus → **hypotension, tachycardia, transient ST changes**; caution in cardiac disease and hypovolaemia. **Antidiuretic (structurally similar to ADH)** → **hyponatraemia and water intoxication with high-dose prolonged infusion in dextrose** |
| **Ergometrine** | Ergot alkaloid; non-selective smooth muscle constriction via 5-HT/α-adrenergic receptors | **250–500 microgram IM** (or IV over 1–2 min); repeat 5-minutely to a **maximum 1 mg** | **Contraindicated in hypertension, pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, severe cardiac/vascular/renal/hepatic disease, retained placenta, sepsis.** Causes marked vomiting → routinely give an antiemetic |
| **Syntometrine** | Fixed combination | Oxytocin 5 IU + ergometrine 500 microgram per mL, IM | Same contraindications as ergometrine; not recommended for routine universal prophylaxis in Australia |
| **Misoprostol** | Synthetic **PGE1** analogue | **800–1000 microgram sublingual or PR**; do not repeat within 2 hours | **TGA Category X in ongoing pregnancy** (abortifacient). Side effects: **pyrexia, shivering/rigors**, diarrhoea. Heat-stable — the agent of choice where cold chain is unavailable. Third-line for PPH in Australia (only when injectables unavailable) |
| **Carboprost** (Hemabate) | Synthetic **15-methyl PGF2α** | **250 microgram IM**, repeat no more frequently than **every 15 minutes**, to a **maximum 2 mg (8 doses)**; 500 microgram intramyometrial is off-label | **Contraindicated in asthma** (bronchoconstriction — the single most examined fact about this drug). Also caution in cardiac, hepatic, renal disease. Side effects: bronchospasm, diarrhoea, vomiting, flushing, pyrexia |
| **Carbetocin** ⚑ | Long-acting oxytocin analogue | Single dose for **prophylaxis only** — never repeated | Heat-stable formulation now available; IM preferred for vaginal birth where cold chain unreliable; IV may be considered at caesarean under regional anaesthesia |
| **Tranexamic acid** ⚑ | Antifibrinolytic (lysine analogue, blocks plasminogen binding) | **1 g IV over 10 min**, as early as possible and **ideally within 3 hours** of PPH onset; may repeat 1 g once if bleeding persists at 30 min or restarts within 24 h | Adjunct, **not a uterotonic**. Not recommended as routine prophylaxis in Australia. Added to PPH protocols after the WOMAN trial (2017) — absent from older textbooks |

PPH escalation: **Oxytocin → Ergometrine → Carboprost/Misoprostol → surgical**, with tranexamic acid early and in parallel. Always cross-check contraindications against the stem: **hypertensive → not ergometrine; asthmatic → not carboprost.** Exam vignettes pair these deliberately.

**The short version**
- **Ergometrine is contraindicated in hypertension/pre-eclampsia; carboprost is contraindicated in asthma.**
- Oxytocin bolus max 10 IU IV in PPH; prolonged high-dose infusion causes **hyponatraemia**.
- Misoprostol is PGE1, carboprost is PGF2α; misoprostol is **Category X** in an ongoing pregnancy.
- **Tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 hours** is now standard in PPH ⚑.

#### 14.3 Tocolytics and antenatal corticosteroids

Tocolysis does **not** improve neonatal outcome by itself. Its only purpose is to buy **48 hours** for antenatal corticosteroids to act and for in-utero transfer.

- **Nifedipine** — first-line in Australia. CCB, oral, typically 20 mg loading with repeats then maintenance per local protocol. Better neonatal outcomes and fewer maternal effects than beta-agonists. Caution **hypotension**; avoid combining with magnesium (neuromuscular blockade).
- **GTN patch** (nitric oxide donor) — alternative; headache dose-limiting. **Atosiban** (oxytocin receptor antagonist) — minimal maternal effects.
- **Indomethacin** — <32 weeks, short courses only: **premature ductal closure** and **oligohydramnios**.
- **Salbutamol/terbutaline** — abandoned for maintenance (tachycardia, hyperglycaemia, hypokalaemia, **pulmonary oedema**); terbutaline still used acutely for **uterine hyperstimulation**.

**Contraindications to tocolysis**: chorioamnionitis, significant APH/abruption, non-reassuring fetal status, severe pre-eclampsia, IUFD, lethal anomaly, advanced labour.

**Antenatal corticosteroids** — **betamethasone 11.4 mg IM, two doses 24 hours apart** (Australian preparation; the 12 mg in overseas texts is a different salt combination). Offered **24+0 to 34+6 weeks** where preterm birth is anticipated within 7 days; consider from 22–23 weeks where active neonatal care is planned. Maximal benefit 24 h–7 days after the second dose; reduces RDS, IVH, NEC and neonatal death. Caveats: **transient maternal hyperglycaemia — check BGLs in diabetes**, and a transient fall in fetal movements and FHR variability.

**Magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection** — a separate indication from eclampsia prophylaxis. Given when birth is anticipated **<30 weeks** (individualised 30–34), ideally ≥4 h before birth; reduces cerebral palsy. Same regimen as below.

#### 14.4 Antihypertensives in pregnancy

⚑ **SOMANZ 2023** made two changes worth knowing: **treat to a target of ≤135/85 mmHg** (tighter than earlier permissive targets, following CHIPS/CHAP), and **aspirin 150 mg nocte** for pre-eclampsia prophylaxis (up from 100 mg), started **before 16 weeks** in high-risk women and continued to **34–36 weeks**. Proteinuria is **no longer required** to diagnose pre-eclampsia — hypertension plus any maternal organ dysfunction or uteroplacental dysfunction (including FGR) suffices.

| Agent | Class | Typical oral dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Labetalol** | Combined α/β blocker | 100–400 mg tds–qid | Widely first-line. Neonatal bradycardia/hypoglycaemia possible; avoid in asthma |
| **Methyldopa** | Central α2 agonist | 250–750 mg tds | **TGA Category A**, longest safety record; slow onset (24 h), sedation, **depression** — avoid postnatally in women at risk of postnatal depression |
| **Nifedipine** (controlled release) | Dihydropyridine CCB | 30–60 mg daily–bd | Well tolerated; headache and flushing |
| **Hydralazine** | Direct vasodilator | 25–50 mg tds | Reflex tachycardia, headache; drug-induced lupus with chronic use |
| **Prazosin** | α1 blocker | 0.5–5 mg tds–qid | Useful add-on; first-dose hypotension |
| **Clonidine** | Central α2 agonist | Add-on | Rebound hypertension on abrupt cessation |

**Acute severe hypertension (≥160/110)** — treat urgently to a post-treatment target of **systolic 130–150, diastolic 80–90 mmHg** (QCG); do not drop it precipitously, as placental perfusion is pressure-dependent:
- **IV labetalol 20–80 mg** bolus over 2 min, repeat every 10 min (onset ~5 min)
- **Oral immediate-release nifedipine 10–20 mg**, repeat at 30–45 min (onset 30–45 min)
- **IV hydralazine 5–10 mg** over **3–10 min**, repeat every 20–40 min, **max 30 mg** cumulative (QCG) (onset ~20 min) — pre-load with fluid as it causes maternal hypotension and fetal distress; a faster push is exactly what precipitates the BP crash and non-reassuring CTG

**Contraindicated**
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs — TGA Category D.** Second- and third-trimester exposure causes the **fetal renin–angiotensin system blockade syndrome**: oligohydramnios, fetal renal failure/anuria, hypocalvaria, limb contractures, pulmonary hypoplasia, neonatal hypotension, death. Stop pre-pregnancy or as soon as pregnancy is confirmed. (**Both are safe in breastfeeding** — enalapril and captopril in particular. Do not confuse the two settings.)
- **Atenolol — associated with fetal growth restriction**; avoid, particularly early and prolonged use. Other beta-blockers are preferred.
- **Diuretics** — avoided in pre-eclampsia (already plasma-volume depleted); may be continued in specific cardiac indications.
- **Sodium nitroprusside** — cyanide/thiocyanate accumulation; last resort only.

**The short version**
- **ACEi/ARB and atenolol out; labetalol, methyldopa, nifedipine, hydralazine, prazosin in.**
- ⚑ SOMANZ 2023: target **≤135/85**; **aspirin 150 mg nocte before 16 weeks**; proteinuria not required for the diagnosis.
- Severe hypertension: IV labetalol, IV hydralazine, or oral IR nifedipine.
- ACE inhibitors are contraindicated in **pregnancy** but acceptable in **breastfeeding**.

#### 14.5 Magnesium sulfate

Two entirely separate obstetric indications — examiners test whether you can keep them apart.

1. **Eclampsia treatment and prevention of seizures in severe pre-eclampsia.** Superior to phenytoin and diazepam (MAGPIE, Collaborative Eclampsia Trial). Mechanism is probably cerebral vasodilatation and NMDA receptor antagonism rather than a classical anticonvulsant effect.
2. **Fetal neuroprotection when preterm birth <30 weeks is imminent** — reduces cerebral palsy.

**Regimen (identical for both)**: **loading dose 4 g IV over 20 minutes**, then **maintenance 1 g/hour by infusion**. For eclampsia, continue for **at least 24 hours after the last seizure or after birth, whichever is later**. Recurrent seizure on magnesium: further 2–4 g IV bolus over 5–10 minutes.

**Monitoring** — clinical, not routine bloods:

| Parameter | Threshold for concern |
|---|---|
| **Deep tendon (patellar) reflexes** | **Must be present before starting; loss of reflexes is the earliest sign of toxicity** — stop the infusion |
| **Respiratory rate** | **<12/min** → stop infusion |
| Oxygen saturation | <95% |
| **Urine output** | **<25 mL/hour** (magnesium is renally cleared — oliguria causes accumulation; reduce or halt maintenance and check level) |
| Serum magnesium | Therapeutic 1.7–3.5 mmol/L; measure only in renal impairment or clinical concern |

**Toxicity sequence (learn the order)**: **loss of patellar reflexes → nausea, flushing, slurred speech, blurred vision, somnolence → respiratory depression → cardiac conduction block and arrest**.

**Antidote: calcium gluconate 1 g in 10 mL (10%) IV slowly over 3–10 minutes** into a large vein. Calcium antagonises magnesium at the neuromuscular junction. This is a guaranteed short-answer question — know the drug, the dose and the volume.

Also note: magnesium causes uterine relaxation and can contribute to **uterine atony and PPH**; and combining magnesium with **nifedipine** risks profound neuromuscular blockade.

**The short version**
- **4 g load over 20 min, then 1 g/hour; continue 24 h after the last seizure or birth.**
- **Reflexes first, respiratory rate second, urine output third.**
- **Antidote = calcium gluconate 1 g (10 mL of 10%) IV.**
- Two indications: **eclampsia** and **fetal neuroprotection <30 weeks**.

#### 14.6 Antimicrobials in pregnancy and lactation

**Safe and routinely used**: penicillins (including benzathine benzylpenicillin, amoxicillin), cephalosporins (cephalexin, ceftriaxone, cefazolin), **erythromycin (not the estolate salt — maternal hepatotoxicity)**, azithromycin, clindamycin, **metronidazole (Category B2)**, nitrofurantoin, aciclovir/valaciclovir (B3).

**Restricted or contraindicated**

| Drug | Problem | Practical rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Tetracyclines (doxycycline)** | **Category D** — permanent **tooth discolouration and enamel hypoplasia**, effects on fetal bone growth (2nd/3rd trimester) | Avoid throughout pregnancy; substitute azithromycin |
| **Trimethoprim (± sulfamethoxazole)** | **Folate antagonist** — neural tube and cardiac defects with first-trimester exposure | **Avoid in the first trimester**; if unavoidable, add **folic acid 5 mg daily**. Sulfonamides near term → neonatal kernicterus |
| **Nitrofurantoin** | **Haemolysis in G6PD-deficient neonates** | Excellent for cystitis in the first and second trimesters; **avoid after 37 weeks** or when early delivery is planned |
| **Aminoglycosides (gentamicin)** | **Category D** — fetal **ototoxicity (eighth nerve) and nephrotoxicity** | Use only when the indication is compelling (e.g. severe listeriosis, chorioamnionitis with sepsis); monitor levels |
| **Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin)** | Arthropathy in animal studies; cartilage concern | Avoid unless no alternative |
| **Chloramphenicol** | **Grey baby syndrome** near term | Avoid |
| **Amoxicillin–clavulanate** | Associated with **neonatal necrotising enterocolitis** when given for **PPROM** (ORACLE I) | **Do not use in PPROM** — the antibiotic for PPROM is **erythromycin** (10 days or until labour) |
| **Fluconazole (oral)** | High-dose prolonged use is teratogenic (craniofacial, skeletal); small signal even with single dose | Use **topical azoles** for candidiasis in pregnancy |

**Breastfeeding**: most antibiotics are compatible. Practical cautions — metronidazole in high single doses alters milk taste (space the 2 g dose from a feed); tetracyclines are acceptable short-term (calcium in milk limits absorption) but avoided if alternatives exist; fluoroquinolones generally avoided; chloramphenicol avoided.

#### 14.7 Anti-emetics in pregnancy

Stepwise, starting non-pharmacological (small frequent meals, ginger, acupressure), then:

| Step | Drug | TGA category | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First line** | **Pyridoxine (vitamin B6)** ± **doxylamine** | Doxylamine **A** | The evidence-based combination; both available OTC in Australia as separate products. ⚑ The historical Bendectin/Debendox withdrawal (1983) has since been overturned by re-analysis — the combination is now first-line internationally |
| First line | Ginger | — | Modest but real benefit |
| Second line | **Metoclopramide** | **A** | Most commonly prescribed antiemetic in Australian pregnancy; **extrapyramidal reactions/oculogyric crisis**, especially in young women — limit to 5 days |
| Second line | **Prochlorperazine**, **promethazine** | Prochlorperazine **C** | Sedation, extrapyramidal effects |
| Third line | **Ondansetron** | **B1** | Effective but **not first-line**. A small absolute increase in **cleft palate** with first-trimester exposure has been reported (background ~11/10,000 rising by roughly 3/10,000); cardiac signal is inconsistent. Reserve for refractory symptoms, ideally after 10 weeks; warn about **constipation** and co-prescribe a laxative |
| Refractory / hyperemesis | **Corticosteroids** (e.g. prednisolone, or IV hydrocortisone) | — | **Avoid before 10 weeks** (possible cleft lip/palate association); requires review to prevent indefinite use |

**Hyperemesis gravidarum** additionally requires: IV rehydration with **sodium chloride 0.9%** (avoid dextrose-only fluids — precipitates Wernicke encephalopathy), potassium replacement, and **thiamine 100 mg daily PO or IV** before any carbohydrate load. Check TFTs (transient biochemical thyrotoxicosis from βhCG cross-reactivity — do **not** treat with antithyroid drugs), and consider VTE prophylaxis given dehydration and immobility.

#### 14.8 Antiseizure medicines and teratogenicity

**Valproate is the highest-risk antiseizure medicine in pregnancy and must be avoided in anyone of childbearing potential unless there is no alternative.**

- Major congenital malformation rate is **dose-dependent: ~3.3% at <1100 mg/day, rising to ~30% at ≥1100 mg/day** — a striking figure worth remembering. Malformations include **neural tube defects (especially lumbosacral), cardiac, craniofacial and limb defects**.
- **Neurodevelopmental harm is the more important risk**: intellectual disability (OR ~2.4–4.5), ADHD (OR ~1.4–1.8), autism spectrum disorder (OR ~1.7–4.4). **No dose has been shown to be free of risk.**
- ⚑ **TGA regulatory requirements in Australia**: valproate for a person of childbearing potential requires specialist involvement, documented counselling, and **effective contraception**. Note the interaction the other way — oestrogen-containing contraceptives **lower lamotrigine levels** (loss of seizure control on starting, toxicity on stopping) and induce valproate glucuronidation.
- ⚑ **2025 TGA advisory extended to male patients**: advise partners of possible risk, effective contraception during and for **3 months after** treatment, and no sperm donation while on valproate. This is new and absent from all textbooks.
- **Preferred alternatives: lamotrigine and levetiracetam** (lowest malformation rates and no identified neurodevelopmental signal), despite lamotrigine's **TGA Category D** label — the classic demonstration that the category letter is not a safety ranking. Lamotrigine clearance rises substantially in pregnancy; levels need monitoring and dose escalation, with reduction postpartum.
- Other agents: **carbamazepine** — neural tube defects, ~5% malformation rate. **Topiramate** — **cleft lip/palate and fetal growth restriction**; also relevant as a migraine and weight-loss drug. **Phenytoin** — fetal hydantoin syndrome (hypoplastic nails/distal phalanges, craniofacial dysmorphism, growth restriction).
- **Folic acid 5 mg daily** pre-conception and through the first trimester for women on antiseizure medicines (note: folate has **not** been shown to abolish valproate-specific risk).
- **Vitamin K** to the neonate is routine in Australia regardless; enzyme-inducing antiseizure medicines are an additional reason not to omit it.
- **Never stop antiseizure medication abruptly on discovering pregnancy** — maternal status epilepticus is more dangerous than continued exposure. Refer urgently for pre-conception or early pregnancy review.

**The short version**
- Valproate: **~3% malformations under 1100 mg/day, ~30% at or above it**, plus autism/ID risk at any dose.
- **Lamotrigine and levetiracetam are the preferred agents** — lamotrigine is Category D anyway, proving categories are not a ranking.
- Folic acid **5 mg**; never stop the drug abruptly.
- ⚑ Australian pregnancy-prevention requirements now extend to **male** valproate users.

#### 14.9 Anticoagulation in pregnancy

**LMWH (enoxaparin) is the anticoagulant of choice.** It does not cross the placenta, is not excreted in clinically significant amounts in breast milk, and does not require routine monitoring.

**Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy** (TGA Category D):
- **Warfarin embryopathy** from exposure at approximately **6–12 weeks**: **nasal hypoplasia and stippled epiphyses (chondrodysplasia punctata)**, limb hypoplasia.
- Second/third-trimester exposure: **fetal CNS abnormalities and intracranial haemorrhage**; risk of fetal bleeding at delivery because warfarin **crosses the placenta and the fetal liver cannot regenerate clotting factors**.
- Practical rule: stop warfarin and convert to LMWH **before 6 weeks' gestation** (ideally pre-conception with a positive pregnancy test protocol). The one arguable exception is a **mechanical mitral valve prosthesis**, where warfarin's superior maternal thromboembolic protection may be judged to outweigh fetal risk — a specialist decision, not a finals-level default.
- **Warfarin is safe in breastfeeding** — it is not secreted in significant amounts in milk. Restart postpartum.

**DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) are avoided in pregnancy and breastfeeding** — insufficient data, and they cross the placenta.

**Enoxaparin dosing (Queensland Clinical Guidelines, weight-banded prophylaxis)**

| Booking weight | Prophylactic dose |
|---|---|
| <50 kg | 20 mg daily |
| 50–90 kg | **40 mg daily** |
| 91–130 kg | 60 mg daily |
| 131–170 kg | 80 mg daily |
| >170 kg | 0.5 mg/kg daily |

Therapeutic: **1 mg/kg SC bd** antenatally; 1.5 mg/kg daily postnatally is an option. Treatment of pregnancy-associated VTE continues for the remainder of pregnancy and **at least 6 weeks postpartum, minimum 3 months total**.

**Neuraxial anaesthesia timing** — reliably examined:
- **Last prophylactic dose to block: ≥12 hours**
- **Last therapeutic dose to block: ≥24 hours**
- **Block/catheter removal to next dose: ≥4–6 hours**

Other points: LMWH does not need routine anti-Xa monitoring in pregnancy except at extremes of weight or in renal impairment; heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is rare with LMWH but check platelets if previously exposed; **long-term heparin causes osteoporosis** (much less with LMWH than UFH). **Unfractionated heparin** is preferred where rapid reversal may be needed (renal failure, imminent delivery, high bleeding risk) because it is short-acting and reversible with protamine.

#### 14.10 Drugs in breastfeeding — general principles

Most drugs are compatible. The relevant quantity is the **relative infant dose (RID)** — <10% of the weight-adjusted maternal dose is generally regarded as acceptable. Risk is higher with: **neonates and preterm infants** (immature clearance), **long half-life drugs**, **high lipid solubility**, **low protein binding**, and **drugs with a narrow therapeutic index**.

| Compatible | Avoid or use with caution |
|---|---|
| Paracetamol, ibuprofen, most penicillins/cephalosporins, **warfarin**, **heparin/LMWH**, **enalapril/captopril**, labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa, insulin, metformin, levothyroxine, most SSRIs (**sertraline** best evidence), inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators | **Codeine** (ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolisers → neonatal opioid toxicity; use alternatives), **cytotoxics including methotrexate**, radioactive iodine, **amiodarone**, lithium (monitorable but requires infant levels), high-dose aspirin, **DOACs**, oestrogen-containing contraceptives before lactation is established, ergotamine, retinoids |

Practical advice: take the dose immediately **after** a feed to minimise peak exposure, and prefer drugs with existing lactation data over newer agents.

#### 14.11 Hormonal contraception — pharmacology

**Combined hormonal contraception (COC, patch, vaginal ring)** — **ethinyloestradiol** (or increasingly oestradiol valerate/estetrol) plus a progestogen.
- **Primary mechanism: progestogen-driven suppression of the LH surge → inhibition of ovulation.** The oestrogen suppresses FSH (preventing dominant follicle selection) and, importantly, **stabilises the endometrium to give cycle control** — that is its main practical job. Secondary effects: thickened cervical mucus, unfavourable endometrium.
- **Progestogen generations**: first/second (norethisterone, **levonorgestrel** — lowest VTE risk); third (desogestrel, gestodene — higher VTE risk); fourth (**drospirenone** — anti-mineralocorticoid and anti-androgenic, useful in PCOS/acne, **risk of hyperkalaemia with ACEi/ARB/spironolactone/potassium-sparing diuretics**); **cyproterone acetate** — most anti-androgenic, highest VTE risk.
- **VTE**: baseline in non-users ~2/10,000 woman-years; **levonorgestrel-containing COC ~5–7/10,000; desogestrel/gestodene/cyproterone ~9–12/10,000; pregnancy ~29/10,000 and the puerperium far higher.** Framing COC risk against pregnancy risk is the standard exam-safe answer.
- **Absolute contraindications (UKMEC/WHOMEC category 4)**: **migraine with aura** (stroke risk), current or past VTE/known thrombophilia, **<6 weeks postpartum if breastfeeding**, smoking ≥15/day at age ≥35, **BP ≥160/100**, current breast cancer, active liver disease/tumour, complicated valvular or ischaemic heart disease, stroke, major surgery with immobilisation, SLE with positive antiphospholipid antibodies, diabetes with vascular complications.
- **Non-contraceptive benefits worth quoting**: reduced ovarian and endometrial cancer risk (persisting decades), lighter and more predictable bleeding, improved dysmenorrhoea, endometriosis, acne and PCOS symptoms. Small increases in breast and cervical cancer risk that regress after cessation.
- **Enzyme inducers** (rifampicin, rifabutin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone, topiramate at higher doses, **St John's wort**) reduce efficacy of COC, POP, implant and emergency levonorgestrel — **switch to depot injection or an IUD, which are unaffected**. **Sodium valproate and lamotrigine are not inducers** — but COC lowers lamotrigine levels (see 14.8). ⚑ Broad-spectrum non-enzyme-inducing antibiotics **no longer** require additional precautions.

**Progestogen-only methods**
- **POP (levonorgestrel/norethisterone)**: mucus thickening is the main mechanism; ovulation inhibited in only ~50% of cycles; **3-hour window**. **Desogestrel POP** inhibits ovulation in ~97% and has a **12-hour window**. ⚑ **Drospirenone-only pill** (24/4 regimen) is now available in Australia with a 24-hour window.
- **Etonogestrel implant (Implanon NXT)**: 3 years; ovulation inhibition; commonest reason for removal is **irregular bleeding**.
- **DMPA (depot medroxyprogesterone acetate)**: 12-weekly; ovulation inhibition. **Reversible reduction in bone mineral density** (matters in adolescents and near menopause) and **delayed return of fertility (up to 12 months)** — the two facts always tested.
- **Levonorgestrel-IUD (52 mg, e.g. Mirena)**: predominantly local — endometrial suppression and mucus thickening; ovulation is often preserved. Licensed in Australia for contraception, **heavy menstrual bleeding**, and **endometrial protection during MHT**.
- **Copper IUD**: non-hormonal; copper ions are spermatotoxic and impair fertilisation; also the **most effective emergency contraception** (>99%), effective up to 5 days after intercourse or after the estimated day of ovulation. Downside: **heavier, more painful periods**.

**Emergency contraception**
- **Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg** — within 72 h (declining efficacy); delays ovulation; **ineffective once the LH surge has begun**; double the dose if BMI >26 or on enzyme inducers.
- **Ulipristal acetate 30 mg** — selective progesterone receptor modulator, effective up to **120 h**, works later in the follicular phase than levonorgestrel. **Do not give progestogen for 5 days afterwards** (it blunts ulipristal); avoid if the woman needs to restart hormonal contraception immediately.
- **Copper IUD** — most effective; also provides ongoing contraception.

#### 14.12 Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT)

**The governing rule: oestrogen relieves symptoms; a progestogen is required for endometrial protection in any woman with an intact uterus.** Unopposed oestrogen with a uterus causes endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma. Vaginal (local) oestrogen at standard low doses does **not** require a progestogen.

| Component | Australian preparations |
|---|---|
| Oral oestrogen | Oestradiol 1–2 mg (Estrofem, Zumenon, Progynova); conjugated equine oestrogens 0.3–0.625 mg (Premarin) |
| **Transdermal oestrogen** | Patches 25–100 microgram twice weekly (Estradot, Estraderm MX); gels (Estrogel 0.75–3 mg daily, Sandrena 0.5–1.5 mg) |
| Progestogen | **Micronised progesterone 100 mg (continuous) or 200 mg (cyclical)**; medroxyprogesterone acetate; norethisterone; dydrogesterone; or **52 mg levonorgestrel-IUD** for endometrial protection |
| Combined continuous | Kliovance, Femoston-conti, Angeliq, **Bijuva** (oestradiol + micronised progesterone) |
| Combined cyclical | Trisequens, Femoston |
| Tibolone | 2.5 mg — synthetic steroid with oestrogenic, progestogenic and androgenic activity; helps libido; not for use within 12 months of the LMP |
| Vaginal | Oestriol cream/pessary 0.5 mg (Ovestin); oestradiol 10 microgram pessary (Vagifem); DHEA 6.5 mg (Intrarosa) |

**Why transdermal matters — the single most examined MHT fact: transdermal oestradiol avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism and does not increase VTE or stroke risk, unlike oral oestrogen.** Choose transdermal for women with obesity, migraine, hypertriglyceridaemia, gallbladder disease, or VTE risk factors.

Risk framing: breast cancer risk rises with **combined** (not oestrogen-only) MHT, is duration-dependent, and is small in absolute terms — roughly comparable to the risk conferred by obesity or regular alcohol. **Micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appear to carry lower breast risk than synthetic progestogens.** ⚑ Post-WHI reanalysis has substantially softened earlier blanket warnings: for symptomatic women **<60 years or within 10 years of menopause**, benefits generally outweigh risks, and MHT is protective against osteoporotic fracture. MHT is **not** used for cardiovascular prevention.

Contraindications: current/past breast cancer, oestrogen-dependent malignancy, undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, active VTE or arterial thromboembolic disease, active liver disease, untreated endometrial hyperplasia. **Premature ovarian insufficiency is different** — replace until the average age of menopause (~51), and treat as physiological replacement rather than as "MHT with risks."

**Non-hormonal alternatives** for vasomotor symptoms: SSRIs/SNRIs (venlafaxine, escitalopram — **avoid paroxetine and fluoxetine in women on tamoxifen**, CYP2D6 inhibition), gabapentin, clonidine, CBT; ⚑ **fezolinetant** (neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist) is a new non-hormonal class.

#### 14.13 Methotrexate for ectopic pregnancy

Folate antagonist — inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, halting DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing trophoblast. **TGA Category D.**

**Eligibility (Queensland Clinical Guidelines)**
- **Haemodynamically stable**, unruptured, willing and able to attend follow-up
- **βhCG below ~5000 IU/L** gives the best success rates (route in Queensland is determined by level: **IM if ≤3000 IU/L, IV if >3000 IU/L**); success falls markedly above 5000
- **Adnexal mass <3–3.5 cm**
- **No fetal cardiac activity**
- Minimal or no free fluid in the pelvis
- Normal FBC, LFTs and renal function

**Dose: 50 mg/m² body surface area, single dose** (a two-dose or multi-dose regimen exists for higher hCG or plateauing levels).

**Monitoring: βhCG on days 4 and 7.** Expect a **≥15% fall between day 4 and day 7** — if not achieved, give a second dose (or reconsider surgery). Then weekly βhCG until undetectable. A **transient rise between day 1 and day 4 is expected and not failure** — a classic MCQ trap.

**Counselling**
- **Avoid folate and folate-containing supplements** during treatment (they antagonise the drug)
- **Avoid alcohol** and **NSAIDs** (renal clearance interaction, marrow toxicity)
- Avoid prolonged **sun exposure** (photosensitivity)
- **No intercourse** during treatment (rupture risk)
- **Delay conception for 3 months** after treatment
- Warn that **abdominal pain around day 3–7 is common** ("separation pain") but that severe or worsening pain, shoulder-tip pain or syncope means presenting immediately — rupture can still occur

**Contraindications**: breastfeeding, immunodeficiency, significant renal or hepatic impairment, blood dyscrasias, active peptic ulcer disease or pulmonary disease, heterotopic pregnancy with a viable intrauterine pregnancy, inability to comply with follow-up, geographic isolation from care.

**Anti-D**: give to Rh D negative women without preformed anti-D — **250 IU if ≤12 weeks, 625 IU if >12 weeks**, within 72 hours.

**The short version**
- **50 mg/m² IM single dose**; check βhCG **days 4 and 7**, expect **≥15% fall**; a day 1→4 rise is normal.
- Need: stable, unruptured, **hCG <5000**, mass **<3.5 cm**, no fetal cardiac activity.
- **No folate, no alcohol, no NSAIDs, no intercourse; avoid pregnancy 3 months.**
