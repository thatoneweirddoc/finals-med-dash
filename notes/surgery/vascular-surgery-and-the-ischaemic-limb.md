## Vascular Surgery and the Ischaemic Limb


### Surgery Master Notes — Vascular Surgery and the Ischaemic Limb

*Sources: ANZSVS; Global Vascular Guidelines on CLTI (2019); ESVS (2024); SVS AAA (2018) and claudication (2025); ESO carotid guideline (2021); Stroke Foundation ANZ Living Stroke Guidelines; Therapeutic Guidelines and AMH; Wounds Australia/AWMA venous leg ulcer guideline; Australian diabetes-related foot disease guidelines (2021). Scope: chronic and acute limb ischaemia, aortic aneurysm and dissection, carotid, venous and diabetic-foot disease, leg ulcers and vascular access.*

#### 7.1 Peripheral arterial disease and intermittent claudication

**The short version**
- Claudication is **reproducible, exertional, relieved within minutes by standing still**; spinal claudication needs flexion or sitting.
- ⚑ **Leriche syndrome triad — buttock/thigh claudication + erectile dysfunction + absent femoral pulses = occlusive atherosclerosis at the aortic bifurcation (aorto-iliac disease).**
- **ABI ≤0.90 diagnoses PAD; ABI >1.40 means incompressible calcified vessels** (diabetes, CKD) and is uninterpretable — use the **toe–brachial index**.
- **Smoking cessation + supervised exercise + antiplatelet + statin come BEFORE revascularisation.** Most claudicants never lose the limb — they die of coronary events.

**Risk factors:** ⚑ **smoking (3–4×) and diabetes weigh disproportionately** here; also age >65, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, CKD, male sex, family history. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians have far higher rates of diabetes-related PAD and amputation. Over 5 years about three-quarters are stable or improved on medical therapy and **only ~1–2% per year progress to CLTI** — but PAD is a coronary risk equivalent.

**Localising the level.** **Buttock/hip/thigh claudication = aorto-iliac disease**, with **erectile dysfunction** and **weak or absent femoral pulses bilaterally** — the **Leriche** picture. **Calf claudication = femoropopliteal disease** (classically SFA at the adductor hiatus): femoral pulse present, popliteal and distal absent. **Foot/arch pain = tibial/pedal disease**, typical of **diabetes** and CKD, often presenting as tissue loss because neuropathy masks pain. A **shortening claudication distance** is a red flag. **Examination:** pulses side-to-side, bruits, hair loss and shiny atrophic skin, **Buerger's test**; palpate for **AAA**, check for **AF**.

##### Ankle–brachial index

**How:** supine, rested 10 min, **hand-held Doppler** (palpation over-reads). Record brachial systolic in **both arms** and, at each ankle, **dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial**; **ABI = highest ankle systolic in that leg ÷ highest brachial systolic**.

| ABI | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **>1.40** | ⚑ **Incompressible calcified vessels — uninterpretable.** Do **TBI**. Diabetes, CKD, elderly |
| 1.00–1.40 | Normal (0.91–0.99 borderline — if symptomatic, **exercise ABI**) |
| **≤0.90** | **Diagnostic of PAD**; 0.40–0.90 is the usual claudication range, **<0.40** severe (rest pain, tissue loss) |

**Toe–brachial index:** digital arteries escape medial calcification, so toe pressures stay valid. **TBI <0.70 = PAD**, and **toe pressure <30 mmHg** or **ankle pressure <50 mmHg** marks a wound unlikely to heal without revascularisation. Image (duplex → CTA/MRA) only once revascularisation is contemplated.

##### Management — the order matters

**1. Lifestyle.** **Smoking cessation** is the highest-yield single intervention. **Supervised exercise therapy** is first-line ahead of revascularisation: **~3 sessions/week for ≥12 weeks, 30–60 min, walking to near-maximal claudication pain, rest, repeat**; where none is available (a real Australian access problem), a **structured home-based** programme is the fallback.

**2. Best medical therapy.**
- **Single antiplatelet: clopidogrel 75 mg daily is generally preferred** over aspirin in symptomatic PAD (CAPRIE); **aspirin 100 mg daily** is an accepted alternative and common Australian practice. **No routine DAPT** in stable PAD.
- **High-intensity statin for all** regardless of baseline lipids (atorvastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 20–40 mg), plus ezetimibe ± PCSK9 inhibitor if targets are missed — statins reduce events **and** improve walking distance. Control BP; ACE inhibitors are not contraindicated.
- ⚑ **Rivaroxaban 2.5 mg BD plus aspirin 100 mg daily** (the "vascular dose") reduced MACE and **major adverse limb events including amputation** in COMPASS and VOYAGER PAD, at the cost of major bleeding. ⚑ It is **not** full-dose anticoagulation, and **PBS listing here has been restrictive**.
- **Cilostazol** helps walking distance but is **contraindicated in heart failure**.

**3. Revascularisation** only when symptoms are **lifestyle- or vocation-limiting despite ≥3 months of exercise and best medical therapy**. **Endovascular** (angioplasty ± stent) suits short lesions, **aorto-iliac** disease and comorbid patients — less morbidity, **lower long-term patency**. **Open** (endarterectomy; **fem-pop bypass with autologous great saphenous vein rather than prosthetic**, especially below knee) suits long occlusions and failed endovascular therapy. ⚑ Endovascular-first is the default, but **preserve the vein conduit**.

#### 7.2 Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia

**The short version**
- ⚑ **"Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia" has replaced "critical limb ischaemia"** (Global Vascular Guidelines 2019) — outcome depends on **wound, ischaemia and infection together** (**WIfI** staging), not one haemodynamic cut-off.
- Definition: **PAD plus ischaemic rest pain, or ulceration/gangrene present ≥2 weeks**, with corroborating haemodynamics.
- **Rest pain: burning forefoot/toe pain, worse lying flat at night, relieved by hanging the leg over the side of the bed** — gravity does the perfusing.
- **Limb- AND life-threatening** — high amputation and death rates within a year. **Urgent vascular referral, not a clinic letter.**

Patients often present with a **dependent oedematous leg** from sleeping upright. ⚑ **Buerger's test:** on elevation, pallor at a low angle (**Buerger's angle <20–30° = severe ischaemia**); on dependency, slow return of colour to a dusky "sunset" red — **dependent rubor**. **Elevation pallor plus dependent rubor is near-pathognomonic.** Also cool shiny hairless skin, venous guttering, ulceration at toes and pressure points, gangrene (**wet gangrene is an emergency**). Ankle pressure <50–70 mmHg, toe pressure <30–50 mmHg or ABI <0.40 supports it — but ⚑ **never exclude CLTI on a "normal" ABI in a diabetic**.

**Management:** **urgent vascular referral**; **opioid analgesia**; best medical therapy as in 7.1; wound care, offloading and infection treatment; **revascularisation** aiming for **direct in-line flow to the foot**; **amputation** where revascularisation is impossible or the foot unsalvageable. **Levels:** digital/ray; transmetatarsal (retains a weight-bearing foot); ⚑ **below-knee (transtibial) preserves the knee and therefore prosthetic ambulation — the level to aim for**; above-knee heals more reliably in poor perfusion but rarely permits walking. **Phantom limb pain** is common.

#### 7.3 Acute limb ischaemia

**The short version**
- **The six Ps in order of appearance: Pain → Pallor → Pulselessness → Perishing cold → Paraesthesia → Paralysis.**
- ⚑ **Paraesthesia and paralysis are the two that matter** — a **threatened limb**. **Fixed non-blanching mottling + rigid tender muscles + anaesthesia and paralysis = irreversible.**
- Muscle tolerates roughly **4–6 hours** of complete ischaemia. **Immediate: IV unfractionated heparin, analgesia, bloods (K⁺, CK, lactate, group & hold), ECG, urgent vascular referral.**
- ⚑ **In a threatened (Rutherford IIb) limb, imaging must not delay revascularisation.**

| Feature | **Embolic** | **Thrombotic (in situ)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | **Sudden**; patient names the hour; more severe | **Gradual**, or a step-change on background claudication |
| **Source** | • **AF** (commonest)<br>• Recent **MI** with mural thrombus<br>• Prosthetic valve, endocarditis<br>• Proximal aneurysm (AAA, **popliteal**) | • **Atherosclerotic plaque rupture**<br>• Low flow, dehydration, thrombophilia<br>• **Graft or stent thrombosis** |
| **Prior claudication** | **Absent** — the limb was normal last week | **Present** — known PAD |
| **Contralateral pulses** | ⚑ **Normal** | ⚑ **Also absent/abnormal** — bilateral disease |
| **Collaterals** | **Few** → more profound ischaemia | **Well developed** → limb survives longer |
| **Angiography** | **Sharp cut-off**, meniscus sign, little atheroma elsewhere | **Tapered, irregular** occlusion, diffuse disease, many collaterals |
| **Management** | ⚑ **Fogarty embolectomy** + lifelong anticoagulation, treat the source | ⚑ **Angiography-guided**: thrombolysis, angioplasty or **bypass** — a Fogarty alone fails and may injure a diseased vessel |

| Rutherford | Sensory / motor | Doppler (arterial/venous) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IIa — marginal** | Toes or none / intact | Arterial often inaudible / venous audible | **Salvageable if promptly treated**; time for imaging |
| **IIb — immediate** | More than toes / **weakness** | Arterial usually inaudible / venous audible | ⚑ **Immediate revascularisation — theatre now** |
| **III — irreversible** | **Anaesthetic / paralysis, rigor** | **Both inaudible** | **Primary amputation** |

⚑ Loss of the **venous** Doppler signal is the bedside discriminator between IIb and III; revascularising a dead limb can kill the patient.

**Immediate management:** oxygen, IV access, **opioid analgesia**; **IV unfractionated heparin** bolus then infusion (UFH not LMWH — titratable and reversible before surgery) to limit propagation and protect the runoff; bloods, ECG, **urgent vascular referral**; limb dependent, NBM.

**Revascularisation.** **Embolectomy** — Fogarty catheter via femoral cut-down, feasible under LA in the unfit. **Catheter-directed thrombolysis** — better for thrombotic occlusion and graft thrombosis, but takes **hours**, so **not** for a IIb limb with motor loss. **Bypass** where the lesion needs anatomical correction; **primary amputation** for Rutherford III. Afterwards treat the cause — anticoagulate a cardiac source, echo/Holter, screen for aneurysms.

##### ⚑ Reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome

Restoring flow washes out the products of anaerobic metabolism and cell death:
- **Hyperkalaemia** → peaked T waves, arrhythmia, arrest. Monitor K⁺ and ECG; calcium gluconate, insulin–dextrose, salbutamol, dialysis.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** → myoglobinuria ("Coca-Cola" urine), **AKI**, ↑↑CK; aggressive IV fluids. Also acidosis, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia.
- **Compartment syndrome** — reperfused muscle swells inside rigid fascia: **pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment.** ⚑ **Pulses and capillary refill may be preserved — do not wait for them to disappear.** The **calf** (four compartments, anterior most often) is the site; **urgent four-compartment fasciotomy**, with many units performing **prophylactic fasciotomy** after prolonged ischaemia.

#### 7.4 Abdominal aortic aneurysm

**The short version**
- **AAA = infrarenal aortic diameter ≥3.0 cm** (or ≥1.5× the adjacent segment); >90% infrarenal.
- **Elective repair: ≥5.5 cm in men, ≥5.0 cm in women, growth >1.0 cm/year, or symptomatic.** So **4.2 cm asymptomatic = surveillance, NOT surgery**; **5.8 cm in a man = elective repair**.
- ⚑ **Australia has NO national AAA screening programme** — screening is targeted/opportunistic.
- **Ruptured AAA triad: sudden severe abdominal/back pain + hypotension + pulsatile expansile mass.** ⚑ Frequently **misdiagnosed as renal colic**. **Unstable → straight to theatre, no CT.**

**Aetiology.** Degenerative — medial elastin and collagen loss, transmural inflammation, matrix metalloproteinase activity — not simply "atherosclerosis." ⚑ **Smoking dominates**; also age >65, **male sex** (6× commoner, but ⚑ **women rupture at smaller diameters with worse outcomes**), **family history** (roughly doubles risk), hypertension, COPD. ⚑ **Diabetes is negatively associated** — a genuine exception. Also connective tissue disease, mycotic and inflammatory aneurysms. Most are **asymptomatic**; symptomatic ones give back/flank pain or distal atheroembolism (**"trash foot"**). **A tender AAA is impending rupture until proven otherwise.**

| Maximum diameter | ANZSVS | ESVS (recommended by RACGP) | SVS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.5–2.9 cm (ectatic) | Repeat US ~5 years | — | — |
| **3.0–3.9 cm** | **Every 2–5 years** | **24 months** | **3 years** |
| **4.0–4.4 cm** | **Annually** | **12 months** | **12 months** |
| **4.5–4.9 cm** | **6-monthly** | **6 months** | **12 months** |
| **5.0–5.4 cm** | **3-monthly** | **3 months** | **6-monthly** |
| **≥5.5 cm (men) / ≥5.0 cm (women)** | **Vascular surgical referral for repair** | | |

⚑ **ANZSVS has published no formal surveillance guideline** — RACGP therefore recommends the **ESVS** schedule, and the three columns above sit within one step of each other. If asked for one number, quote ESVS. Rupture below 4 cm is vanishingly rare, which is why the small-aneurysm intervals are long. The **repair thresholds** are consistent: **men ≥5.5 cm; women ≥5.0 cm; growth >1.0 cm/year; symptomatic or embolising at any diameter**; also saccular, mycotic, or iliac aneurysm ≥3.5 cm. Below threshold: surveillance plus **absolute smoking cessation** (the only intervention shown to slow expansion), BP control and statin. ⚑ **No drug reliably reduces AAA growth — doxycycline, beta-blockers and macrolides have all failed.**

**The two exam stems.** *Incidental 4.2 cm AAA, 68-year-old smoker* → **do not refer**; **12-monthly ultrasound** (4.0–4.4 cm is annual on all three schedules) plus hard risk-factor treatment and safety-netting — rupture risk <1%/year, below elective operative mortality. *5.8 cm AAA, 72-year-old man* → **refer for elective repair**, with CTA for EVAR planning and cardiorespiratory risk assessment.

**Screening.** ⚑ **No national Australian programme**, unlike the UK and Sweden (single ultrasound for men at 65); instead **opportunistic targeted** ultrasound (⚑ no dedicated MBS screening item exists) in **men >65 with a smoking history**, anyone **>65 with a family history**, **Marfan or Loeys–Dietz syndrome**, and anyone with a known **popliteal or thoracic aneurysm** (RACGP).

##### Ruptured AAA

**Triad: sudden severe abdominal or back pain + hypotension/collapse + pulsatile expansile mass.** ⚑ **Misdiagnosis is the exam point** — it is mistaken for **renal colic** (identical loin-to-groin pain), diverticulitis, perforated viscus and musculoskeletal back pain. **A first episode of "renal colic" over 50–60, or back pain with haemodynamic disturbance, needs the aorta assessed.** Retroperitoneal rupture may tamponade and present sub-acutely.

**Immediate vascular surgery, anaesthetics and theatre activation**; two large-bore IVs, **urgent cross-match / massive transfusion protocol**, bloods, ECG. ⚑ **Permissive hypotension** — resuscitate to a **palpable radial pulse and preserved cerebration / systolic ~70–90 mmHg — ⚑ **lower than the ~80–90 quoted for blunt trauma (§5.3)**, because the aim is to avoid dislodging clot at the rupture site with maintained consciousness**, since aggressive crystalloid dislodges clot and worsens bleeding; prefer **blood products** and correct coagulopathy. ⚑ **Unstable with a convincing history → straight to theatre, no CT**; **stable** patients get rapid **CTA** for EVAR suitability, then **open repair or emergency EVAR**. Consider **ceiling of care** — operative mortality remains ~40–50%.

**EVAR vs open.** EVAR needs an adequate **infrarenal neck** and iliac access; open repair suits any anatomy and is required for juxta/suprarenal disease. ⚑ EVAR has **lower 30-day mortality** and shorter stay, but ⚑ **the survival advantage is lost by ~2–3 years**, with more reinterventions, persisting late rupture risk and mandatory ⚑ **lifelong imaging surveillance**. Open repair has higher perioperative mortality (MI, AKI, **colonic ischaemia**, spinal cord ischaemia, graft infection) but is **more durable**.

**Endoleak = persistent flow into the sac.** **I** (inadequate proximal/distal seal) and **III** (fabric defect/component separation) → **treat**; **II** (**retrograde lumbar/IMA flow, commonest**) → observe, treat if the sac expands; **IV** (porosity) and **V** (endotension) rarely need action.

##### Thoracic aneurysm and aortic dissection — brief

**TAA** is usually asymptomatic; consider connective tissue disease or bicuspid aortic valve in the young. Thresholds are **around 5.5 cm for degenerative ascending disease, lower (~4.5–5.0 cm) in connective tissue disease or bicuspid valve**. **TEVAR** for descending disease.

**Aortic dissection** — intimal tear, blood tracking into the media to create a false lumen. Abrupt **tearing pain radiating to the interscapular back**, maximal at onset; **>20 mmHg inter-arm BP difference**; pulse deficit; new **aortic regurgitation**; malperfusion (stroke, **inferior MI** from right coronary involvement, paraplegia, mesenteric/renal or limb ischaemia). **CTA** diagnoses.
- ⚑ **Stanford: Type A involves the ascending aorta** (wherever the tear); **Type B does not**. Type A is ~two-thirds and a **surgical emergency** — graft replacement of the ascending aorta ± root/valve; untreated mortality rises ~1–2% per hour early on.
- ⚑ **Uncomplicated Type B = medical "impulse control"**: **IV beta-blocker first** (esmolol/labetalol) to **HR ~60**, then a vasodilator to **systolic ~100–120 mmHg** — reduce **dP/dt**, not just BP. ⚑ **Never give the vasodilator first** (reflex tachycardia extends the dissection). **TEVAR** for complicated type B.

#### 7.5 Carotid disease and stroke prevention

**The short version**
- **Symptomatic** = ipsilateral TIA, amaurosis fugax or non-disabling stroke — ⚑ **the Australian guideline specifies a recent event, <3 months** (6 months is the NASCET/ESVS trial window). **70–99% → carotid endarterectomy, strong benefit; 50–69% → smaller benefit, individualise** (greater in men, older patients, hemispheric events); **<50% → no benefit.**
- ⚑ **Timing: operate once stable, ideally within 2 weeks (14 days) of the index event** — recurrent stroke risk is front-loaded and the NNT rises steeply with delay.
- **CEA is preferred to stenting** in most symptomatic patients, especially over 70.
- **Asymptomatic:** ⚑ **the Australian Stroke Foundation guideline says carotid endarterectomy or stenting should NOT be performed** for asymptomatic stenosis — best medical therapy only. The "intervene in selected higher-risk patients at centres with complication rates <3%" position is US/SVS framing; do not quote it in an Australian paper.

Duplex first, confirmed by **CTA or MRA**; stenosis is graded by **NASCET** (against the distal normal ICA) — ⚑ the older **ECST** method gives higher percentages for the same lesion. A **bruit is neither sensitive nor specific**.

**CEA vs stenting.** ⚑ CEA has **lower periprocedural stroke and death**, particularly over 70; stenting causes less MI and no cranial nerve injury, and suits a **hostile neck** (surgery/radiotherapy), high bifurcation, restenosis after CEA, or severe cardiac comorbidity. Stenting needs **DAPT**; aspirin continues through CEA. All patients get antiplatelet therapy, **high-intensity statin**, BP and diabetes control and smoking cessation.

**Complications of CEA.** **Stroke** (~2–3%) and death. **Cranial nerve injury** — **hypoglossal (XII)**: tongue deviates to the operated side; **vagus/recurrent laryngeal (X)**: hoarseness; **marginal mandibular VII**: drooping mouth corner. ⚑ **An expanding neck haematoma compromises the airway and needs immediate wound opening at the bedside.** Also carotid-sinus haemodynamic instability, MI, restenosis, and ⚑ **cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome** days later (ipsilateral headache, seizures, focal deficit, intracerebral haemorrhage) — **treat with strict BP control**.

#### 7.6 Venous disease

**The short version**
- Varicose veins = dilated tortuous superficial veins from **valvular incompetence and venous hypertension**: **great saphenous** (medial thigh/calf → sapheno-femoral junction, groin) or **small saphenous** (posterior calf → sapheno-popliteal junction).
- Aching **worse at the end of the day, relieved by elevation** — the opposite of arterial pain.
- **Duplex** defines the incompetent junction and **excludes deep venous obstruction** before any superficial vein is destroyed; **endovenous ablation has largely replaced stripping**.
- **CVI skin changes: haemosiderin staining, varicose eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche.**

**Risk factors:** age, female sex, parity, family history, obesity, prolonged standing, previous DVT. **Intervene for:** persistent symptoms despite conservative measures; **CVI skin changes**; **venous ulceration**; **superficial thrombophlebitis**; **bleeding from a varicosity** (elevation and direct pressure). Cosmesis alone is not an indication.

**Treatment.** Conservative: weight loss, calf-pump exercise, elevation, **graduated compression stockings after an ABI check**. **Endovenous thermal ablation** (radiofrequency or laser) under tumescent LA — first-line; alternatives are glue, mechanochemical ablation, **ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy** and **surgery** (SFJ ligation and stripping, phlebectomy) for very large veins. Complications: recurrence, **saphenous nerve injury with GSV below knee, sural nerve with SSV**, DVT.

**Chronic venous insufficiency.** Sustained ambulatory venous hypertension → capillary leak of fibrinogen and red cells → **haemosiderin staining** (brown gaiter pigmentation); **varicose eczema** (itchy scaly weeping dermatitis — watch for contact allergy to topicals); **lipodermatosclerosis** (dermal fibrosis, tight woody tapered calf = ⚑ **"inverted champagne bottle" leg**); **atrophie blanche** (ivory-white atrophic plaques, ulcer-prone); **ulceration at the medial gaiter area**. Graded by **CEAP**. Manage with compression (after ABI), elevation, exercise and emollients, plus ⚑ **early endovenous ablation of superficial reflux, which speeds ulcer healing and reduces recurrence** (EVRA).

**Superficial thrombophlebitis.** Tender erythematous palpable **cord** along a superficial vein. ⚑ Not trivial — it extends into the deep system in a meaningful minority, so **do a duplex**, especially above the knee or near the sapheno-femoral junction. NSAIDs, compression, mobilise; **anticoagulate (prophylactic LMWH or fondaparinux, ~6 weeks) for long-segment (≥5 cm) or junction-proximal disease**. Recurrent migratory thrombophlebitis (**Trousseau's sign**) → seek occult malignancy.

#### 7.7 Leg ulcers

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Venous ~70%, arterial ~10%, neuropathic ~10%** — but **assume mixed until the ABI says otherwise**.
- ⚑ **Compression treats venous ulcers and is dangerous in arterial disease — NEVER compress without measuring the ABI first.**
- **Any ulcer unhealed at ~3 months, or with heaped rolled everted edges, gets biopsied** — Marjolin's.

| Feature | **Venous** | **Arterial** | **Neuropathic (diabetic)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Site** | **Medial gaiter area**, above medial malleolus | **Pressure/end points** — toes, heel, lateral malleolus, bony prominences | **Plantar pressure points** — **metatarsal heads**, heel, under callus |
| **Edge and depth** | Sloping, irregular, **shallow** | **Punched-out, well-demarcated, deep** | **Punched-out with thick surrounding callus; deep** |
| **Base** | Granulating, sloughy, exudative | **Pale, grey, necrotic**; may expose tendon/bone | Deeper than it looks — **probe it** |
| **Pain** | Aching, **relieved by elevation** | **Severe, worse on elevation and at night, eased by dependency** | ⚑ **Painless** — the diagnostic clue |
| **Surrounding skin** | Haemosiderin, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche | Cool, shiny, hairless; dependent rubor | **Callus**, dry cracked skin, deformity (clawed toes, **Charcot**) |
| **Pulses** | **Present** | **Absent/reduced** | Often present — **may be absent (neuroischaemic)** |
| **ABI** | **>0.8** | **<0.8, usually <0.5** | ⚑ **May be falsely normal or >1.4** — use **TBI** |
| **Management** | ⚑ **Graduated multilayer compression**, elevation, **early ablation of superficial reflux** | ⚑ **Revascularise — no compression.** Analgesia, wound care, risk factors | **Offloading** (⚑ **non-removable knee-high device first-line**), debride callus, glycaemic control, MDT foot service |

**The compression safety rule** — write it out in any ulcer answer. **Measure the ABI with Doppler in both legs before any compression.** **≥0.8** takes full compression (~40 mmHg at the ankle, multilayer); **below 0.8** Wounds Australia does **not** authorise routine compression — refer for vascular assessment and specialist-supervised reduced compression at most; ⚑ international guidelines put the hard contraindication anywhere from **<0.5 to <0.8**, so the safe answer in a written paper is **compress above 0.8, refer below it**; **>1.40 (§7.1), or diabetes/CKD**, means the reading is unreliable, so get toe pressures. Re-measure periodically. ⚑ **Antibiotics only for clinical infection; never treat a colonised ulcer.**

**Marjolin's ulcer.** ⚑ **Squamous cell carcinoma arising in chronically inflamed or scarred skin** — long-standing venous ulcers, burn scars, sinus tracts, chronic osteomyelitis. Suspect on **heaped rolled everted edges, exuberant granulation, or failure to heal**; **biopsy the margin, not the necrotic centre**. Also consider **pyoderma gangrenosum** (violaceous undermined border, pathergy — ⚑ **do not debride**).

#### 7.8 The diabetic foot

**The short version**
- **Neuropathy + ischaemia + infection** — **neuropathy is the permissive lesion**, removing the pain that would force protection; it is **sensory**, **motor** (clawing → new pressure points) and **autonomic** (dry cracked skin, AV shunting).
- ⚑ **ABI is unreliable in diabetes** — use **toe pressure/TBI**. **Screening intervals shorten with risk**, from annually at very low risk to every 1–3 months at high risk.
- ⚑ **Charcot: a hot, red, swollen, relatively painless foot with intact skin and bounding pulses** — immobilise; it is not cellulitis.

**Pathophysiology.** Sensory neuropathy removes protective sensation, so repetitive trauma goes unnoticed. Motor neuropathy wastes the intrinsics → **clawed toes, prominent metatarsal heads** → focal pressure → callus → subcallus haemorrhage → ulcer. Autonomic neuropathy → **dry fissured skin** and AV shunting that makes a poorly perfused foot feel deceptively warm. **PAD** (infrapopliteal, diffuse, calcified) impairs healing and antibiotic delivery; infection then spreads along tendon sheaths.

**Assessment.** **10 g monofilament** at defined plantar sites (inability to feel it = **loss of protective sensation**), 128 Hz tuning fork, ankle reflexes; pulses with **ABI and TBI/toe pressures** — ⚑ never the ABI alone, and **ankle pressure <50 mmHg, ABI <0.5 or toe pressure <30 mmHg → urgent vascular imaging/revascularisation**. Inspect skin, interdigital spaces, nails, deformity and footwear; ⚑ **previous ulcer or amputation is the strongest predictor of a future ulcer**. **Screen and prevent:** risk-stratify at least annually — **very low risk annually; low 6–12 monthly; moderate 3–6 monthly; high risk (previous ulcer/amputation, ESKD, severe deformity/PAD) every 1–3 months** — with education (daily self-inspection, never barefoot), **therapeutic footwear**, podiatry and risk-factor control.

**Charcot neuroarthropathy.** Unrecognised microfracture in an insensate, well-perfused foot triggers uncontrolled inflammation and bone resorption, then collapse → the **rocker-bottom foot**.

| | **Acute Charcot** | **Infection / osteomyelitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Skin | ⚑ **Intact — no wound, no portal of entry** | Ulcer, wound or fissure present |
| Pain / temperature / elevation | Disproportionately **mild**; ⚑ **>2 °C hotter than the other foot**; ⚑ **redness resolves on elevation** | More painful; warm; redness **persists** |
| Bloods / X-ray | CRP/WCC normal or mildly raised; film early normal, later fragmentation and midfoot collapse | Fever; CRP/ESR/WCC raised (blunted in diabetes); cortical erosion, periosteal reaction, sequestrum |
| Treatment | ⚑ **Immediate offloading in a total contact cast / non-removable knee-high device for months**, until temperature normalises; failure to immobilise causes irreversible deformity | Antibiotics ± drainage/debridement |

MRI distinguishes them if uncertain.

**Osteomyelitis.** Suspect in any ulcer that is deep, overlies bone, has lasted >2 weeks or fails to heal; a **"sausage toe"** is classic. ⚑ **Probe-to-bone test:** a **hard gritty** surface on advancing a sterile blunt probe is positive, and in a deep infected ulcer largely **rules in** bone. ⚑ **First-line workup: probe-to-bone + ESR/CRP + plain radiograph**; films lag by ~2 weeks, so **repeat in 2–4 weeks** if suspicion persists, and ⚑ **MRI is the imaging test of choice** if still uncertain. **Bone biopsy and culture** is the gold standard — superficial swabs must not direct antibiotics. Treat with prolonged targeted antibiotics ± resection of infected bone, and ⚑ **revascularise first if ischaemic — antibiotics do not reach tissue with no blood supply.**

**Management principle.** ⚑ **Multidisciplinary "high-risk foot service"** — strong evidence for reducing amputation, and the answer the examiner wants: physician, vascular surgeon, podiatrist, wound/diabetes nurse, orthotist, infectious diseases and orthopaedic surgeon, with rapid access to revascularisation. Concurrently **offload, debride** (⚑ not in ischaemic or dry gangrenous feet), **treat infection, revascularise and control glycaemia and cardiovascular risk**.

#### 7.9 Vascular access and other

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Fistula first: autologous AV fistula > AV graft > tunnelled central venous catheter** — best patency, least infection. **Radiocephalic (wrist) before brachiocephalic (elbow) before brachiobasilic transposition**; fistulae need **weeks to months to mature**. ⚑ **Never take a BP, venepuncture or cannula from a fistula arm.**

**AV fistula.** The anastomosis **arterialises** the vein, which dilates and thickens for repeated large-bore cannulation. A healthy fistula has a **palpable thrill and continuous machinery bruit**; ⚑ a **pulsatile fistula with only a systolic bruit suggests outflow stenosis**. Complications: failure to mature; **stenosis and thrombosis** (commonest cause of access loss — rising venous pressures on dialysis, prolonged bleeding after needle removal, loss of the thrill; treat by fistuloplasty); ⚑ **steal syndrome** (cold, painful, pale hand, worse **during dialysis** — banding, DRIL or ligation); ⚑ **high-output cardiac failure**; cannulation-site aneurysm risking catastrophic haemorrhage; infection.

**Central venous access.** *Insertion:* ⚑ **pneumothorax** (subclavian > IJ; ⚑ **ultrasound guidance is now standard and markedly reduces mechanical complications**), arterial puncture, haemothorax, air embolism, guidewire arrhythmia, malposition, thoracic duct injury, retained guidewire. *Delayed:* **catheter-related bloodstream infection** (avoid femoral; strict asepsis; remove the line once not needed), thrombosis, central vein stenosis. **Confirm the tip at the cavo-atrial junction and exclude pneumothorax before use.**

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Buttock/thigh claudication + erectile dysfunction + absent femoral pulses | **Leriche syndrome** — aorto-iliac occlusion at the bifurcation |
| ABI diagnostic of PAD; ABI >1.40 in a diabetic | **≤0.90**; >1.40 = **incompressible**, so use **TBI** (PAD if **<0.70**) |
| Foot pain worse lying flat, eased by hanging the leg out of bed | **Ischaemic rest pain** → CLTI (⚑ replaces "critical limb ischaemia") |
| The two of the six Ps marking a threatened limb | **Paraesthesia and paralysis** |
| ALI, sudden onset, AF, **normal** contralateral pulses, no claudication | **Embolic** → Fogarty **embolectomy** + anticoagulation (**thrombotic** = absent contralateral pulses, prior claudication → thrombolysis/bypass) |
| Peaked T waves, dark urine, ↑↑CK after revascularisation | **Reperfusion injury**; check for **compartment syndrome** → fasciotomy |
| Elective AAA repair thresholds (AAA = aorta ≥3.0 cm) | **≥5.5 cm men, ≥5.0 cm women, growth >1.0 cm/yr, or symptomatic** |
| Incidental asymptomatic 4.2 cm AAA | **12-monthly ultrasound + risk factor therapy**, no operation. ⚑ **No national Australian screening programme** |
| Sudden back pain + hypotension + pulsatile mass, unstable | **Ruptured AAA → theatre, no CT**; permissive hypotension (~70–90 systolic); often misread as **renal colic** |
| Commonest endoleak after EVAR | **Type II** (lumbar/IMA) — observe; **I and III need treatment**. Tongue deviating to the operated side after CEA = **hypoglossal (XII)** injury |
| Uncomplicated type B dissection — drug order | ⚑ **Beta-blocker (HR ~60) FIRST, then vasodilator**; type A → theatre |
| Symptomatic 70–99% carotid stenosis — treatment, timing | **Endarterectomy, ideally within 2 weeks (14 days)** of the event |
| ABI below which compression is contraindicated | ⚑ **<0.5** (full compression safe **≥0.8**; **0.5–0.8** modified only) |
| Leg ulcer unhealed at ~3 months, rolled everted edges | **Biopsy the margin — Marjolin's ulcer (SCC)** |
| Hot, swollen, red, painless foot, skin intact, redness settles on elevation | **Acute Charcot** → total contact cast, not antibiotics |
| Suspected diabetic foot osteomyelitis — first tests | **Probe-to-bone + ESR/CRP + plain film**; **MRI** if uncertain |
| Cold painful hand during dialysis in a fistula arm | **Steal syndrome** |
