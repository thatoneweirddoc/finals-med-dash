## The Surgical Acute Abdomen and Bowel Obstruction


### Surgery Master Notes — The Surgical Acute Abdomen and Bowel Obstruction

*Sources: WSES (adhesive SBO 2017, mesenteric ischaemia 2022), CODA, AVOD/DIABOLO and the van Dijk IPD meta-analysis, ACP 2022, NICE NG147, Cancer Council Australia Optimal Care Pathway and surveillance colonoscopy guidelines, National Bowel Cancer Screening Program, eviQ, Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic, RACS. Scope: surgical decision-making in the acute abdomen, appendicitis, perforation, diverticulitis, obstruction, colorectal cancer and intestinal ischaemia. Pancreatitis, biliary disease, PUD, GI bleeding, IBD and coeliac disease → see Gastroenterology.*

#### 3.1 Approach to the acute abdomen

**The short version**
- **Visceral** pain is dull and midline; **parietal** pain is sharp and localised. **Migration** means inflammation has reached the parietal peritoneum — the most useful single item in the history.
- **Peritonism** is the sign, **peritonitis** the diagnosis; rigidity is involuntary and is the hard sign. ⚑ **Percussion tenderness has replaced deep rebound** — same information, better tolerated, more reliable.
- Exclude the **mimics** — MI, DKA, lower lobe pneumonia, ruptured ectopic, testicular torsion, ruptured AAA — and do a **βhCG in every woman of reproductive age**.

**Pain character.** *Visceral* (C fibres, bilateral autonomic afferents) — dull, colicky, patient writhes, felt **midline only**: foregut epigastric, midgut periumbilical, hindgut suprapubic. *Parietal* (A-δ, segmental somatic) — sharp, constant, patient lies still, over the inflamed peritoneum. *Referred* — diaphragm to the **shoulder tip (C3–5)**, ureteric loin-to-groin, biliary to the right scapula.

| Region | Surgical | Medical / other |
|---|---|---|
| **RUQ** | Cholecystitis, cholangitis (→ Gastro); duodenal perforation | Hepatitis; RLL pneumonia; Fitz-Hugh–Curtis |
| **Epigastric** | Perforated ulcer; pancreatitis (→ Gastro); **ruptured AAA** | **Inferior MI**; DKA; gastritis |
| **RIF** | **Appendicitis**; caecal Ca; mesenteric adenitis; Meckel's; ileal Crohn's | **Ruptured ectopic**, ovarian torsion, PID; **testicular torsion** |
| **Periumbilical** | **Early appendicitis**; SBO; **mesenteric ischaemia**; **AAA** | Gastroenteritis; DKA |
| **LIF** | **Diverticulitis**; sigmoid Ca; sigmoid volvulus | Ectopic, ovarian pathology; ureteric colic |
| **Diffuse** | **Perforation**; **mesenteric ischaemia**; advanced obstruction | DKA; hypercalcaemia |

**Signs.** Voluntary guarding is overcome by distraction; **involuntary guarding** is not, and means true peritoneal irritation. **Rigidity** is board-like and sustained — the hardest sign, suggesting free contamination. ⚑ **Percussion tenderness** gives the same information as rebound but is better tolerated and more reliable, rebound having poor inter-observer agreement and many false positives. RUQ pain and a positive βhCG both go to **ultrasound first**. **Never omit** hernial orifices, the scrotum, and PR.

**Mimics to exclude actively.** **Inferior MI** (epigastric pain, diaphoresis, often no chest pain — **ECG and troponin mandatory** over ~40); **DKA** (Kussmaul breathing, ketotic fetor); **lower lobe pneumonia** (commonest in children); **ruptured ectopic** (amenorrhoea, syncope, shoulder tip pain); **testicular torsion** (examine the scrotum, then theatre); **ruptured AAA**. Also hypercalcaemia, Addisonian crisis, porphyria.

**Investigations.** Bedside — obs, **βhCG**, urinalysis, BSL, **ECG**, VBG with lactate. Bloods — FBC, U&E, LFT, lipase, CRP, coags, group & hold (a normal WCC/CRP does not exclude early appendicitis). Imaging by presentation:

| Presentation | Imaging |
|---|---|
| ?Perforation | **Erect CXR** — sensitivity only ~**70–80%**, worse if poorly erect or small-volume, so **a normal film does not exclude perforation** → CT |
| RIF pain in a **child, young woman or pregnancy** | **Ultrasound** — assesses adnexa, no radiation. **A non-visualised appendix is non-diagnostic, not negative** |
| Adult male/older patient, equivocal US, or a sick undifferentiated adult | **CT abdo-pelvis with contrast** — the default; **CT angiography** if ischaemia suspected |

⚑ **AXR is not a screening test for abdominal pain** — its narrow uses are suspected obstruction, volvulus and following known pseudo-obstruction.

#### 3.2 Appendicitis

**The short version**
- Luminal obstruction → distension → **visceral periumbilical pain** → mucosal ischaemia and bacterial invasion → transmural inflammation → **localised RIF parietal pain**. That sequence *is* the history.
- **Anorexia → pain → vomiting**; vomiting before pain should make you doubt the diagnosis.
- ⚑ **US first in children, young women and pregnancy**; CT where US is negative or equivocal.
- ⚑ **Appendicectomy remains standard in Australia**; antibiotics-first is an accepted option in *selected* uncomplicated cases, and **an appendicolith predicts antibiotic failure**.

**Eponymous signs.** **McBurney's** — maximal tenderness ⅓ along the ASIS–umbilicus line. **Rovsing's** — LIF palpation reproduces RIF pain. **Psoas** — pain on passive right hip extension (**retrocaecal**). **Obturator** — pain on internal rotation of the flexed right hip (**pelvic**).

**Alvarado score (MANTRELS).** Two points each for **RIF tenderness** and **leucocytosis**; one each for **M**igration, **A**norexia, **N**ausea/vomiting, **R**ebound, **E**levated temperature, **S**hift to the left. Total 10; ≤3–4 reasonably excludes, ≥7 supports, and the middle band is where imaging earns its place.

| Atypical group | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Retrocaecal** (commonest) | Caecum shields the parietal peritoneum → mild anterior tenderness, flank pain, **psoas sign**; mislabelled renal colic |
| **Pelvic** | Irritates rectum and bladder → diarrhoea, tenesmus, urinary frequency, suprapubic pain, soft abdomen; mistaken for gastroenteritis/UTI. **Obturator sign** |
| **Pregnancy** | Physiological leucocytosis and nausea are baseline; the uterus limits examination. ⚑ The classical (Baer) teaching of upward migration to the RUQ is **overstated — modern MRI shows ~1 cm displacement and McBurney's point remains the best guide**; say the pain is *often* higher and less localised, but don't quote a trimester map. **US → MRI**, and **operate on suspicion** — perforation harms the fetus far more than a negative appendicectomy |
| **Elderly** | Blunted pain and inflammatory response → much **higher perforation rate**; CT first; consider caecal cancer as the cause |
| **Very young (<5)** | Cannot localise, short omentum cannot wall off → vomiting, fever, **often already perforated** |

**Investigation (Australia).** βhCG, FBC, CRP, U&E, urinalysis (mild pyuria/haematuria from ureteric irritation does not exclude appendicitis). **US first** in children, young women and pregnancy — a non-compressible blind-ending tube >6 mm with periappendiceal fluid. **CT with IV contrast** if US is equivocal or an alternative diagnosis is plausible; **MRI** in pregnancy. **Diagnostic laparoscopy** is legitimate when suspicion is high and imaging equivocal.

**Antibiotics vs appendicectomy.** **CODA** (~1552 US adults, uncomplicated appendicitis including appendicoliths) found a 10-day antibiotic course **non-inferior for 30-day general health status**, but the practical numbers matter more: **~29% had appendicectomy by 90 days, ~40% by one year and ~50% by 3–4 years**, with more ED re-presentations; with an **appendicolith**, ~41% by 90 days and more serious complications. ⚑ **Australian position:** laparoscopic appendicectomy remains standard; antibiotics-first is reasonable in **selected** patients (CT-confirmed uncomplicated disease, no appendicolith, no perforation/abscess, not septic, immunosuppressed or pregnant) after consent that **about half eventually need the operation**.

**Complications.** *Perforation* → resuscitate, IV antibiotics, **urgent appendicectomy with lavage**. *Appendix mass/phlegmon* (~5+ day history, tender RIF mass, stable) → ⚑ **non-operative first**, with **colonic imaging afterwards if >40** to exclude caecal cancer or Crohn's; interval appendicectomy is selective. *Abscess* → antibiotics plus **percutaneous drainage** if ≳3–4 cm.

#### 3.3 Perforated viscus

**The short version**
- Sudden, severe, **constant** pain with **rigidity** = perforation until disproved.
- Causes: **perforated peptic ulcer** (→ Gastro), **perforated diverticulitis**, malignancy, ischaemic or obstructed bowel, iatrogenic, trauma.
- **Erect CXR misses up to a third** → if suspicion persists, CT.
- The operation is **source control**, not elegance.

Abrupt onset (patients name the minute), worse on movement, patient lies rigidly still; board-like rigidity, tachycardia, then septic shock. **The elderly and immunosuppressed may perforate with few signs.** Take the erect CXR after ~10–15 minutes sitting up; **CT is definitive** and identifies the site and cause.

**Management.** Resuscitate — large-bore access, crystalloid, analgesia, NG tube, IDC, **broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Gram-negatives and anaerobes (Therapeutic Guidelines)**, HDU/ICU. **Source control** = washout plus repair: perforated duodenal ulcer → **omental (Graham) patch**; perforated colon → resect the diseased segment, with **a stoma if unstable or grossly contaminated** and anastomosis for the stable fit patient. Use **damage control** (resect, leave discontinuous, temporary closure, return after ICU resuscitation) in the physiologically deranged. Non-operative management only for the **sealed, contained** perforation.

#### 3.4 Diverticular disease and diverticulitis

**The short version**
- **Diverticulosis** = pouches exist; **diverticular disease** = they cause symptoms; **diverticulitis** = they are acutely inflamed.
- ⚑ **Left-sided (sigmoid) in Western populations; right-sided in Asian populations** — easily misdiagnosed as appendicitis.
- ⚑ **Uncomplicated diverticulitis in a well, immunocompetent patient is increasingly managed WITHOUT antibiotics.**
- **CT-diagnosed diverticulitis warrants interval colonoscopy** — cancer imitates it exactly. Abscess → **percutaneous drainage**; peritonitis → theatre.

Pulsion diverticula herniate through the muscularis where the vasa recta penetrate, driven by raised intraluminal pressure and low fibre; inflammation follows micro-perforation of an obstructed diverticulum. **Presentation:** constant LIF pain over days, fever, altered bowel habit, localised tenderness ± mass, raised WCC/CRP. Diagnose on **CT with contrast**; **colonoscopy is contraindicated acutely.**

**Hinchey classification (in my own words)** — severity of *perforated* diverticular disease, graded by what has escaped the bowel and where it went:

| Stage | What has happened | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Inflammation confined next to the colon — small **pericolic** phlegmon/abscess | Antibiotics; drain if reachable |
| **II** | **Larger walled-off abscess further away** — pelvic, retroperitoneal | Antibiotics + **percutaneous drainage** |
| **III** | Abscess has burst giving **generalised purulent peritonitis**, defect sealed | **Surgery** — Hartmann's, or resection with anastomosis in the fit |
| **IV** | Unsealed perforation with **faecal peritonitis** | **Emergency Hartmann's**; high mortality |

⚑ **Antibiotics in uncomplicated disease.** **AVOD** and **DIABOLO** randomised CT-confirmed uncomplicated diverticulitis to observation vs antibiotics and found no difference in recovery, progression, recurrence or resection; the pooled individual-patient meta-analysis (~1100 patients) confirmed this at one year and found **no subgroup that benefited** — including higher pain scores, WCC >13.5 or prior episodes. NICE NG147 and Australian practice have followed. **"Uncomplicated" = all of:** no abscess, no free gas, no fistula, no obstruction, no peritonitis on CT. **Antibiotics and admission ARE needed if** systemically unwell; **complicated** on CT; **immunosuppressed**; comorbid, frail or pregnant; unable to tolerate oral intake; or no improvement at 48–72 h — covering Gram-negatives and anaerobes per **Therapeutic Guidelines** (amoxicillin + gentamicin + metronidazole IV, then amoxicillin–clavulanate).

**Complications.** *Abscess* — antibiotics plus **percutaneous drainage** if ≳3–4 cm. *Peritonitis* — laparotomy: **Hartmann's** if unstable, contaminated or comorbid, **primary anastomosis ± defunctioning ileostomy** increasingly preferred in the fit; ⚑ **laparoscopic lavage without resection is not recommended as routine practice**, though WSES allows an individualised role in selected Hinchey III. *Colovesical fistula* — **pneumaturia and faecaluria** ± polymicrobial UTIs; elective resection with bladder repair. *Stricture* — **distinguish from malignant stricture**. *Bleeding* — painless and brisk (→ Gastro).

⚑ **Interval colonoscopy.** Cancer produces a CT appearance indistinguishable from diverticulitis, so **book colonoscopy ~6–8 weeks after the episode settles** unless a good-quality one was very recent. *Source disagreement:* ACP restricts this to **complicated** diverticulitis (where cancer prevalence is severalfold higher); most surgical guidance and Australian practice scopes after any CT-proven episode.

**Past exam question — 58-year-old, LIF pain, fever, raised inflammatory markers, CT findings.** Structure: **diagnosis** = acute sigmoid diverticulitis → **classify** uncomplicated vs complicated on CT (abscess? free gas? fistula? obstruction?) → **management**: analgesia, oral intake as tolerated, and if well and uncomplicated, **observation without antibiotics** with safety-netting and 48–72 h review; antibiotics if the criteria above are met; drainage for an abscess; **and in every case interval colonoscopy at 6–8 weeks** — the mark candidates drop.

#### 3.5 Bowel obstruction

**The short version**
- **SBO**: adhesions > hernia > malignancy. **LBO**: malignancy > volvulus > diverticular stricture. Vomiting **early** in SBO and **late** in LBO; absolute constipation **late** in SBO, **early** in LBO.
- **Valvulae conniventes** cross the full lumen; **haustra** do not. **3-6-9 rule**: small bowel >3 cm, colon >6 cm, caecum >9 cm.
- ⚑ **Colicky pain becoming CONSTANT with tachycardia, fever, localised tenderness and rising lactate = STRANGULATION → theatre now**; **closed-loop obstruction** will not resolve conservatively.

| Feature | **Small bowel** | **Large bowel** |
|---|---|---|
| Causes | • **Adhesions (~60–75%)** • **Hernia** • Malignancy • Crohn's stricture • Gallstone ileus, intussusception | • **Colorectal carcinoma** • **Volvulus** (sigmoid > caecal) • Diverticular stricture • Impaction, pseudo-obstruction |
| **Vomiting** | **Early**; proximal copious bilious, distal later and **faeculent** | **Late**, sometimes absent |
| **Distension** | Proportional to level | **Marked** |
| **Constipation** | **Late** | **Early — the presenting symptom** |
| **Plain film** | **Central** loops; **valvulae conniventes span the whole lumen**; short fluid levels | **Peripheral** loops; **haustra are incomplete and do not cross**; fewer, longer levels |
| **Diameter** | **>3 cm** | **>6 cm**; **caecum >9 cm** |

**Closed loop and strangulation.** A **closed loop** is obstruction at two points, so neither proximal decompression nor distal emptying relieves it — volvulus, a hernia trapping a loop, adhesive bands, and **LBO with a competent ileocaecal valve** (the caecum, having the largest diameter, perforates first by Laplace's law). Rising pressure exceeds venous then arterial pressure → **strangulation** → necrosis → perforation. Simple obstruction gives **colicky pain with pain-free intervals** and a soft abdomen. ⚑ **Strangulation is signalled by pain becoming constant and unremitting, with fever, tachycardia, localised tenderness with guarding and rising lactate**; CT shows wall thickening, **reduced or absent mural enhancement**, mesenteric oedema, a whirl sign, free fluid, pneumatosis or portal venous gas. **The trap:** lactate and peritonism are *late*.

**Management.** NBM, **NG tube on free drainage**, **IV resuscitation** with careful potassium replacement, IDC, analgesia, VTE prophylaxis, lactate, and **CT with contrast** to define level, cause and ischaemia. The conservative trial (**"drip and suck"**) suits **adhesive SBO** without peritonism, strangulation or a closed loop: WSES considers **up to ~72 hours** safe. A **water-soluble contrast (Gastrografin) challenge** is diagnostic and mildly therapeutic — ⚑ **if contrast has not reached the colon at 24 hours, non-operative management is very likely to fail** → theatre.

**Absolute indications for surgery:** peritonitis, strangulation or ischaemia; **closed-loop obstruction** on CT; **irreducible/strangulated hernia**; complete obstruction needing resection; perforation; failed conservative management or contrast challenge; ⚑ **first-presentation SBO with no previous abdominal surgery** — the cause is not adhesions, so do not "trial and see". In **LBO**, an obstructing left-sided cancer may be resected, defunctioned, or **stented as a bridge to elective one-stage surgery** or palliation; right-sided tumours go straight to right hemicolectomy.

**Volvulus.** *Sigmoid* — commoner; **elderly, institutionalised, constipated, on neuropsychiatric medication**; AXR shows the **"coffee bean"**, a huge loop from the pelvis pointing to the **RUQ** with loss of haustra (whirl sign on CT). ⚑ **Endoscopic decompression first** if there is no ischaemia or peritonitis, but **recurrence is ~50–60%, so fit patients get semi-elective sigmoid colectomy on the same admission**. *Caecal* — **younger** patients with a mobile caecum; the dilated caecum sits in the **LUQ** with an **SBO pattern behind it**. ⚑ **Straight to theatre** for **right hemicolectomy** — endoscopic decompression is unreliable.

**Other causes.** **Adhesions** — commonest SBO cause; risk rises with each laparotomy. **Gallstone ileus** — a stone erodes through a **cholecystoduodenal fistula** and impacts at the terminal ileum; **Rigler's triad = pneumobilia + SBO + an ectopic gallstone**; elderly women, treated by enterolithotomy. **Adult intussusception** — ⚑ unlike in children, **almost always has a pathological lead point** (tumour, polyp, lipoma, Meckel's), so **resect rather than simply reduce**. **Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie's)** — massive colonic dilatation **without mechanical obstruction** in the unwell, immobile or post-operative patient (orthopaedic and cardiac surgery, sepsis, electrolytes, opioids, anticholinergics). **CT first to exclude a mechanical cause**, then conservative measures (treat the precipitant, stop opioids/anticholinergics, correct K⁺/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺, NG, mobilise, flatus tube, serial caecal measurement); ⚑ **neostigmine 2 mg IV over ~3–5 minutes** if that fails at ~24–48 h, with **cardiac monitoring and atropine available**, contraindicated in mechanical obstruction, perforation, bradycardia, bronchospasm and renal impairment; then **colonoscopic decompression**, surgery last. ⚑ **Caecal diameter is the number: >9 cm defines it; >12 cm, or persistence beyond ~48–72 h, mandates escalation.**

#### 3.6 Colorectal cancer — the surgical angle

**The short version**
- **Right-sided** → **iron-deficiency anaemia** and weight loss. **Left-sided** → altered bowel habit and obstruction. **Rectal** → fresh bleeding, **tenesmus**, urgency.
- ⚑ **IDA in any man or post-menopausal woman is colorectal cancer until proven otherwise.**
- ⚑ **NBCSP**: **iFOBT**, **2-yearly**, automatic for ages **50–74**; since **1 July 2024** those aged **45–49 may request** a first kit.
- **CEA is for monitoring, never diagnosis or screening**; **MRI rectum** drives neoadjuvant decisions, while colon cancer usually goes straight to resection.

**Presentation by site.** *Right colon/caecum* — wide lumen and liquid stool, so the tumour bleeds slowly rather than obstructing: **iron-deficiency anaemia**, weight loss, RIF mass, late presentation. *Left colon/sigmoid* — narrow lumen, formed stool, annular "apple-core" growth: **change in bowel habit**, **obstruction**, PR bleeding mixed with stool. *Rectum* — **fresh PR bleeding**, **tenesmus**, urgency, palpable on PR.

⚑ **Red flags mandating urgent referral (Australia).** Refer for **colonoscopy** (Optimal Care Pathway target: within about four weeks) for a **positive iFOBT** (never repeat it — scope it); **rectal bleeding**; **unexplained iron-deficiency anaemia**; **persistent change in bowel habit**; an **unexplained abdominal or rectal mass**; or **unexplained weight loss**.

**National Bowel Cancer Screening Program.** Free **iFOBT** home kit, **every 2 years**, mailed automatically to ages **50–74**. ⚑ From **1 July 2024** ages **45–49** are eligible but must **request** their first kit — they are not mailed one unsolicited — after which they are enrolled automatically. **A positive result means colonoscopy, not a repeat iFOBT.** It is not for symptomatic patients or those already on surveillance.

**Diagnosis and staging.** **Colonoscopy with biopsy** is the gold standard and clears the colon for synchronous tumours; if an obstructing tumour prevents completion, finish it **3–6 months post-operatively**. **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** for distant staging. **MRI rectum** is mandatory for rectal tumours — it defines **T stage, nodes, extramural venous invasion and the circumferential resection margin (CRM)**, and the CRM drives neoadjuvant decisions. ⚑ **CEA** — take a **pre-operative baseline** and use it for **surveillance**; it is neither sensitive nor specific, so never use it to diagnose or exclude. **MMR/MSI testing** is prognostic, guides immunotherapy and screens for Lynch.

**Operations by site.** Caecum/ascending → **right hemicolectomy**; hepatic flexure → **extended right hemicolectomy**; splenic flexure/descending → **left hemicolectomy**; sigmoid → **high anterior resection**; upper–mid rectum → **anterior resection with total mesorectal excision (TME)**; low rectum with sphincters spared → **low anterior resection + TME** ± defunctioning ileostomy; sphincters involved → **abdominoperineal resection with end colostomy**; obstructed left-sided → **Hartmann's** or a stent. Principles: adequate margins, **high vascular ligation with en-bloc lymphadenectomy (≥12 nodes)**, and **TME** — sharp dissection along the mesorectal fascial plane.

**Neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer.** The rectum sits in a bony pelvis with a tight CRM, so downstaging matters as it does not for colon cancer. **MRI-defined locally advanced disease (T3 with threatened CRM, T4, or node-positive)** receives **long-course chemoradiotherapy** (~5 weeks with a fluoropyrimidine radiosensitiser, surgery after ~8–12 weeks) or **short-course radiotherapy** (5 fractions). ⚑ **Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT)** is increasingly standard for high-risk disease, and complete clinical responders may be considered for **watch-and-wait**. **Colon cancer receives adjuvant, not neoadjuvant, chemotherapy.**

**Surveillance after curative resection.** Clinical review, **CEA**, imaging and colonoscopy for ~5 years. The Australian colonoscopy schedule is **1 year** post-resection (unless a complete perioperative colonoscopy was done), then **~6 years**, then **~11 years**.

**Hereditary syndromes.** **Lynch (HNPCC)** — autosomal dominant **MMR** mutation (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2); **few polyps but rapid adenoma–carcinoma progression**, **right-sided**, young onset, metachronous tumours, with extracolonic risk led by **endometrial** cancer, then ovarian, gastric and urothelial. eviQ: **colonoscopy 1–2 yearly from 25** (MLH1/MSH2) or **from 35** (MSH6/PMS2), **low-dose aspirin 100–300 mg daily actively considered**, and **risk-reducing hysterectomy ± BSO** after childbearing (~40; ~50 for PMS2). **FAP** — autosomal dominant **APC**; **hundreds to thousands** of adenomas from adolescence and **~100% cancer risk untreated**, plus duodenal cancer, desmoids and CHRPE. eviQ: **annual colonoscopy from age 12** (18–20 if attenuated) until colectomy, **upper GI surveillance from 25**, and **prophylactic proctocolectomy with IPAA or colectomy with IRA**, with lifelong surveillance of the retained rectum or pouch.

**Past exam questions.** *60-year-old, weeks of loose stools and intermittent PR bleeding* — change in bowel habit plus bleeding over 50 is a **red flag**: history, **rectal examination**, FBC, iron studies, **CEA baseline** and **urgent colonoscopy** — not an iFOBT, not laxatives. *65-year-old, change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss* — colorectal cancer until excluded; **IDA in a man or post-menopausal woman needs colonoscopy AND gastroscopy**, then staging CT, MRI if rectal, and MDT. The trap is treating the anaemia without scoping.

#### 3.7 Mesenteric ischaemia

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Pain out of proportion to examination findings** — agonising pain with a soft abdomen in an arteriopath or a patient in AF.
- Four mechanisms: **embolic, thrombotic, non-occlusive (NOMI), venous**.
- **Lactate is a late marker** — a normal lactate does not exclude it.
- **Biphasic CT angiography** immediately: **each ~6 hours of delay roughly doubles mortality**, which is broadly 50%+ when delayed.

| Type | Share | Patient and key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Arterial embolic** | ~25% | **AF**, recent MI, valve disease. **Sudden**; lodges in the **SMA** just distal to the middle colic origin, sparing proximal jejunum |
| **Arterial thrombotic** | ~40% | Atherosclerosis, often preceded by **chronic mesenteric ischaemia**. Occludes at the **SMA origin** — large infarct territory |
| **NOMI** | ~25% | **Shock, vasopressors, sepsis, dialysis, cardiac failure** — vasoconstriction with patent vessels; insidious in the ICU patient. Treat the low-flow state |
| **Venous** | <10% | **Younger**; thrombophilia, malignancy, portal hypertension, OCP. Insidious over days, better prognosis, often **anticoagulation alone** |

Severe, poorly localised, constant central pain with early vomiting and diarrhoea, but an initially **soft, non-tender abdomen** because ischaemia is mucosal and has not reached the parietal peritoneum. **Peritonism, distension, shock and high lactate are LATE and mean transmural infarction.** Raised amylase can mislead toward pancreatitis. On **biphasic CTA**: arterial filling defects, wall thickening or a paper-thin wall, **absent mural enhancement**, mesenteric stranding, **pneumatosis intestinalis** and **portal venous gas**. ⚑ **Do not withhold contrast for renal impairment** here. Treat with resuscitation, antibiotics, heparin, correction of the low-flow state and **revascularisation** (endovascular where expertise exists, otherwise open embolectomy/bypass); resect necrotic bowel and use **damage control with a planned second look at 24–48 h** when viability is borderline.

**Chronic mesenteric ischaemia ("intestinal angina").** Atherosclerotic stenosis of ≥2 of coeliac/SMA/IMA causing **post-prandial epigastric pain 15–60 minutes after eating**, **food fear** and **weight loss** — often misattributed to malignancy and diagnosed only after a negative cancer workup. CTA/duplex confirms; treat with revascularisation (usually stenting) plus risk-factor modification. It is the substrate for acute thrombotic ischaemia.

#### 3.8 Ischaemic colitis

**The short version**
- A **different disease** from acute mesenteric ischaemia: **colonic**, usually **non-occlusive**, far commoner, and usually settles conservatively.
- **Watershed zones**: **splenic flexure (Griffiths' point**, SMA/IMA junction) and **rectosigmoid (Sudeck's point)**; the rectum has dual supply and is usually spared.
- **Left-sided crampy pain then bloody diarrhoea within 24 hours**, patient comparatively well.

| | **Acute mesenteric ischaemia** | **Ischaemic colitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Vessel / bowel | SMA/coeliac ± venous, often **occlusive**; small bowel ± right colon | Colonic branches, usually **non-occlusive**; segmental colon at watershed zones |
| Pain / bleeding | **Severe, out of proportion**, central; bleeding late and ominous | Moderate, crampy, left-sided; **early bloody diarrhoea — relatively reassuring** |
| Systemic | Shocked, acidotic | Usually well |
| Management | **Emergency** revascularisation ± resection | **Conservative in ~80%** — bowel rest, IV fluids, optimise perfusion, stop vasoconstrictors |

CT shows segmental wall thickening in a vascular territory; **colonoscopy after the acute phase** confirms and excludes other colitides. Precipitants: hypotension, cardiac failure, **aortic surgery (IMA ligation)**, vasculitis, sickle cell disease, cocaine. **Surgery** (segmental colectomy with a stoma) only for gangrene, perforation, massive bleeding or failure to improve. Late complication: **ischaemic stricture**.

#### 3.9 Ruptured/symptomatic AAA as a cause of abdominal pain

**The short version**
- ⚑ **Any patient over 50 with abdominal or back pain and hypotension has a ruptured AAA until excluded** — routinely mislabelled renal colic.
- Triad: **abdominal/back pain + hypotension + pulsatile expansile mass** (all three in a minority).
- **Unstable → theatre**, not the CT scanner; **stable → CT angiogram** to plan EVAR vs open repair. Bedside ultrasound confirms the aneurysm but will not reliably show the rupture.
- Use **permissive hypotension** (maintain cerebration and a radial pulse) until the aorta is clamped.

**→ See Section 7 (Vascular)** for screening, size thresholds, EVAR vs open repair and endoleak. What belongs here is the reflex: exclude it actively in any older patient with an acute abdomen, and image before accepting a first "renal colic" label over 50.

#### 3.10 The acute abdomen in special groups

**The short version**
- **Pregnancy** — physiology masks everything; appendicitis is still the commonest surgical cause; US then MRI. **Elderly** — blunted signs, more dangerous causes, **low threshold for CT**.
- **Immunosuppressed/neutropenic** — minimal signs despite catastrophic pathology; think **typhlitis**.
- **Post-operative** — a new acute abdomen after surgery is an **anastomotic leak** until proven otherwise.

**Pregnancy.** Leucocytosis, tachycardia and nausea are normal, flattening every signal, and the gravid uterus lifts the omentum so contamination is poorly contained. **Appendicitis is the commonest non-obstetric surgical emergency**, then biliary disease. Involve obstetrics; consider ectopic, ovarian torsion, abruption and pre-eclampsia/HELLP (RUQ pain + hypertension + proteinuria). **US first, MRI second, CT only if essential.** ⚑ **Laparoscopy is safe in all trimesters**; necessary surgery should never be withheld on trimester grounds.

**Elderly.** Comorbidity, polypharmacy and low reserve obscure the picture; fever and leucocytosis are often absent, and **delirium or a fall may be the only presentation of an intra-abdominal catastrophe**. The differential shifts toward the dangerous — **mesenteric ischaemia, ruptured AAA, obstructing malignancy, perforated diverticulitis, volvulus, biliary sepsis**. Emergency laparotomy mortality in the frail is substantial; discuss goals of care early.

**Immunosuppressed and neutropenic.** Steroids, biologics, transplant immunosuppression, chemotherapy and advanced HIV suppress the response that produces physical signs — **peritonitis may present as nothing more than tachycardia and a rising CRP**, so treat the imaging, not the examination. **Neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis)**: RIF pain, fever and diarrhoea in a **neutropenic** patient ~7–14 days after chemotherapy, with **caecal and right colonic wall thickening** on CT. ⚑ **Management is medical** — bowel rest, IV fluids, **broad-spectrum antibiotics with anaerobic and Pseudomonas cover**, no antimotility agents; **surgery only for perforation, bleeding or deterioration**. Differential: appendicitis, *C. difficile*, CMV colitis.

**Post-operative patients.** **Anastomotic leak** — classically day 5–7: tachycardia, new AF, fever, prolonged ileus, low urine output or delirium precede peritonism. ⚑ **Unexplained persistent tachycardia after bowel surgery is a leak until disproven** — CT with contrast, not reassurance and review tomorrow. Distinguish **post-operative ileus** (diffuse, quiet, self-limiting) from **mechanical obstruction** (transition point on CT). Also consider collection, dehiscence, bleeding, *C. difficile*, Ogilvie's, MI and PE. Opioids and epidurals mask signs — trust trends in observations and lactate.

#### 3.12 Abdominal compartment syndrome

**The short version**
- **Intra-abdominal hypertension = sustained IAP ≥12 mmHg**; **abdominal compartment syndrome = sustained IAP >20 mmHg WITH new organ dysfunction**.
- Measured via the bladder with the patient supine at end-expiration, zeroed at the mid-axillary line.
- Settings: massive fluid resuscitation, pancreatitis, ruptured AAA, major burns, damage-control laparotomy, bowel obstruction.
- Consequences: ↑ airway pressures, ↓ preload and cardiac output, oliguric AKI, ↓ splanchnic perfusion, ↑ ICP.
- Medical measures first — sedation and analgesia, neuromuscular blockade, NG and rectal decompression, drain ascites, avoid over-resuscitation, position <30°. **Decompressive laparotomy** if these fail.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Better-tolerated, more reliable alternative to rebound | Percussion tenderness |
| Pain on internal rotation of the flexed right hip / on hip extension | Obturator sign (pelvic appendix) / psoas sign (retrocaecal appendix) |
| CODA antibiotic-arm patients eventually needing appendicectomy | ~29% by 90 days, ~40% at 1 year, ~50% by 3–4 years; more with an appendicolith |
| Tender RIF mass, 5-day history, well patient | Appendix mass — non-operative first, then colonic imaging if >40 |
| Sensitivity of erect CXR for pneumoperitoneum | ~70–80%; a normal film does not exclude perforation — do a CT |
| CT-confirmed uncomplicated diverticulitis, well and immunocompetent | Observation **without antibiotics** (AVOD, DIABOLO, pooled IPD) |
| Follow-up after CT-diagnosed diverticulitis | Interval colonoscopy at ~6–8 weeks to exclude a mimicking cancer |
| Upper limits of bowel diameter on plain film | 3-6-9 rule — small bowel 3 cm, colon 6 cm, caecum 9 cm |
| Lines crossing the full lumen width on AXR | Valvulae conniventes = small bowel (haustra are incomplete = large bowel) |
| Colicky obstruction pain becoming constant, with fever and rising lactate | Strangulation — stop conservative management, operate now |
| Contrast has not reached the colon at 24 h in adhesive SBO | Non-operative management will very likely fail → surgery |
| "Coffee bean" pointing to the RUQ vs dilated caecum in the LUQ | Sigmoid volvulus (scope and decompress, then sigmoidectomy) vs caecal (theatre, right hemicolectomy) |
| Adult intussusception | Assume a pathological lead point — resect, do not simply reduce |
| Caecum >12 cm with no mechanical cause after 48–72 h | Ogilvie's — neostigmine 2 mg IV slowly with cardiac monitoring, then colonoscopy |
| Iron-deficiency anaemia in a man or post-menopausal woman | Colorectal cancer until excluded — colonoscopy **and** gastroscopy |
| NBCSP test, interval, ages and the recent change | iFOBT, 2-yearly, automatic 50–74; since 1 July 2024 ages 45–49 may request a kit |
| Severe central pain, soft abdomen, patient in AF | Acute mesenteric ischaemia — biphasic CTA now; lactate is late |
| Unexplained tachycardia on day 5 after a colonic anastomosis | Anastomotic leak until proven otherwise — CT with contrast |
