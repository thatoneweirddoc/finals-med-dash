## Systemic, Opportunistic and Travel-Related Infection


### Infectious Disease Master Notes — Systemic, Opportunistic and Travel-Related Infection

##### 5.1 Pyrexia of Unknown Origin (PUO)

**Classic definition:** fever **>38.3°C** on several occasions, lasting **>3 weeks**, with no diagnosis after **1 week** of inpatient investigation (or equivalent outpatient work-up).

**The four-category differential:**

1. **Infective** (commonest overall) — TB (often extrapulmonary/disseminated), abscess (intra-abdominal, dental), infective endocarditis, atypical/zoonotic infection.
2. **Malignant** — lymphoma (esp. Hodgkin — **Pel-Ebstein fever**), renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, leukaemia.
3. **Autoimmune/inflammatory** — adult-onset Still's disease, giant cell arteritis/PMR, vasculitis, SLE.
4. **Other** — drug fever (diagnosis of exclusion — resolves on stopping the culprit), factitious fever, familial Mediterranean fever, thyrotoxicosis.

**Work-up logic:** repeat, thorough history (travel, occupation, animal/sexual contact, drugs) and examination are more valuable than a shotgun panel; then a staged approach — basic bloods/cultures/urinalysis/CXR, then imaging (CT chest/abdo/pelvis), then targeted biopsy or PET-CT. **Avoid empirical antibiotics or steroids before a diagnosis** — they mask the underlying process.

##### 5.2 Opportunistic Infections — the Immunosuppressed-Host Framework

Match the defect to the pathogen (a recurring exam logic):

| Immune defect | Setting | Typical pathogens |
|---|---|---|
| **Neutropenia** | Chemotherapy, marrow failure | Gram-negative bacteraemia, invasive fungal (*Aspergillus*, *Candida*) |
| **T-cell/cellular** | HIV, transplant on calcineurin inhibitors, high-dose steroids | PCP, CMV, TB, *Cryptococcus*, *Toxoplasma*, *Listeria* |
| **B-cell/humoral (antibody)** | Myeloma, CLL, splenectomy | **Encapsulated organisms** — *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *N. meningitidis* |
| **Complement deficiency** | Terminal complement (C5–9) deficiency | **Recurrent *Neisseria* infections** |
| **Biologic therapy** (anti-TNF, JAK inhibitors) | RA, IBD on biologics | **TB reactivation** (screen before starting), serious bacterial infection |

**Asplenia/splenectomy** — lifelong risk from encapsulated organisms; requires vaccination (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib) and prophylactic penicillin, especially in the first years post-splenectomy and in children.

##### 5.3 Travel and Vector-Borne Infection

**Malaria** — *Plasmodium* species (**falciparum** most severe — cerebral malaria, blackwater fever, high parasitaemia); cyclical fever classically taught but often irregular. **Always test (thick and thin films) in a febrile returned traveller regardless of prophylaxis history.** Severe falciparum: **IV artesunate**.

**Dengue** — mosquito-borne (*Aedes*), fever + retro-orbital headache + myalgia (**"breakbone fever"**) + rash; warning signs for severe dengue (plasma leakage, bleeding, shock) — abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy.

**Enteric (typhoid) fever** — *Salmonella typhi*; stepwise fever, **relative bradycardia**, **rose spots**, constipation then diarrhoea; fluoroquinolone resistance common in some regions — ceftriaxone/azithromycin often preferred.

**Traveller's diarrhoea** — mostly enterotoxigenic *E. coli*; usually self-limiting/supportive; antibiotics reserved for severe/dysenteric presentations.

##### 5.4 Infective Gastroenteritis

Most acute gastroenteritis is self-limiting and managed with rehydration alone — the exam skill is recognising the organism from the history.

| Organism | Signature clue | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Campylobacter jejuni* | Commonest bacterial cause; undercooked poultry; bloody diarrhoea + severe abdominal pain (can mimic appendicitis) | ⚑ Associated with **Guillain–Barré syndrome** and reactive arthritis |
| *Salmonella* (non-typhoidal) | Eggs, poultry, reptiles | Usually self-limiting; **antibiotics can prolong carriage** — reserve for severe/immunosuppressed/extremes of age |
| *Shigella* | Very low infective dose → person-to-person spread; dysentery (blood and mucus) | Can cause HUS (Shiga toxin) |
| Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC/O157) | Undercooked beef; bloody diarrhoea | ⚑ **Avoid antibiotics — they increase HUS risk** |
| *Vibrio cholerae* | Endemic areas; profuse **"rice-water" stool** | Death is from dehydration — aggressive rehydration is the treatment |
| Norovirus | Outbreaks (cruise ships, hospitals, care homes); prominent vomiting, short-lived | Highly contagious — infection-control implications |
| Rotavirus | Young children; vaccine-preventable | Major global paediatric cause |
| *Giardia lamblia* | Freshwater/camping; prolonged, bloating, foul-smelling steatorrhoea, **no blood** | Metronidazole |
| *C. difficile* | Post-antibiotic (see 5.5) | The one where the antibiotic history is the diagnosis |

**Discriminator logic:** bloody diarrhoea → invasive/toxin-producing bacteria (Campylobacter, Shigella, STEC, Salmonella); watery and profuse → toxin-mediated secretory (cholera, ETEC); vomiting-predominant, outbreak → norovirus; prolonged and non-bloody after freshwater exposure → giardia.

**When to investigate:** bloody diarrhoea, systemic toxicity, immunosuppression, recent antibiotics, recent travel, prolonged (>7 days) symptoms, or outbreak context → stool culture ± toxin/PCR panel.

##### 5.5 Healthcare-Associated Infection

***Clostridioides difficile*** — toxin-mediated colitis following disruption of normal gut flora. High-risk antibiotics — the **"4 Cs"**: **C**lindamycin, **C**o-amoxiclav, **C**ephalosporins, **C**iprofloxacin. Diagnosis: toxin PCR/EIA on stool. Treatment: stop the culprit antibiotic, **oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin first-line** (⚑ **metronidazole downgraded from first-line** in most current guidance except mild disease/resource-limited settings); recurrent disease may need faecal microbiota transplant.

**MRSA/VRE screening and infection control:** contact precautions, screening swabs on admission in higher-risk settings, decolonisation protocols (nasal mupirocin, chlorhexidine washes) for MRSA carriers pre-surgery.

#### Recognition / Formulation Summary

##### Part A — By Presenting Syndrome

**A1. Fever with no obvious source.** Sepsis Six bundle first (O₂, cultures, IV antibiotics, fluids, lactate, urine output) → broad empirical cover pending source. If >3 weeks, undiagnosed → PUO framework.

**A2. Fever in the immunosuppressed patient.** Match defect to pathogen: neutropenic → Gram-negative/fungal (antibiotics within 1 hour); T-cell defect (HIV/transplant) → PCP/CMV/TB/Cryptococcus/Toxoplasma; asplenic/B-cell → encapsulated organisms.

**A3. Fever in the returned traveller.** Always test for malaria (thick/thin films) regardless of prophylaxis. Consider dengue, typhoid by exposure/region and symptom pattern.

**A4. New diarrhoea in hospital / post-antibiotic.** Think C. difficile — check the antibiotic history ("4 Cs"), send toxin testing, stop the culprit, start oral vancomycin/fidaxomicin.

**A5. Genital ulcer / discharge.** Painless single ulcer → syphilis. Painful, recurrent → HSV. Purulent discharge → gonorrhoea/chlamydia. Frothy + strawberry cervix → trichomoniasis.

**A6. New HIV diagnosis / risk assessment.** Stage by CD4; screen for opportunistic infection at diagnosis if CD4 low; start ART (2 NRTIs + integrase inhibitor); PEP within 72h; PrEP for ongoing risk.

**B2. CD4 count → opportunistic infection**

| CD4 | Infection |
|---|---|
| <200 | PCP, oesophageal candidiasis |
| <100 | Toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis |
| <50 | CMV retinitis, disseminated MAC |

**B3. Antibiotic → signature toxicity/rule**

| Drug | Rule |
|---|---|
| Aminoglycosides | Nephro/ototoxic — level monitoring |
| Clindamycin | Highest C. diff risk; toxin suppression in nec fasc |
| Linezolid | Serotonin syndrome with SSRIs |
| Daptomycin | Never for pneumonia (surfactant inactivates) |
| Metronidazole | Disulfiram reaction with alcohol |
| Fluoroquinolones ⚑ | Tendon rupture, aortic dissection, QT — restricted use |
| Rifampicin | CYP450 inducer, red body fluids |
| Vancomycin | Red man syndrome (rate-related, not allergy) |

**B4. Resistance mechanism → organism**

| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Altered PBP2a | MRSA |
| Altered vancomycin target | VRE |
| Beta-lactamase (extended-spectrum) | ESBL Enterobacterales — carbapenems usually still work |
| Carbapenemase | CRE — Reserve-tier agents needed |

**B5. Empiric therapy timing rules (the "don't wait" list)**

| Scenario | Rule |
|---|---|
| Neutropenic sepsis | Antibiotics within 1 hour, before counts/imaging |
| Bacterial meningitis | Don't delay for LP/imaging if septic/deteriorating |
| Necrotising fasciitis | Surgery is the treatment — don't wait for imaging |
| PID | Treat empirically on clinical suspicion, don't wait for swabs |
| Surgical prophylaxis | Within 60 min pre-incision |

**B6. AWaRe tier examples**

| Tier | Examples |
|---|---|
| Access | Amoxicillin, flucloxacillin, gentamicin, doxycycline, nitrofurantoin |
| Watch | Ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, macrolides, vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam |
| Reserve | Colistin, linezolid, newer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase-inhibitor combos for CRE |

##### Part C — Confusable Pairs

| Pair | Separating feature |
|---|---|
| TACO vs TRALI *(detail in haem/onc build)* | TACO hypertensive/fluid-overloaded; TRALI hypotensive |
| ESBL vs CRE | ESBL usually still carbapenem-susceptible; CRE is not |
| MRSA vs VRE mechanism | MRSA = altered PBP2a; VRE = altered vancomycin-binding target |
| Metronidazole vs vancomycin for C. diff ⚑ | Vancomycin/fidaxomicin now first-line; metronidazole downgraded except mild/resource-limited |
| SIRS vs Sepsis-3 ⚑ | SIRS no longer part of the formal definition; sepsis = organ dysfunction (SOFA ≥2) from infection |
| Primary vs secondary syphilis | Primary = painless chancre; secondary = disseminated rash incl. palms/soles |
| Cellulitis vs necrotising fasciitis | Pain out of proportion + rapid spread + crepitus/necrosis → necrotising fasciitis, urgent surgery |
