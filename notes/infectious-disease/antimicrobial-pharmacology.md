## Antimicrobial Pharmacology


### Infectious Disease Master Notes — Antimicrobial Pharmacology

#### 2.1 Beta-Lactams

**Mechanism:** inhibit penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking in the bacterial cell wall → bactericidal.

**Bactericidal vs bacteriostatic** — bactericidal agents kill (cell-wall inhibitors: beta-lactams, glycopeptides; plus aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, metronidazole); bacteriostatic agents inhibit replication and rely on host immunity. Mnemonic **"ECSTaTIC"**: Erythromycin, Clindamycin, Sulfamethoxazole, Trimethoprim, Tetracyclines, Chloramphenicol. (The distinction matters most in immunosuppression, endocarditis and meningitis, where bactericidal therapy is preferred.)

**CNS penetration:** beta-lactams generally cross into the CNS only when the meninges are inflamed — the exceptions with reliably good CNS penetration are **ceftriaxone and aztreonam** (part of why ceftriaxone is the empirical meningitis agent). Elimination is primarily renal (ceftriaxone is both renal and biliary — hence use without renal dose adjustment).

| Class | Spectrum progression | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penicillins | Narrow (Gram+) → broader | Benzylpenicillin, amoxicillin, flucloxacillin (anti-staphylococcal, beta-lactamase-stable), piperacillin-tazobactam (broad, antipseudomonal + beta-lactamase inhibitor) | Amoxicillin-clavulanate adds beta-lactamase cover |
| Cephalosporins | Generations broaden Gram-negative cover, generally lose some Gram-positive | 1st gen (cefalexin — Gram+ skin), 3rd gen (ceftriaxone — meningitis, gonorrhoea, broad), ceftazidime (antipseudomonal) | **Enterococcus and Listeria are intrinsically resistant to all cephalosporins** |
| Carbapenems | Broadest — Gram+, Gram−, anaerobes | Meropenem, imipenem | Reserve/Watch-tier — for ESBL and severe polymicrobial infection; **not active against MRSA or *E. faecium*** |
| Monobactams | Gram-negative only | Aztreonam | **Safe in severe penicillin allergy** (no cross-reactivity) |

**Beta-lactam allergy — the exam-favourite discriminator:** true cross-reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is **low (~1–2%)**, much lower than historically taught (⚑ the old "10%" figure is outdated) — a documented mild penicillin rash does not contraindicate cephalosporins; **anaphylaxis** to penicillin is the scenario that mandates avoiding all beta-lactams (aztreonam is safe here). Carbapenems have minimal cross-reactivity with penicillins.

#### 2.2 Protein Synthesis Inhibitors

| Class | Target | Examples | Key toxicity/notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aminoglycosides | 30S ribosome | Gentamicin, amikacin | **Nephrotoxic, ototoxic** — narrow therapeutic index, requires level monitoring; synergy with beta-lactams for endocarditis |
| Macrolides | 50S ribosome | Azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin | Atypical cover, QT prolongation, GI upset; CYP3A4 interactions (clarithromycin > azithromycin) |
| Tetracyclines | 30S ribosome | Doxycycline | Atypicals, Lyme, acne; **avoid in pregnancy and children <8** (teeth/bone deposition); photosensitivity |
| Clindamycin | 50S ribosome | — | Anaerobes, **toxin suppression** in necrotising fasciitis/toxic shock; **highest C. difficile risk of any class** |
| Linezolid | 50S ribosome | — | MRSA/VRE cover; **serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs** (weak MAOI), thrombocytopenia with prolonged use |

#### 2.3 Nucleic Acid / Other Mechanisms

| Drug | Mechanism | Key use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vancomycin (glycopeptide) | Inhibits cell wall synthesis (different site to beta-lactams) | MRSA, C. diff (oral, severe disease) | Nephrotoxic, needs level monitoring; **red man syndrome** if infused too fast (histamine, **not** allergy) |
| Fluoroquinolones | DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV inhibition | Ciprofloxacin (Gram-negative/atypical), levofloxacin (adds pneumococcal cover) | ⚑ **Tendon rupture, aortic aneurysm/dissection risk, QT prolongation** — increasingly restricted; avoid in pregnancy, caution in elderly/steroid use |
| Metronidazole | DNA damage (anaerobic activation) | Anaerobes, C. difficile, protozoa (giardia, amoebiasis, trichomonas) | **Disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol** |
| Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) | Folate synthesis inhibition (sequential blockade) | UTI, PCP prophylaxis/treatment, some MRSA | **Hyperkalaemia**, interacts with warfarin/methotrexate |
| Rifampicin | RNA polymerase inhibition | TB, meningococcal/Hib prophylaxis | Potent **CYP450 inducer**, red/orange body fluids |
| Daptomycin | Cell membrane disruption | MRSA/VRE bacteraemia | **Inactivated by pulmonary surfactant — never use for pneumonia** |

#### 2.4 Antifungals and Antivirals

**Antifungals:** azoles (fluconazole — *Candida*; itraconazole/voriconazole — *Aspergillus*; CYP450 interactions), echinocandins (caspofungin — invasive candidiasis, fungal cell wall synthesis), amphotericin B (broad but nephrotoxic, reserved for severe/resistant invasive fungal disease).

**Antivirals:** aciclovir/valaciclovir (HSV/VZV — thymidine kinase-dependent activation), oseltamivir (influenza, neuraminidase inhibitor, most effective within 48h of onset), antiretrovirals (Section 4).

#### 2.5 The WHO AWaRe Framework ⚑

Now the global standard for antimicrobial stewardship and increasingly examined directly:

- **Access** — narrow-spectrum, first/second-line for common infections, low resistance potential (amoxicillin, flucloxacillin, gentamicin, doxycycline, nitrofurantoin). WHO/UN target: **≥60–70% of antibiotic use should be Access-tier by 2030**.
- **Watch** — higher resistance potential or reserved for specific syndromes (ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, macrolides, vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam). Use judiciously.
- **Reserve** — last-resort for confirmed/suspected multidrug-resistant infection (colistin, linezolid, newer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase-inhibitor combinations for carbapenem-resistant organisms). Requires ID/microbiology input in most settings.

#### 2.6 Resistance Mechanisms

Four core mechanisms — every resistant organism uses at least one:

1. **Enzymatic inactivation** — beta-lactamases (**ESBL** — hydrolyses most penicillins/cephalosporins, often still susceptible to carbapenems; **AmpC** — inducible, hydrolyses cephalosporins including some 3rd-gen; **carbapenemases** — CRE).
2. **Target modification** — altered PBP (**MRSA** — *mecA* gene, altered **PBP2a**), altered ribosomal target (macrolide resistance), altered DNA gyrase (fluoroquinolone resistance), altered vancomycin target (**VRE**).
3. **Efflux pumps** — actively pump the drug out (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones).
4. **Reduced permeability** — porin loss (Gram-negatives, especially *Pseudomonas*, *Acinetobacter*).

**Worked examples:** MRSA — altered PBP2a; treat with vancomycin, linezolid, or daptomycin (not pneumonia). ESBL-producing Enterobacterales — resistant to penicillins and most cephalosporins; **carbapenems remain first-line** for serious infection. CRE ⚑ — top global priority pathogen; Reserve-tier agents and ID input. VRE — altered vancomycin binding target; linezolid or daptomycin.

### Infectious Disease (ANU) — MECHANISM OF ACTION — Antimicrobial Pharmacology

Bactericidal

- Kill bacteria by inhibiting cell wall synthesis
- “Very finely proficient at ccell murder”: vancomycin, fluoroquinolones, penicillin, aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, carbapenems, metronidazole

Bacteriostatic

- Arrest bacterial growth, by interfering with bacterial protein production, DNA replication, bacterial cellular metabolism
- “ECSTaTIC”: erythromycin, clindamycin, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim, tetracyclines, chloramphenicol

### Infectious Disease (ANU) — beta-lactam antibiotics — Antimicrobial Pharmacology

- Includes penicillin, carbapenem’s, aztreonam and cephalosporins
- Mechanism of action:
- Inhibits cell wall synthesis by binding to penicillin-binding proteins which inhibits peptidoglycan cross-linking
- Beta-lactam antibiotics are bactericidal
- CNS penetration: only when meninges are inflamed
- Exceptions: ceftriaxone and aztreonam always have good CNS penetration
- Route of elimination: primarily renal (ceftriaxone is both renal and biliary)
- General adverse effects
- Penicillin allergy (hypersensitivity reactions)
- Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction
- Beta-lactamase inhibitors
- Beta-lactamases, which are usually produced by gram-negative and anaerobic organisms, can split the beta-lactam ring and render certain beta-lactam antibiotics ineffective
- Beta-lactamase inhibitors increase the spectrum of antibiotic activity (mnemonic CAST): Clavulanic Acid, Sulbactam, Tazobactam
