## Neurophysiology


### Neurology Master Notes — Neurophysiology

#### 1.1 Resting potential, action potential, conduction

**The short version**

- RMP −70 mV is set by the K⁺ gradient with a small Na⁺ leak, held by the electrogenic Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in).
- AP sequence: threshold ≈ −55 mV → Na⁺ influx to ≈ +35 mV → Na⁺ inactivation + delayed-rectifier K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarisation and refractory period.
- Myelin gives saltatory conduction at the nodes of Ranvier — so demyelination slows **velocity**, axonal loss drops **amplitude**.
- Fibre size sorts the deficit: Aα/Aβ (vibration, proprioception) go first to compression; C fibres (pain, warmth) go in small-fibre disease with normal NCS.

The resting membrane potential (**−70 mV** in a typical neurone) is set by the K⁺ gradient with a small Na⁺ leak, maintained by the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in)** — electrogenic, and a major consumer of the brain's 20% share of resting oxygen use.

| Phase | Ionic event |
|---|---|
| Threshold (≈ −55 mV) | Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open |
| Upstroke | Rapid Na⁺ influx → depolarisation to ≈ +35 mV |
| Repolarisation | Na⁺ channel inactivation + delayed-rectifier K⁺ efflux |
| Hyperpolarisation | K⁺ efflux overshoots; absolute then relative refractory period |

**Saltatory conduction:** myelin (oligodendrocytes centrally, Schwann cells peripherally) increases membrane resistance and lowers capacitance, so current jumps node-to-node at the **nodes of Ranvier**, where Na⁺ channels are concentrated.

**Why this matters clinically:** demyelination slows or blocks conduction → the *conduction-velocity slowing* of demyelinating neuropathy (GBS, CIDP, CMT1) on nerve conduction studies, versus *reduced amplitude* in axonal neuropathy. Heat worsens conduction block — **Uhthoff's phenomenon** in MS.

| Fibre | Myelin | Function | Clinical note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aα/Aβ | Heavy | Proprioception, vibration, light touch, motor | Lost first in compression; large-fibre neuropathy |
| Aδ | Thin | Fast "first" pain, cold | Sharp, well-localised pain |
| C | None | Slow "second" pain, warmth, autonomic | Burning pain; small-fibre neuropathy (normal NCS) |

> **Hook:** *Pressure kills big fibres, ischaemia kills small fibres* — compression neuropathy loses vibration/proprioception first; vasculitic/diabetic small-fibre disease loses pain/temperature with normal nerve conduction studies.

#### 1.2 Synaptic transmission and neurotransmitters

**The short version**

- Presynaptic Ca²⁺ entry → SNARE-mediated vesicle fusion → transmitter release → EPSP/IPSP, summed at the axon hillock.
- Glutamate is the principal excitatory transmitter (AMPA, NMDA); GABA is the principal inhibitory one centrally, glycine in cord/brainstem.
- Every transmitter in the table carries its drug/disease list — learn the clinical link column, not the anatomy.
- Tetanus blocks inhibitory glycine/GABA → rigidity; botulinum blocks ACh release → flaccid paralysis.

Depolarisation opens presynaptic **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** → vesicle fusion (SNARE proteins) → transmitter release → postsynaptic receptor binding → EPSP or IPSP → summation at the axon hillock.

| Transmitter | Main role | Key clinical link |
|---|---|---|
| Glutamate | Principal excitatory (AMPA, NMDA) | Excitotoxicity in stroke; anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis; memantine blocks NMDA |
| GABA | Principal inhibitory (GABA-A ionotropic Cl⁻, GABA-B metabotropic) | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol; anti-GAD antibodies (stiff person) |
| Glycine | Inhibitory in cord/brainstem | Blocked by strychnine and by tetanus toxin |
| Acetylcholine | NMJ, autonomic ganglia, basal forebrain | Myasthenia, cholinesterase inhibitors in Alzheimer's |
| Dopamine | Nigrostriatal, mesolimbic, tuberoinfundibular | Parkinson's; antipsychotic side effects; prolactin |
| Noradrenaline | Locus coeruleus — arousal | Depression, ADHD drugs |
| Serotonin | Raphe nuclei — mood, pain, sleep | Triptans, SSRIs, serotonin syndrome |

**Toxin logic worth memorising:** **tetanus toxin** blocks release of *inhibitory* glycine/GABA in the cord → spasticity and rigidity; **botulinum toxin** blocks release of *acetylcholine* at the NMJ (cleaves SNARE proteins) → flaccid paralysis. Same family of proteases, opposite clinical picture.

#### 1.3 The neuromuscular junction

**The short version**

- Chain to know: P/Q Ca²⁺ channel → ACh release → nicotinic AChR → endplate potential → DHP receptor → ryanodine receptor → Ca²⁺ → cross-bridge cycling.
- There is normally a large **safety factor**; each NMJ disease removes it at a different point in that chain.
- MG hits the postsynaptic AChR → **decrement**; LEMS hits the presynaptic P/Q channel → **increment** after exercise.
- Botulism blocks ACh release (descending flaccid paralysis, fixed dilated pupils); organophosphates inhibit AChE → cholinergic crisis then depolarising block.

Nerve action potential → presynaptic **P/Q-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** open → ACh released → binds **nicotinic ACh receptors** on the postsynaptic folds → endplate potential → muscle fibre action potential → T-tubule depolarisation → **dihydropyridine receptor** → **ryanodine receptor** → sarcoplasmic Ca²⁺ release → cross-bridge cycling. ACh is degraded by acetylcholinesterase in the cleft.

There is normally a large **safety factor** (far more ACh released than needed). Disease removes it in different places:

| Disorder | Target | Consequence on repetitive stimulation |
|---|---|---|
| Myasthenia gravis | Postsynaptic ACh receptor (or MuSK/LRP4) | **Decrement** at low frequency; fatigable weakness |
| Lambert–Eaton | Presynaptic P/Q Ca²⁺ channel | **Increment** after exercise/high-frequency; weakness improves briefly with use |
| Botulism | Presynaptic SNARE-mediated ACh release | Descending flaccid paralysis, fixed dilated pupils, autonomic features |
| Organophosphate poisoning | Acetylcholinesterase inhibited | Cholinergic crisis then depolarising block |

#### 1.4 CSF and the Monro–Kellie doctrine

CSF is produced mainly by the **choroid plexus** of the lateral, third and fourth ventricles at roughly **0.35 mL/min ≈ 500 mL/day**, with a standing volume of about **150 mL** — so the whole volume is turned over three to four times daily. Flow: lateral ventricles → foramina of Monro → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → foramina of Luschka/Magendie → subarachnoid space → absorbed at arachnoid granulations into the superior sagittal sinus (and, per more recent work, via meningeal lymphatics and the glymphatic system).

Normal opening pressure on lumbar puncture: **10–20 cmH₂O** (adults, lateral decubitus); >25 cmH₂O is clearly raised. ⚑ For idiopathic intracranial hypertension, current diagnostic criteria use **>25 cmH₂O** in adults.

**Monro–Kellie doctrine:** the cranium is a fixed box containing brain (~80%), blood (~10%) and CSF (~10%). An increase in one must be offset by a decrease in another, or ICP rises. Initial compensation is displacement of CSF and venous blood; once exhausted, the pressure–volume curve turns steeply upward — which is why a patient can look stable and then deteriorate abruptly.

**Hydrocephalus split:** *non-communicating (obstructive)* — block within the ventricular system (aqueduct stenosis, colloid cyst, posterior fossa tumour); *communicating* — impaired absorption (post-SAH, post-meningitis) or, rarely, overproduction (choroid plexus papilloma).

#### 1.5 Cerebral blood flow and perfusion

Normal CBF ≈ **50 mL/100 g/min**. Thresholds worth knowing: below ~20 mL/100 g/min electrical function fails (the **ischaemic penumbra** — dysfunctional but salvageable); below ~10 mL/100 g/min membrane failure and infarction follow within minutes. The penumbra is the entire rationale for reperfusion therapy.

**Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) = MAP − ICP.** Target CPP is generally **60–70 mmHg** in traumatic brain injury; ICP is treated when sustained **>22 mmHg** ⚑ (Brain Trauma Foundation 4th edition lowered the older ">20" threshold to >22).

**Autoregulation** keeps CBF constant across a MAP of roughly **60–150 mmHg**, shifted rightward in chronic hypertension (so a "normal" BP may under-perfuse). Autoregulation is impaired or lost in acute stroke and TBI, making CBF pressure-passive — the reason for permissive hypertension after ischaemic stroke and for avoiding hypotension in head injury.

CBF also responds to gases: it rises with **hypercapnia** and falls with hypocapnia (roughly 1–2 mL/100 g/min per mmHg PaCO₂) — the basis of brief hyperventilation as a temporising measure for herniation, and of why it is harmful if prolonged.

#### 1.6 The blood–brain barrier

Formed by **tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells**, supported by astrocyte end-feet and pericytes. Lipid-soluble small molecules cross freely; glucose (GLUT1) and amino acids need carriers. **Circumventricular organs** lack a barrier — area postrema (chemoreceptor trigger zone), median eminence, pineal, subfornical organ.

Clinical consequences: levodopa crosses but dopamine does not (hence peripheral decarboxylase inhibitors); most antibiotics penetrate poorly unless the meninges are inflamed (why meningitic doses are higher); contrast enhancement on CT/MRI marks BBB breakdown (tumour, abscess, active demyelinating plaque).

#### 1.7 Motor physiology — UMN versus LMN

**The short version**

- UMN: ↑tone, brisk reflexes, extensor plantars, no wasting, no fasciculations. LMN: the exact reverse.
- UMN weakness is **pyramidal** — arm extensors and leg flexors weak, hence the flexed arm / extended leg posture.
- Acutely (spinal shock, acute stroke) tone and reflexes may be *reduced*; spasticity develops later — a classic exam trap.
- ~85–90% of corticospinal fibres decussate at the medullary pyramids; reflex roots: biceps C5/6, supinator C6, triceps C7, knee L3/4, ankle S1/2.

The corticospinal tract originates in the precentral gyrus (Betz cells in layer V), descends through the **corona radiata → posterior limb of the internal capsule → cerebral peduncle → pons → medullary pyramids**, where ~85–90% decussate to form the lateral corticospinal tract; the remainder continue as the anterior corticospinal tract and cross at segmental level.

| Feature | Upper motor neurone | Lower motor neurone |
|---|---|---|
| Tone | Increased (spastic, clasp-knife) | Decreased (flaccid) |
| Reflexes | Brisk, clonus | Reduced or absent |
| Plantars | Extensor (Babinski) | Flexor |
| Wasting | Late, disuse only | Early and marked |
| Fasciculations | Absent | Present |
| Weakness pattern | Pyramidal — extensors weak in arm, flexors weak in leg | Follows the nerve/root/segment |

> **Hook:** in the arms an UMN lesion spares the "carrying" muscles — **arm flexors strong, extensors weak; leg extensors strong, flexors weak** — hence the hemiplegic posture of flexed arm and extended leg.

Note that in the first hours to days after an acute UMN lesion (spinal shock, acute stroke) tone and reflexes may be *reduced*; spasticity develops later. This trips people up in exams.

**Reflex arc:** muscle spindle (Ia afferent) → dorsal root → monosynaptic connection to the α-motor neurone → contraction. Golgi tendon organs (Ib) mediate inhibition. Reflex roots worth reciting: **biceps C5/6, supinator C6, triceps C7, knee L3/4, ankle S1/2**.

#### 1.8 Sensory physiology — the two systems

**The short version**

- Dorsal column–medial lemniscus = vibration, proprioception, fine touch; spinothalamic = pain, temperature, crude touch.
- **Where they cross is the whole point**: dorsal columns in the medulla, spinothalamic within 1–2 segments in the cord.
- That difference generates Brown-Séquard — ipsilateral dorsal column loss and weakness, contralateral pain/temperature loss.
- Both converge on VPL of thalamus → postcentral gyrus.

| | Dorsal column–medial lemniscus | Spinothalamic (anterolateral) |
|---|---|---|
| Modalities | Vibration, proprioception, fine touch | Pain, temperature, crude touch |
| First-order cell | DRG → ascends **ipsilaterally** in the cord | DRG → synapses in dorsal horn |
| Decussation | **Medulla** (internal arcuate fibres) | **Within 1–2 segments in the cord** (anterior white commissure) |
| Second-order | Nucleus gracilis/cuneatus → medial lemniscus | Lateral spinothalamic tract |
| Third-order | VPL of thalamus → postcentral gyrus | VPL of thalamus → postcentral gyrus |

That difference in where the tracts cross is what generates **Brown-Séquard syndrome**: ipsilateral loss of vibration/proprioception and ipsilateral UMN weakness *below* the lesion, with **contralateral** loss of pain and temperature starting one or two segments below.

**Pain modulation:** the gate control theory (Aβ input inhibits dorsal-horn transmission — why rubbing helps) and descending inhibition from the periaqueductal grey via serotonergic/noradrenergic pathways — the mechanism exploited by tricyclics and SNRIs in neuropathic pain.

#### 1.9 Cerebellum and basal ganglia

**Cerebellum** — compares intended with actual movement and corrects it. Output is **ipsilateral** (double crossing), so a cerebellar hemisphere lesion causes ipsilateral limb signs. Midline (vermis/flocculonodular) disease causes truncal/gait ataxia and nystagmus; hemispheric disease causes limb dysmetria, intention tremor and dysdiadochokinesia.

**Basal ganglia circuitry** (know the direction of the arrows, not every synapse):

- **Direct pathway** (facilitates movement): cortex → striatum → (inhibits) internal globus pallidus → (disinhibits) thalamus → cortex. Dopamine at **D1** receptors *excites* this pathway.
- **Indirect pathway** (suppresses movement): cortex → striatum → external globus pallidus → subthalamic nucleus → internal globus pallidus → thalamus. Dopamine at **D2** receptors *inhibits* this pathway.

Net effect: nigrostriatal dopamine promotes movement. Losing it (Parkinson's — degeneration of the substantia nigra pars compacta, with Lewy bodies containing α-synuclein) produces hypokinesia. Losing the striatal GABAergic neurones of the indirect pathway (Huntington's) or the subthalamic nucleus (vascular lesion) produces hyperkinesia — chorea and hemiballismus respectively. Motor symptoms of PD appear only after roughly **50–70% of nigral neurones are lost**, which is why disease is well established at diagnosis.

#### 1.10 Consciousness and sleep

Consciousness requires **arousal** (ascending reticular activating system in the upper brainstem tegmentum, projecting via thalamus to cortex) plus **content** (cortex). Therefore coma requires either a **brainstem/upper reticular lesion**, **bilateral hemispheric dysfunction**, or a **diffuse metabolic/toxic insult**. A unilateral hemispheric lesion does not cause coma unless it is large enough to compress the brainstem.

Sleep cycles roughly every 90 minutes through N1–N3 (slow-wave, restorative, where sleepwalking and night terrors occur) and REM (dreaming, muscle atonia, where nightmares and — when atonia fails — **REM sleep behaviour disorder** occur; RBD is a strong predictor of later synucleinopathy, i.e. Parkinson's disease, DLB or MSA).

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators — Section 1

**The short version**

- Nerve conduction: slowed velocity = demyelinating, reduced amplitude = axonal, normal NCS with burning feet = small-fibre.
- NMJ: decrement = MG, increment after exercise = LEMS.
- Numbers to have cold: CSF 10–20 cmH₂O, CPP = MAP − ICP, treat ICP >22 mmHg ⚑, infarction below ~10 mL/100 g/min.
- Crossings and sides: dorsal columns in the medulla, spinothalamic in the cord, cerebellar signs ipsilateral.

Split into two passes — peripheral nerve and NMJ first, then central physiology.

##### Peripheral nerve, NMJ and toxins

| Clue / question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Neuropathy with slowed conduction velocity | Demyelinating (GBS, CIDP, CMT1) |
| Neuropathy with reduced action potential amplitude, preserved velocity | Axonal (diabetes, alcohol, toxic) |
| Painful burning feet, normal nerve conduction studies | Small-fibre neuropathy |
| Toxin causing rigidity and trismus | Tetanus (blocks inhibitory glycine/GABA) |
| Toxin causing descending flaccid paralysis with fixed pupils | Botulinum (blocks ACh release) |
| Decrement on repetitive nerve stimulation | Myasthenia gravis |
| Increment after brief exercise | Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome |

##### CSF, perfusion, tracts and central physiology

| Clue / question | Answer |

### Neurology (ANU) — Adrenergic receptors — Neurophysiology

**The short version**

- α1 = vessels, sphincters, iris radial muscle → **contraction**; prazosin is the antagonist (bladder obstruction).
- α2 = presynaptic sympathetic terminals, platelets, fat, GIT wall → **inhibitory**; clonidine is the agonist (hypertension).
- β1 = heart (SA/AV node, ventricle) → ↑HR, ↑conduction velocity, ↑contractility; blocked by metoprolol and friends.
- β2 = skeletal muscle vessels, bronchi, GIT and bladder → **relaxation/dilatation**; salbutamol is the agonist (asthma).

| α1 | • Located on: vascular smooth muscle (skin, splanchnic), GIT and bladder sphincters, radial muscle of iris<br>• Excitatory effects: contraction of vessels and sphincters, constriction of muscles<br>• Prazosin is an antagonist used for bladder obstruction |
| --- | --- |
| α2 | • Located on: sympathetic post-ganglionic nerve terminals, platelets, fat cells and walls of GIT<br>• Inhibitory effects: relaxation or dilatation<br>• Clonidine is an agonist used in the treatment of hypertension |
| β1 | • Located in: heart (SA node, AV node and ventricular muscle)<br>• Excitatory effects: increase HR, increase conduction velocity, increase contractility<br>• Proponalol, metoprolol, etc. are antagonists used in the treatment of hypertension |
| β2 | • Located on: vascular smooth muscle (skeletal muscle), bronchial smooth muscle, walls of GIT and bladder<br>• Inhibitory effects: dilatation of vessels, relaxation of bronchial smooth muscles and bladder wall<br>• Salbutamol is an agonist used in asthma |

### Neurology (ANU) — Cholinergic receptors — Neurophysiology

**The short version**

- Nicotinic — autonomic ganglia (both SNS and PNS), NMJ, adrenal medulla; activated by ACh or nicotine; always excitatory.
- Muscarinic — heart, smooth muscle, glands; activated by ACh or muscarine.
- Muscarinic effect splits by tissue: **inhibitory in the heart** (↓HR, ↓AV conduction), **excitatory in smooth muscle and glands** (↑GI motility, ↑secretions).
- Atropine is the muscarinic antagonist — used for certain bradycardias.

| Nicotonic | • Located in: autonomic ganglia of SNS and PNS; NMJ; adrenal medulla<br>• Activated by ACh or nicotine<br>• Excitatory effects |
| --- | --- |
| Muscarinic | • Located in: heart, smooth muscle, glands<br>• Activated by ACh or muscarine<br>• Inhibitory effect in the heart: decrease HR, decrease conduction velocity in AV node<br>• Excitatory effect in smooth muscle and glands: increase GI motility, increase secretions<br>• Atropine is an antagonist and is used for treatment of certain bradycardias |

### Neurology (ANU) — embryology and areas of the brain — Neurophysiology

**The short version**

- Frontal = executive function, cognition, voluntary motor → lesion gives personality change, disinhibition, apathy, one-sided weakness.
- Parietal = somatosensory and visuospatial → lesion gives sensory loss, aphasia, **hemispatial neglect**.
- Temporal = recognition, language, learning and memory → lesion gives temporal lobe epilepsy, short-term memory loss, word-finding failure.
- Occipital = vision → visual loss; cerebellum = coordination, motor learning, balance → ataxia and **intention tremor**.

### Neurology (ANU) — spinocerebellar tract — Neurophysiology

**The short version**

- Carry **unconscious** proprioception from muscle to the **ipsilateral** cerebellum, refining motor output.
- Four pathways: anterior and posterior spinocerebellar plus cuneocerebellar.
- Lesion = ipsilateral loss of muscle co-ordination — rarely isolated, usually with descending motor tract signs (weakness, paralysis).

| Spinocerebellar Tracts |
| --- |
| • Carry unconscious proprioceptive information to ipsilateral cerebellum to co-ordinate + refine motor movements<br>• Transmit information from the muscles to the cerebellum<br>• Four pathways- the anterior and posterior spinocerebellar and cuneocerebellar tracts.<br>• Lesions present as an ipsilateral loss of muscle co-ordination. However, unlikely to be damaged in isolation, usually accompanied by lesions to descending motor tracts (muscle weakness, paralysis) |
