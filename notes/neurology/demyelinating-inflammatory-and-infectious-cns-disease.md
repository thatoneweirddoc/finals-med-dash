## Demyelinating, Inflammatory and Infectious CNS Disease


### Neurology Master Notes — Demyelinating, Inflammatory and Infectious CNS Disease

#### 8.1 Multiple sclerosis — pathology, phenotypes, diagnosis

**Pathology:** chronic immune-mediated inflammatory demyelination of the CNS with axonal loss. Plaques are perivenular and favour the periventricular white matter, corpus callosum, juxtacortical region, optic nerves, brainstem/cerebellar peduncles and spinal cord. **Prevalence rises with distance from the equator**; peak onset 20–40 years; female:male ~3:1; risk factors include low vitamin D, **Epstein–Barr virus infection** (now regarded as a near-necessary prerequisite ⚑), smoking, adolescent obesity, and HLA-DRB1*15:01.

**Phenotypes:**

| Phenotype | Course |
|---|---|
| **Clinically isolated syndrome** | First demyelinating episode; may or may not go on to MS |
| **Relapsing–remitting (~85% at onset)** | Discrete relapses with partial or full recovery |
| **Secondary progressive** | Steady progression after an initial relapsing course (most RRMS eventually) |
| **Primary progressive (~10–15%)** | Progressive from onset; older, more male, more spinal cord |
| **Radiologically isolated syndrome** | Incidental typical MRI lesions without symptoms ⚑ — now diagnosable as MS in some circumstances |

**Common presentations:** **optic neuritis** (subacute painful monocular visual loss, pain on eye movement, red desaturation, RAPD), **partial transverse myelitis** (sensory level, Lhermitte's, bladder symptoms), brainstem syndromes (diplopia, **internuclear ophthalmoplegia**, vertigo, trigeminal neuralgia in a young person), cerebellar syndromes, fatigue, cognitive change. **Uhthoff's phenomenon** — transient worsening with heat.

**Diagnosis — dissemination in space (DIS) and time (DIT).** The 2017 McDonald criteria required lesions in ≥2 of four typical locations (periventricular, cortical/juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord) for DIS, and either a new lesion over time, simultaneous enhancing and non-enhancing lesions, or **CSF-specific oligoclonal bands** as a substitute for DIT.

⚑ **The 2024 revisions (published 2025) change several things:**
- The **optic nerve** is added as a **fifth topography** for dissemination in space (evidenced by MRI, OCT or visual evoked potentials).
- **Central vein sign** and **paramagnetic rim lesions** are incorporated as MS-specific imaging biomarkers.
- **Kappa free light chains** in CSF are accepted as an alternative to oligoclonal bands.
- When enough topographies are involved, **separate evidence of dissemination in time is no longer required**.
- MS can now be diagnosed in **asymptomatic** individuals (previously labelled radiologically isolated syndrome) when supportive biomarkers are present.
- The stated trade-off is a risk of **overdiagnosis** — the criteria are only valid once mimics have been excluded.

**Investigations:** MRI brain and whole spine with gadolinium (**"Dawson's fingers"** — ovoid periventricular lesions perpendicular to the ventricles — are characteristic), CSF for oligoclonal bands unmatched in serum, visual evoked potentials, and blood tests to exclude mimics (**AQP4 and MOG antibodies, ANA/ENA, ACE, B12, HIV, syphilis, borrelia where relevant**).

**Mimics to exclude:** NMOSD, MOGAD, neurosarcoidosis, SLE/Sjögren's, Behçet's, vasculitis, B12 deficiency, HIV, syphilis, Lyme, CADASIL, small-vessel ischaemia, HTLV-1 myelopathy, ADEM.

#### 8.2 MS — relapse management and disease-modifying therapy

**Relapse:** new or worsening symptoms lasting **>24 hours** in the absence of infection or fever, ≥30 days from the last relapse. **First exclude a pseudo-relapse** — infection (especially urinary), fever, metabolic upset unmasking old deficits; treating the infection is the treatment.

**Acute relapse treatment:** **methylprednisolone 500 mg orally for 5 days** (or 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days if severe or unable to take oral). Steroids shorten the relapse but do not change long-term disability. Plasma exchange for severe steroid-refractory relapse.

**Disease-modifying therapy** — for relapsing MS; the direction of travel is towards **earlier use of higher-efficacy agents** ⚑.

| Class/drug | Route | Key risk to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Interferon beta, glatiramer acetate | SC/IM | Modest efficacy; flu-like symptoms; injection reactions; safe in pregnancy |
| **Teriflunomide** | Oral | **Teratogenic** — needs washout with cholestyramine; hepatotoxicity |
| **Dimethyl fumarate** | Oral | Flushing, GI upset, **lymphopenia → PML risk** |
| **Sphingosine-1-phosphate modulators** (fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod) | Oral | **First-dose bradycardia**, macular oedema, VZV reactivation, **rebound disease on stopping** |
| **Cladribine** | Oral (short courses) | Lymphopenia, herpes zoster, contraception for 6 months |
| **Natalizumab** (anti-α4-integrin) | IV/SC | **Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy** — risk rises with **JC virus antibody positivity**, long duration and prior immunosuppression |
| **Anti-CD20** (ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, rituximab, ublituximab) | IV/SC | Infusion reactions, hypogammaglobulinaemia, infections; **ocrelizumab is the only licensed DMT for primary progressive MS** |
| **Alemtuzumab** (anti-CD52) | IV | Powerful but **secondary autoimmunity — thyroid disease (~40%), ITP, anti-GBM nephritis**; needs 5 years of monthly monitoring |
| **Autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** | — | For highly active disease failing therapy ⚑ |

**Progressive MS:** ⚑ **ocrelizumab** for early inflammatory PPMS; **siponimod** for active SPMS. Otherwise the focus is symptom control and rehabilitation.

**Symptomatic management:** spasticity — baclofen, tizanidine, gabapentin, botulinum toxin, **nabiximols** in refractory cases; **fatigue** — exercise, sleep, amantadine/modafinil (limited evidence); bladder — anticholinergics, intermittent self-catheterisation (check post-void residual first); neuropathic pain — gabapentinoids, amitriptyline, duloxetine; **fampridine** for walking speed in selected patients; depression, sexual dysfunction, osteoporosis, vitamin D.

#### 8.3 NMOSD and MOGAD ⚑

These are **not** MS and must be identified because MS drugs (interferon, natalizumab, fingolimod) can make NMOSD worse.

| | **Multiple sclerosis** | **NMOSD (AQP4-IgG)** | **MOGAD (MOG-IgG)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody | None | **Aquaporin-4 IgG** (astrocytopathy) | **MOG-IgG** (oligodendrocyte myelin) |
| Demographics | F>M 3:1, 20–40 y, Northern European | **Strong female predominance, often Afro-Caribbean or East Asian, older onset** | Children and adults, no sex bias |
| Optic neuritis | Usually unilateral, mild-moderate | **Bilateral, severe, posterior/chiasmal, poor recovery** | **Bilateral, severe, with optic disc swelling, but good steroid response** |
| Myelitis | Short segment, partial | **Longitudinally extensive (≥3 vertebral segments)** | Longitudinally extensive, often conus |
| Other clues | Dawson's fingers, cognitive change | **Area postrema syndrome — intractable hiccups, nausea and vomiting** | ADEM phenotype in children; steroid-dependent relapses |
| CSF bands | Positive in >85% | Usually **negative** | Usually negative |
| Acute treatment | Steroids | High-dose steroids **plus early plasma exchange** | High-dose steroids (very responsive) |
| Maintenance | MS DMTs | **Rituximab, eculizumab, satralizumab, inebilizumab** ⚑ | Steroid-sparing (azathioprine, MMF, rituximab, IVIg) if relapsing |
| MS drugs | — | **Harmful** | Not indicated |

> **Hook:** *Longitudinally extensive myelitis + bilateral severe optic neuritis + hiccups = check AQP4.*

#### 8.4 Optic neuritis, transverse myelitis and ADEM

**Optic neuritis:** subacute (hours–days) monocular loss of acuity and colour vision, **pain worse on eye movement**, central scotoma, **RAPD**; the disc is normal in two-thirds ("the patient sees nothing and the doctor sees nothing" — retrobulbar). Recovery over weeks is usual; **optic disc pallor** and a **delayed VEP P100** persist. MRI brain determines the risk of MS. High-dose steroids speed recovery but do not change final acuity. **Atypical features — bilateral, severe, no recovery, disc swelling, older patient — mean test for AQP4/MOG and consider GCA or compressive causes.**

**Transverse myelitis:** acute or subacute cord dysfunction with a sensory level, weakness and sphincter disturbance. Always image urgently to **exclude compression first**. Causes: MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, post-infectious, SLE/Sjögren's, sarcoidosis, infections (VZV, HSV, enterovirus, schistosomiasis), paraneoplastic, and **spinal cord infarction** (which is sudden and painful, with sparing of dorsal columns).

**ADEM:** monophasic, usually post-infectious or post-vaccination, more common in children; **encephalopathy is required** for the diagnosis; large, poorly demarcated, simultaneously enhancing lesions. Treated with high-dose steroids ± IVIg/plasma exchange. **Recurrence should prompt MOG antibody testing.**

#### 8.5 Bacterial meningitis

**Organisms by group:**

| Group | Commonest organisms |
|---|---|
| Neonates | Group B streptococcus, *E. coli*, **Listeria** |
| Children/young adults | *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, (Hib if unvaccinated) |
| Adults | ***Streptococcus pneumoniae*** (commonest overall in adults), *N. meningitidis* |
| **>60 years, pregnancy, immunosuppressed, alcohol excess** | Add ***Listeria monocytogenes*** |
| Post-neurosurgery, shunts, penetrating trauma | Staphylococci, Gram-negatives |
| Basal skull fracture/CSF leak | *S. pneumoniae* |

**Recognition:** the classic triad of fever, neck stiffness and altered mental state is present in fewer than half — **most have at least two of headache, fever, neck stiffness and altered consciousness**. Look for photophobia, vomiting, seizures, focal signs, and the **non-blanching petechial/purpuric rash of meningococcal sepsis** (a late sign; its absence means nothing). Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are specific but insensitive.

**Immediate management (NICE NG240, 2024, and UK Joint Specialist Societies guidance):**
1. Recognise, resuscitate; **blood cultures and bloods (including meningococcal/pneumococcal PCR)**.
2. ⚑ **Antibiotics within 1 hour of arrival** — do not wait for imaging or LP. In the community, benzylpenicillin IM/IV before transfer if meningococcal disease is suspected and transfer will be delayed.
3. **Empirical therapy: IV ceftriaxone 2 g twice daily** (or cefotaxime). **Add amoxicillin/ampicillin 2 g 4-hourly for *Listeria* cover if over 60 years, pregnant, immunosuppressed, or with alcohol excess/malnutrition.** Add vancomycin if there is a risk of penicillin-resistant pneumococcus (recent travel to high-prevalence areas). Aciclovir if encephalitis is also possible.
4. **Dexamethasone 10 mg 6-hourly for 4 days**, given **with or just before the first dose of antibiotics** — reduces hearing loss and mortality in pneumococcal meningitis. Avoid or stop if meningococcal sepsis or septic shock predominates, or in post-surgical/immunosuppressed cases.
5. **CT before LP** only if: reduced or fluctuating consciousness (GCS <12 or a fall of ≥2), focal neurological signs, new seizures, papilloedema, severe immunocompromise, or signs of raised ICP/shock. **A normal CT does not exclude raised ICP.**
6. **LP as soon as it is safe** (ideally within 1 hour of arrival if no contraindication): opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose (with **paired serum glucose**), Gram stain, culture, PCR, lactate.
7. Contact tracing and **prophylaxis for close contacts** of meningococcal disease: **ciprofloxacin single dose** (first line; rifampicin an alternative), plus vaccination as advised by public health. Notify the local health protection team — meningococcal disease is **notifiable**.

**Complications:** septic shock and DIC, hearing loss (**all survivors need audiology**), hydrocephalus, subdural empyema, cerebral abscess, venous sinus thrombosis, seizures, cognitive impairment, cranial nerve palsies, SIADH, **Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome** (adrenal haemorrhage in meningococcal sepsis).

#### 8.6 Viral and autoimmune encephalitis

**Encephalitis = brain parenchymal inflammation → altered mental state, personality or behaviour change, seizures, focal deficits, fever.** The discriminator from meningitis is **altered brain function**, not just meningism.

**Herpes simplex encephalitis (HSV-1)** — the commonest sporadic viral encephalitis and the treatable one:
- Fever, confusion, **prominent behavioural and memory change**, dysphasia, seizures.
- MRI: **asymmetric temporal and inferior frontal lobe** oedema/haemorrhage; EEG: **temporal periodic lateralised epileptiform discharges**.
- CSF: lymphocytosis, raised protein, normal glucose, **red cells may be present**; **HSV PCR** (may be falsely negative in the first 24–48 hours — repeat if suspicion is high).
- **Start IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg 8-hourly immediately on suspicion**, for 14–21 days. Delay is the main determinant of poor outcome; untreated mortality ~70%.

**Other infective causes:** VZV, enterovirus, EBV, CMV and HHV-6 (immunosuppressed), measles (SSPE), mumps, HIV seroconversion, arboviruses (Japanese encephalitis, West Nile, Murray Valley/Kunjin in Australia ⚑, tick-borne encephalitis), rabies, *Listeria* rhombencephalitis, cerebral malaria.

**Autoimmune encephalitis** ⚑ — increasingly recognised and treatable:

| Antibody | Picture | Association |
|---|---|---|
| **Anti-NMDA receptor** | Young woman; prodrome → psychiatric symptoms/psychosis → seizures → **orofacial dyskinesias**, autonomic instability, reduced consciousness | **Ovarian teratoma** — image the pelvis |
| **LGI1** | Older man; hyponatraemia; **faciobrachial dystonic seizures**; amnesia | Rarely tumour |
| **CASPR2** | Morvan syndrome, neuromyotonia, insomnia | Thymoma |
| **GABA-B** | Prominent seizures | Small cell lung cancer |
| Classic paraneoplastic (Hu, Ma2, CV2) | Limbic encephalitis, brainstem, cerebellar | SCLC, testicular, others |

Treatment: **remove the tumour** where present, plus first-line immunotherapy (steroids, IVIg, plasma exchange) and second-line rituximab/cyclophosphamide. Recovery can be slow but is often substantial.

#### 8.7 Brain abscess, TB and cryptococcal meningitis

**Brain abscess:** headache, fever, focal deficit — but the classic triad is present in a minority. Sources: contiguous (otitis, mastoiditis, sinusitis, dental), haematogenous (endocarditis, lung, cyanotic congenital heart disease with right-to-left shunt), penetrating trauma, neurosurgery. Imaging: **ring-enhancing lesion with a smooth thin wall and restricted diffusion on DWI** (which distinguishes it from tumour). **Do not LP.** Treatment: neurosurgical aspiration/excision plus prolonged IV antibiotics (typically ceftriaxone + metronidazole ± vancomycin), and dexamethasone only for significant mass effect.

**Tuberculous meningitis:** subacute over weeks — headache, low-grade fever, personality change, then **cranial nerve palsies (especially VI), hydrocephalus and basal exudates with enhancement on imaging**; stroke from vasculitis of perforators. CSF: **lymphocytosis, very high protein (often >1–5 g/L), low glucose**, AFB smear (low yield), **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra**, culture. Treat empirically on suspicion: **RIPE (rifampicin, isoniazid + pyridoxine, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) for 2 months then rifampicin/isoniazid — 12 months total for CNS TB — plus adjunctive corticosteroids**, which reduce mortality.

**Cryptococcal meningitis:** in HIV with CD4 <100 (also other immunosuppression). Insidious headache, fever, **very high opening pressure**. CSF **India ink** and **cryptococcal antigen (CrAg — the test of choice, highly sensitive)**. Treat with **liposomal amphotericin B + flucytosine induction, then fluconazole**, and — critically — **serial therapeutic lumbar punctures to control intracranial pressure**, which is the main determinant of early mortality. Beware immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome when antiretrovirals are started.

#### 8.8 Neurosyphilis, HIV and opportunistic infection

**Neurosyphilis** ("the great imitator") — early forms: asymptomatic, meningeal, meningovascular (stroke in a young person). Late: **general paresis** (dementia, psychiatric change) and **tabes dorsalis** (lightning pains, sensory ataxia, **Argyll Robertson pupils**, absent reflexes, Charcot joints). Diagnose with serum treponemal tests plus CSF (VDRL specific but insensitive; raised protein and lymphocytes). Treat with **IV benzylpenicillin** for 14 days; warn about the **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction**.

**HIV neurology by CD4 count:**

| CD4 | Condition |
|---|---|
| Any | Seroconversion meningoencephalitis, distal sensory polyneuropathy, HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder, inflammatory demyelinating neuropathy |
| <200 | **Cerebral toxoplasmosis** (multiple ring-enhancing lesions in basal ganglia, treat with pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine; response confirms the diagnosis), **primary CNS lymphoma** (single periventricular enhancing lesion, EBV in CSF, thallium-avid) |
| <100 | **Cryptococcal meningitis**, CMV retinitis and polyradiculitis |
| <50–200 | **Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy** — JC virus; multifocal non-enhancing white matter lesions, no mass effect, progressive deficits; treat by restoring immunity (also seen with natalizumab, rituximab and other immunosuppressants) |

#### 8.9 Tetanus, rabies and prion disease

**Tetanus:** *Clostridium tetani* toxin blocks inhibitory neurotransmitter release → **trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus, opisthotonus, muscle spasms with preserved consciousness**, autonomic instability. Treat with wound debridement, **human tetanus immunoglobulin**, metronidazole, benzodiazepines/magnesium, ventilation, and immunisation (infection does not confer immunity).

**Rabies:** long variable incubation; **hydrophobia, aerophobia, agitation** (furious form) or ascending paralysis (paralytic form); **almost invariably fatal once symptomatic** — the whole management is post-exposure prophylaxis (wound washing, vaccine ± immunoglobulin).

**Prion disease (sporadic CJD):** rapidly progressive dementia over months with **myoclonus**, ataxia, visual disturbance, akinetic mutism. Investigations: **EEG periodic sharp wave complexes**, MRI **cortical ribboning and pulvinar/hockey-stick sign (variant CJD)**, CSF **14-3-3 protein and RT-QuIC** (the most useful modern test ⚑). No treatment; invariably fatal. Variants: variant CJD (bovine spongiform encephalopathy, younger, psychiatric onset, painful sensory symptoms), familial (PRNP mutation), iatrogenic.

#### 8.10 Autoimmune and paraneoplastic neurology (overview)

| Syndrome | Antibody | Tumour |
|---|---|---|
| Limbic encephalitis | Hu, Ma2, LGI1, GABA-B | SCLC, testicular |
| **Lambert–Eaton** | **P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channel** | **Small cell lung cancer** |
| Paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration | **Yo** (also Tr, Hu) | **Breast, ovarian**, Hodgkin lymphoma |
| Opsoclonus–myoclonus | Ri | Neuroblastoma (children), breast/lung |
| Stiff person syndrome | **GAD65**, amphiphysin | Breast |
| Sensory neuronopathy | Hu | SCLC |
| Dermatomyositis | Mi-2, TIF1-γ, NXP2 | Ovarian, lung, GI (adults) |

The general rule: **a subacute, otherwise unexplained neurological syndrome in a smoker or in someone with weight loss deserves antibody testing and a CT chest/abdomen/pelvis ± PET.**

| Investigation | Vitals (febrile) and examination (focal neuro deficits)Bloods- FBC (↑WCC- lymphocytes in viral, neutrophils in bacterial), ↑CRP, EUC, serum PCR (viral)Throat swab, urine MCS, swabbing sites of infectionLumbar puncture: opening pressure (ICP), cell count, glucose, protein, microscopy, stain, PCRCT head, MRI: for HSV encephalitis (medial temporal lobe necrosis), EEG shows early focal slowing and periodic discharges  EEG: seizure activity Brain tissue biopsy guided via imaging if diagnosis is unclear |
| Management | Supportive: fluid (maintain BP, ↓cerebral perfusion), bed rest, ↑ head of bed, monitor vitalsTreat: IV antibiotics if bacterial, IV acyclovir (10 mg/kg/8h IV over 1h) until HSV encephalitis is ruled outVaccinations after recovery |
| Complications | HSV encephalitis 🏳 has a high mortality rateComplications: stroke, seizures, memory loss, hypothalamic/autonomic dysfunction |
