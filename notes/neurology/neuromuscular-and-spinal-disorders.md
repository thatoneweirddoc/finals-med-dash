## Neuromuscular and Spinal Disorders


### Neurology Master Notes — Neuromuscular and Spinal Disorders

#### 10.1 Peripheral neuropathy — pattern first, cause second

**Step 1 — what is the pattern?**

| Pattern | Meaning | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Symmetrical distal sensorimotor (glove and stocking)** | Length-dependent axonal | **Diabetes**, alcohol, B12 deficiency, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, drugs, paraprotein, idiopathic |
| **Predominantly motor, symmetrical, proximal and distal** | Demyelinating | **GBS** (acute), **CIDP** (chronic), CMT1, lead, porphyria |
| **Predominantly sensory / small fibre** | Small fibre or sensory ganglionopathy | Diabetes, amyloid, Sjögren's, **B6 excess**, paraneoplastic (anti-Hu), HIV |
| **Mononeuritis multiplex** — several individual nerves, asymmetric, painful | Vasculitic/infiltrative | **Vasculitis (ANCA, PAN, rheumatoid)**, diabetes, sarcoid, leprosy, HIV, amyloid, lymphoma |
| **Autonomic** | Small fibre/autonomic | Diabetes, amyloidosis, GBS, paraneoplastic |

**Step 2 — is it axonal or demyelinating (nerve conduction studies)?** Axonal = **reduced amplitudes** with relatively preserved velocity. Demyelinating = **slowed conduction velocity, prolonged distal latencies, conduction block, prolonged/absent F-waves**.

**Step 3 — routine screen:** glucose/HbA1c, **B12 and folate**, U&E, LFT, TFT, FBC, ESR/CRP, **serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation**, HIV and syphilis serology; then targeted (ANCA, ANA/ENA, ACE, anti-ganglioside, genetic, nerve biopsy if vasculitis or amyloid suspected).

**Neuropathic pain management** (see Section 12): amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin as first-line options; **capsaicin cream for localised pain**; **carbamazepine only for trigeminal neuralgia**; foot care and falls prevention.

#### 10.2 Guillain–Barré syndrome and CIDP

**GBS — acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy.**

- **Trigger** in ~two-thirds: *Campylobacter jejuni* (associated with the axonal AMAN variant and worse recovery), CMV, EBV, *Mycoplasma*, Zika, hepatitis E, HIV, surgery, vaccination (rarely).
- **Presentation:** progressive, **ascending, symmetrical weakness** with **areflexia**, developing over days to a nadir by **≤4 weeks** (usually 2); back and limb pain is common and often precedes weakness; sensory symptoms are usually mild relative to weakness; **autonomic instability** (arrhythmias, labile BP, ileus) in up to two-thirds; **facial and bulbar weakness** and **respiratory failure in ~20–30%**.
- **Investigations:** CSF **albuminocytological dissociation** (raised protein, normal cells — often normal in the first week); nerve conduction studies (demyelinating features, may lag); anti-ganglioside antibodies (**GQ1b in Miller Fisher**); stool/serology for *Campylobacter*. **A CSF pleocytosis >50 cells should make you consider HIV, Lyme, sarcoid or malignant infiltration instead.**
- **Variants:** **Miller Fisher syndrome — ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia (anti-GQ1b)**; AMAN/AMSAN (axonal); pharyngeal-cervical-brachial; Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis.
- **Management:** **monitor FVC and autonomic status closely** (see Section 5.6); **IVIg 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days OR plasma exchange** — equally effective, **do not combine**; **steroids are ineffective and should not be used** ⚑ (a common exam point); VTE prophylaxis, pain control (gabapentinoids), physiotherapy, and ICU if FVC falls.
- **Prognosis:** ~80% walk independently at 6 months; mortality 3–7%; poor prognostic markers are older age, rapid onset, need for ventilation, preceding diarrhoea/*Campylobacter*, and low distal motor amplitudes.

**CIDP** — the chronic counterpart: progression over **>8 weeks**, relapsing or progressive, symmetric proximal *and* distal weakness with sensory loss and areflexia. Unlike GBS, **it responds to steroids**, as well as to IVIg and plasma exchange; long-term immunosuppression or maintenance IVIg is often needed.

> **Hook:** *GBS — steroids don't work. CIDP — they do.*

#### 10.3 Hereditary and diabetic neuropathies

**Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy):** commonest inherited neuropathy; **CMT1A (autosomal dominant, PMP22 duplication)** is the commonest subtype and is demyelinating. Features: childhood-to-early-adult onset, **pes cavus, hammer toes, "inverted champagne bottle" legs**, distal wasting and weakness, foot drop, areflexia, mild sensory loss, palpably thickened nerves. Management is supportive (orthotics, physiotherapy, surgery), plus genetic counselling; **avoid neurotoxic drugs (vincristine)**.

**Diabetic neuropathies — know that there are several:**

| Type | Picture |
|---|---|
| Distal symmetrical polyneuropathy | Commonest; glove-and-stocking, painful, with foot ulceration risk |
| Autonomic neuropathy | Postural hypotension, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction, silent ischaemia, fixed tachycardia |
| **Diabetic amyotrophy (proximal diabetic neuropathy / lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy)** | Severe thigh pain, then proximal wasting and weakness, weight loss; often after improved glycaemic control; usually recovers over months |
| Mononeuropathy | Median, ulnar, or **cranial (classically a pupil-sparing third nerve palsy)** |
| Mononeuritis multiplex | Vasculitic |

#### 10.4 Mononeuropathies and entrapments

| Nerve | Syndrome | Key signs and tests | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Median at wrist** | **Carpal tunnel syndrome** | Nocturnal paraesthesia in the radial 3½ digits, shaken out; **thenar wasting and weak thumb abduction**; **Tinel and Phalen tests**; associations: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly, obesity, amyloid | Splint, steroid injection, **surgical decompression** if severe or with wasting |
| Ulnar at elbow | Cubital tunnel | Weak interossei, **Froment's sign**, claw hand, sensory loss over the little finger **and the dorsal ulnar hand** (which distinguishes it from wrist lesions) | Avoid elbow pressure; decompression |
| Radial in spiral groove | **Saturday night palsy** | **Wrist drop**, weak finger extension, triceps spared, sensory loss in the anatomical snuffbox | Splint; usually recovers |
| Common peroneal at fibular neck | **Foot drop** | Weak dorsiflexion **and eversion**, inversion intact, ankle jerk intact | Avoid crossing legs/casts; splint |
| Lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh | **Meralgia paraesthetica** | Burning numbness over the lateral thigh, **no motor signs** | Weight loss, loose clothing, reassurance |
| Long thoracic | Winged scapula | Serratus anterior weakness | Usually recovers |
| Posterior interosseous | Finger drop with preserved wrist extension | No sensory loss | — |

#### 10.5 Radiculopathy and plexopathy

**Cervical radiculopathy:** neck and arm pain in a dermatomal distribution, worse on neck extension/rotation (**Spurling's test**), with segmental weakness and a lost reflex. C6 (biceps/supinator, thumb), **C7 (triceps, middle finger — commonest)**, C8. Most improve with conservative management; MRI and surgery for progressive deficit or myelopathy.

**Cervical myelopathy** — the diagnosis not to miss in an older patient with neck problems: **clumsy hands, gait disturbance, brisk reflexes below the level, Hoffmann's sign, inverted supinator jerk, Lhermitte's, sphincter change**. Requires MRI and surgical opinion; delay causes irreversible cord injury.

**Lumbosacral radiculopathy (sciatica):** radicular leg pain below the knee ± dermatomal sensory loss, myotomal weakness, lost reflex; positive **straight-leg raise**. **L5 (foot drop with weak inversion and hip abduction, first web space) and S1 (weak plantarflexion, absent ankle jerk, lateral foot)** are the commonest. NICE NG59: most settle; **do not offer imaging in a non-specialist setting**; treat with exercise and analgesia (NSAIDs; **not** gabapentinoids for sciatica ⚑), and consider epidural injection or surgery for severe or persistent radicular pain. **Screen every episode for cauda equina red flags.**

**Plexopathy:**
- **Neuralgic amyotrophy (Parsonage–Turner)** — abrupt severe shoulder pain for days to weeks, followed by **patchy proximal arm weakness and wasting as the pain settles**; often post-infection or post-vaccination; recovery over months.
- Traumatic brachial plexus injury; **Erb's palsy** (C5/6, waiter's tip) and **Klumpke's** (C8/T1, claw hand ± Horner's).
- **Pancoast tumour** — lower plexus (C8/T1) pain and wasting with **Horner's syndrome**.
- Radiation plexopathy (painless, with myokymia) vs malignant infiltration (painful, with Horner's) — a classic discriminator in breast cancer follow-up.

#### 10.6 Neuromuscular junction disorders

##### Myasthenia gravis

**Pathophysiology:** IgG autoantibodies against the **postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor** (~85% of generalised, ~50% of ocular), or against **MuSK** (~5–8%; more bulbar and respiratory, poor response to anticholinesterases) or LRP4. **Bimodal epidemiology:** young women (20s–30s, associated with **thymic hyperplasia** and other autoimmune disease) and older men (60s–70s, associated with **thymoma**).

**Clinical picture:** **fatigable weakness** — worse with use and at the end of the day, better with rest.
- **Ocular in >50% at onset (and remains purely ocular in ~15%)**: asymmetric ptosis (worsens on sustained upgaze), variable diplopia. **Pupils are always spared** — a crucial discriminator from third nerve palsy and botulism.
- Bulbar: dysarthria that deteriorates as the patient speaks, dysphagia, nasal regurgitation, jaw fatigue.
- Limb: proximal weakness; neck extensor weakness ("dropped head").
- Reflexes and sensation are normal.

**Investigations:** **serum AChR antibodies**, then **MuSK** if negative; **repetitive nerve stimulation showing a decrement**; **single-fibre EMG (most sensitive)**; ice-pack test for ptosis at the bedside; **CT/MRI thorax for thymoma in everyone**; screen for associated thyroid disease and other autoimmunity. (The edrophonium/Tensilon test is largely historical because of cardiac risk ⚑.)

**Treatment:**
1. **Pyridostigmine** (symptomatic; muscarinic side effects — cramps, diarrhoea, salivation).
2. **Corticosteroids** — start low and titrate up, because rapid initiation can transiently worsen weakness; add a steroid-sparing agent (azathioprine — check **TPMT** first, mycophenolate, methotrexate, ciclosporin, tacrolimus).
3. **Thymectomy** — mandatory for thymoma; also beneficial in **AChR-positive generalised MG under ~50–65 years without thymoma** (MGTX trial) ⚑.
4. **Crisis or severe exacerbation: IVIg or plasma exchange**, plus ICU-level respiratory monitoring.
5. ⚑ **Newer targeted therapies for refractory generalised MG:** **complement inhibitors** (eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan — for AChR-positive disease; require **meningococcal vaccination**) and **neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) blockers** (efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab — accelerate IgG clearance). Rituximab is particularly effective in **MuSK-positive** disease.

**Drugs that worsen myasthenia — a favourite exam question:** **aminoglycosides, macrolides, fluoroquinolones**, telithromycin (contraindicated), **beta-blockers**, calcium channel blockers, magnesium (including IV magnesium in obstetrics), **quinine and quinolones**, phenytoin, lithium, penicillamine (can cause MG), procainamide, statins, botulinum toxin, iodinated contrast, and neuromuscular blocking agents (extreme sensitivity to non-depolarising blockers).

**Myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis:**

| | **Myasthenic crisis** | **Cholinergic crisis** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Undertreatment, infection, surgery, drugs, pregnancy | Excess anticholinesterase |
| Pupils | Normal | **Miosis** |
| Secretions | Normal | **Salivation, lacrimation, bronchorrhoea** |
| GI | Normal | Diarrhoea, cramps |
| Heart rate | Tachycardia | **Bradycardia** |
| Fasciculations | Absent | Present |
| Management | IVIg/plasma exchange, steroids, ventilate | Withhold pyridostigmine, atropine for muscarinic effects, support |

##### Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome

Antibodies to **presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels**; **~50–60% are paraneoplastic, almost always small cell lung cancer** — so investigate for malignancy (CT chest, and repeat surveillance if initially negative).

**Discriminators from MG:** **proximal lower-limb weakness first** (difficulty rising from a chair), **hyporeflexia that returns after brief exercise (post-tetanic potentiation)**, **prominent autonomic features (dry mouth, constipation, erectile dysfunction)**, **ocular involvement is mild or absent**, and **strength briefly improves with sustained activity**. Repetitive nerve stimulation shows an **increment** at high frequency. Treat the tumour; symptomatic treatment with **3,4-diaminopyridine (amifampridine)**, plus immunotherapy.

##### Botulism

Preformed toxin (canned/home-preserved food), wound botulism (injecting drug use), or infant botulism (honey). **Descending, symmetrical, flaccid paralysis with prominent bulbar onset (diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia), fixed dilated pupils, dry mouth, and no sensory loss or fever.** Treat with antitoxin, supportive ventilation, and (for wound botulism) debridement and antibiotics.

> **Discriminator triad:** *MG spares the pupils; botulism dilates them; LEMS makes the mouth dry and the reflexes come back after exercise.*

#### 10.7 Myopathies

**Recognition:** **symmetrical proximal weakness** (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, washing hair), **no sensory loss**, preserved reflexes until late, ± myalgia, ± raised CK.

| Group | Examples | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| **Inflammatory** | **Dermatomyositis** — heliotrope rash, **Gottron's papules**, shawl sign; **strong malignancy association in adults**. **Polymyositis**. **Inclusion body myositis** — over 50, **asymmetric, distal involvement (finger flexors and quadriceps)**, **poor steroid response** ⚑. **Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy** — very high CK, anti-HMGCR (statin-associated) or anti-SRP | Raised CK, EMG myopathic, MRI oedema, biopsy; treat with steroids ± immunosuppression (except IBM) |
| **Dystrophies** | **Duchenne** (X-linked, dystrophin absent; Gowers' sign, calf pseudohypertrophy, CK ×10–100, cardiomyopathy) and **Becker** (milder, reduced dystrophin); **myotonic dystrophy type 1** — CTG repeat in *DMPK*, **distal weakness, grip myotonia, frontal balding, cataracts, cardiac conduction block, diabetes, testicular atrophy, anticipation**; **facioscapulohumeral**; limb-girdle | Genetic diagnosis; cardiac and respiratory surveillance; corticosteroids and exon-skipping/gene therapies in DMD ⚑ |
| **Metabolic/endocrine** | Hypothyroid and hyperthyroid myopathy, Cushing's/steroid myopathy (**normal CK**), osteomalacia, hypokalaemia; McArdle disease (**second-wind phenomenon**, exercise-induced cramps and myoglobinuria) | Treat the cause |
| **Drug-induced** | **Statins** (myalgia to rhabdomyolysis; consider anti-HMGCR necrotising myopathy if it continues after stopping ⚑), corticosteroids, colchicine, amiodarone, antiretrovirals, alcohol | History |
| **Channelopathies** | Hypokalaemic and hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis, myotonia congenita | Episodic weakness, potassium at the time of an attack |
| **Mitochondrial** | MELAS, MERRF, **CPEO/Kearns–Sayre** | Maternal inheritance, ragged red fibres, lactate, multisystem |

**Myotonia** = delayed relaxation after contraction (patient cannot let go of a handshake; percussion myotonia). Seen in myotonic dystrophy, myotonia congenita and paramyotonia; treat with mexiletine if disabling.

#### 10.8 Spinal cord syndromes

(Anatomy and the full syndrome table are in Section 2.8 — here is the clinical approach.)

**First question in any myelopathy: is it compressive?** Compression is treatable and urgent — **image the whole spine with MRI**. Causes: **degenerative cervical spondylosis** (the commonest), **metastatic disease**, disc prolapse, epidural abscess (fever, back pain, focal tenderness, raised inflammatory markers, IV drug use or bacteraemia), haematoma (anticoagulation), trauma.

**Non-compressive causes:** transverse myelitis (MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, post-infectious), **spinal cord infarction** (sudden, painful, anterior cord pattern, often after aortic surgery or dissection), **B12 or copper deficiency (subacute combined degeneration)**, syringomyelia, dural arteriovenous fistula (a treatable cause of progressive myelopathy in older men), radiation, HTLV-1 myelopathy, hereditary spastic paraplegia.

**Subacute combined degeneration:** B12 deficiency (pernicious anaemia, ileal disease, metformin, vegan diet, **nitrous oxide abuse** ⚑ — an increasingly common cause in young people) affecting **dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts**: paraesthesia, sensory ataxia, **absent ankle jerks with extensor plantars**, and cognitive/psychiatric change. **Treat with intramuscular B12; neurological damage may be irreversible if treatment is delayed, and B12 must be replaced before folate** to avoid precipitating deterioration.

**Syringomyelia:** a fluid cavity in the central cord (often with Chiari I malformation, or post-traumatic). Classic picture: **"cape" distribution dissociated sensory loss (pain and temperature lost, dorsal columns preserved)**, painless burns and injuries, wasting of the small hand muscles, then long-tract signs; **Horner's syndrome** if it extends to T1. MRI diagnoses it; treatment is decompression of the underlying cause.

#### 10.9 Cranial neuropathies

**Bell's palsy:** acute (<72 h) **lower motor neurone** facial weakness — **the forehead is involved** — with ipsilateral loss of taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, hyperacusis, and sometimes retro-auricular pain.
- **The critical split:** **forehead sparing means an upper motor neurone lesion → stroke.** Bilateral facial palsy suggests **sarcoidosis, Lyme disease, GBS, HIV or bilateral parotid disease**.
- **Treatment: prednisolone 50 mg for 10 days (or 60 mg tapering), started within 72 hours** — clearly beneficial. Antivirals add little for Bell's palsy alone but are used if there is a zoster component. **Eye care is mandatory** — lubricants, taping at night, referral if the cornea is at risk.
- Prognosis: ~85% recover well; incomplete palsy carries the best outlook. Failure to improve by 3 months should prompt imaging (parotid tumour, cholesteatoma).

**Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus):** facial palsy **plus a painful vesicular rash in the ear canal/auricle or palate**, often with vertigo and hearing loss. Worse prognosis than Bell's; **treat with aciclovir plus prednisolone**.

**Third nerve palsy:** see Section 2.5 — pupil involvement means compression until proven otherwise.

**Sixth nerve palsy:** may be a false localising sign in raised ICP; also microvascular, cavernous sinus, and skull base pathology.

#### 10.10 Dizziness and vertigo

**Step 1 — is it vertigo (an illusion of movement) or something else** (presyncope, disequilibrium, anxiety)?

**Step 2 — peripheral or central?**

| | **Peripheral** | **Central** |
|---|---|---|
| Nystagmus | **Unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, suppressed by fixation** | **Direction-changing, vertical or pure torsional, not suppressed** |
| Hearing | Often affected (Ménière's, labyrinthitis) | Usually spared (except AICA stroke) |
| Other neurology | None | Present (diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, weakness) |
| Gait | Unsteady but can walk | **Cannot stand or walk unaided** |
| **HINTS exam** (in acute continuous vertigo) | **H**ead **I**mpulse **abnormal** (corrective saccade), **N**o direction-changing nystagmus, **N**o **T**est **o**f **S**kew | **Normal head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus, or skew deviation = central — HINTS "INFARCT"** ⚑ |

| Condition | Duration and trigger | Discriminator | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BPPV** | **Seconds**, on head turning/lying down | **Dix–Hallpike positive** with rotatory nystagmus and latency/fatigability | **Epley manoeuvre** |
| **Vestibular neuritis** | **Days**, continuous, after viral illness | No hearing loss; abnormal head impulse | Short-course vestibular sedatives (≤3 days), then **vestibular rehabilitation** |
| **Labyrinthitis** | Days | Vertigo **with hearing loss** | As above ± steroids |
| **Ménière's disease** | **Minutes to hours**, episodic | **Fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness** with vertigo | Salt restriction, betahistine, ENT referral |
| **Vestibular migraine** | Minutes–days | Migraine history, photophobia, headache often absent during attacks | Migraine preventives |
| **Posterior circulation stroke/TIA** | Sudden, continuous | Central features, vascular risk factors, **inability to walk** | Stroke pathway |
| Vestibular schwannoma | Chronic | **Progressive unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus** | MRI internal acoustic meatus |

> **Hook:** *Seconds = BPPV; minutes–hours with hearing symptoms = Ménière's; days = neuritis; sudden with an inability to walk = stroke.*

**Dr McGarity’s List for Peripheral Neuropathy**

- Genetic: Charcot-Marie Tooth
- Autoimmune: GBS, CIDP
- Toxins: EtOH, chemotherapy, heavy metal (Pb), fluoroquinolones, thalidomide, statins, HIV drugs
- Environmental: hypoxia, prolonged hypothermia, trauma
- Systemic: DM, amyloidosis, hypothyroid, ↓ vitamin B12, Lyme Disease, HIV

**Investigations**

- NEUROPHYSIOLOGY: assess presence and extent; only for large fibre function
- BLOODS: FBC, EUCs, LFTs, fasting blood sugar, glucose tolerance test, serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, B12, ESR, ANA/ANCA, HIV/syphillis

**Treatment**

- ANALGESIA: ↓ dose TCA (amitriptyline/gabapentin)
- SECONDARY PREVENTION: regular podiatry review and foot care

### Neurology (ANU) — vertigo — Neuromuscular and Spinal Disorders

- DEFINITION: illusion of rotational, linear, or tilting movement of self or environment, produced by peripheral (inner ear) or central (brainstem-cerebellum) stimulation
- Important to distinguish vertigo from other causes of “dizziness”
- Sense of balance is dependent on vestibular function (sensation of head position and movement in space), proprioception (joint position sense), and visual function
