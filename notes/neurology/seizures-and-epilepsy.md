## Seizures and Epilepsy


### Neurology Master Notes — Seizures and Epilepsy

#### 6.1 Classification (ILAE 2017) ⚑

The 2017 framework replaced "partial/simple/complex/secondary generalised" with plainer language. Classify at three levels: **seizure type → epilepsy type → epilepsy syndrome**, always asking about aetiology and comorbidity.

**The short version**

- Classify at three levels: **seizure type → epilepsy type → epilepsy syndrome**, plus aetiology and comorbidity.
- ⚑ Learn the renamed terms — **focal aware** (was simple partial), **focal impaired awareness** (was complex partial), **focal to bilateral tonic–clonic** (was secondary generalised).
- Onset is focal, generalised or unknown; aetiology is structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune or unknown.
- **Epilepsy** = two unprovoked seizures >24 h apart, **or** one plus ≥60% 10-year recurrence risk, **or** a defined syndrome.

| Old term | ⚑ Current term |
|---|---|
| Simple partial | **Focal aware** |
| Complex partial | **Focal impaired awareness** |
| Secondary generalised | **Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic** |
| Petit mal | Absence |
| Grand mal | Generalised tonic–clonic |

**Onset categories:** focal, generalised, unknown. Focal seizures are further described as **motor** or **non-motor** onset and by awareness. Generalised seizures: tonic–clonic, absence (typical/atypical), myoclonic, tonic, atonic, clonic.

**Aetiological groups:** structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, unknown.

**Definition of epilepsy (ILAE 2014, still current):** ⚑ any one of —
1. **Two** unprovoked seizures >24 hours apart;
2. **One** unprovoked seizure **plus** a ≥60% recurrence risk over 10 years (e.g. a structural lesion or clearly epileptiform EEG);
3. Diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.

#### 6.2 Semiology by lobe

**The short version**

- **Temporal** — the commonest focal epilepsy: rising epigastric aura, déjà vu, **lip-smacking/fumbling automatisms**, prolonged post-ictal confusion ± dysphasia if dominant.
- **Frontal** — brief, nocturnal, bizarre/hyperkinetic, **Jacksonian march**, **Todd's paresis**, and characteristically **minimal post-ictal confusion**.
- **Parietal** — contralateral paraesthesia and distorted body image; **occipital** — simple flashes/colours/circles (not migraine's fortification spectra).

| Lobe | Features |
|---|---|
| **Temporal** | Rising epigastric aura, déjà vu/jamais vu, fear, olfactory or gustatory hallucination, **lip-smacking and fumbling automatisms**, post-ictal confusion and dysphasia (if dominant). The commonest focal epilepsy |
| **Frontal** | Brief, nocturnal, hyperkinetic/bizarre motor activity, **Jacksonian march**, **Todd's paresis** post-ictally, head/eye version away from the focus, minimal post-ictal confusion |
| **Parietal** | Contralateral paraesthesia, distorted body image |
| **Occipital** | Simple visual hallucinations — flashes, colours, circles (contrast with the fortification spectra of migraine) |

#### 6.3 The first seizure

**Immediate questions:** was it a seizure at all (versus syncope, cardiac arrhythmia, functional attack, migraine, TIA, hypoglycaemia, parasomnia)? Was it **provoked**? Is there a structural cause?

**Features favouring a seizure over syncope:** aura, head-turning, unusual posturing, prolonged limb-jerking (>30 s), **lateral tongue biting** (highly specific), incontinence (less specific), cyanosis, prolonged post-ictal confusion (>5 min), amnesia for the event, occurrence in sleep. *Brief myoclonic jerks are common in syncope and do not make it a seizure.*

**Workup:** history from a witness (the single most valuable investigation), examination, ECG (**always — exclude long QT, Brugada, heart block**), glucose, electrolytes including calcium and magnesium, toxicology, pregnancy test where relevant. Then **EEG** (within 2 weeks per NICE; supports classification, does not confirm or exclude epilepsy) and **MRI brain** (NICE: within 6 weeks; especially if focal onset, onset <2 years or >60 years, or refractory).

**When to start treatment:** generally after a **second** unprovoked seizure. Consider after a first if there is a structural lesion, an unequivocally epileptiform EEG, a neurological deficit, or the patient judges the risk of another seizure unacceptable. Treating provoked seizures long-term is usually wrong — treat the provocation.

**Driving (UK DVLA, Group 1):** first unprovoked seizure — **6 months** off driving (12 months if there is an abnormality likely to cause further seizures); established epilepsy — **12 months seizure-free**; seizures only in sleep — may drive after an established pattern of ≥12 months of sleep-only seizures; withdrawal of medication — no driving during the change and for **6 months** afterwards. Group 2 (HGV/PSV) rules are far stricter (typically 10 years seizure-free off medication). **The patient must inform the DVLA; the doctor must document the advice.**

**Safety advice:** showers rather than baths, supervision when swimming, care with heights, ladders, machinery and cooking, and — for those of childbearing potential — pre-conception counselling.

#### 6.4 Epilepsy syndromes worth recognising

**The short version**

- Age of onset is the first filter: infancy → **West** (3–12 mo) and **Dravet**; 3–10 y → **Lennox–Gastaut**, **childhood absence**, **benign rolandic**; teens → **JME**.
- EEG signature names the syndrome: **3 Hz spike-and-wave** = childhood absence; **hypsarrhythmia** = West; **slow spike-and-wave <2.5 Hz** = Lennox–Gastaut.
- Drug of choice is syndrome-specific: **ethosuximide** (absence), **vigabatrin** ± steroids/ACTH (West, especially with tuberous sclerosis), levetiracetam/valproate (JME), **often nothing** for benign rolandic.
- Two "avoid" rules: in **JME** avoid carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, pregabalin (and lamotrigine can worsen myoclonus); in **Dravet** avoid sodium-channel blockers.

| Syndrome | Age | Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Childhood absence epilepsy** | 4–10 y | Brief blank spells ×10s/day, induced by hyperventilation; **3 Hz generalised spike-and-wave** | **Ethosuximide** first line |
| **Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy** | Teens | **Early-morning myoclonic jerks** (throwing the cereal bowl), GTCS, absences<br>• photosensitive<br>• sleep deprivation and alcohol provoke it<br>• usually lifelong treatment | Levetiracetam or valproate (valproate restricted — see 6.7); **avoid carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin and pregabalin — they aggravate myoclonus and absences; lamotrigine can also worsen myoclonus** |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Any | As 6.2; often **hippocampal sclerosis** on MRI; frequently drug-resistant | Lamotrigine/levetiracetam; consider surgery |
| **Infantile spasms (West syndrome)** | 3–12 mo | Salaam spasms in clusters, developmental regression, **hypsarrhythmia** on EEG; tuberous sclerosis association | **Vigabatrin** (especially with tuberous sclerosis) and/or steroids/ACTH |
| **Lennox–Gastaut** | 3–5 y | Multiple seizure types, tonic seizures in sleep, slow spike-and-wave <2.5 Hz, learning disability | Valproate, lamotrigine, rufinamide, cannabidiol |
| **Self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (benign rolandic)** | 4–10 y | Nocturnal hemifacial twitching, drooling, speech arrest; **excellent prognosis, remits in adolescence** | Often no treatment |
| **Dravet syndrome** | Infancy | Prolonged febrile hemiclonic seizures, SCN1A | Avoid sodium-channel blockers; valproate, clobazam, stiripentol, fenfluramine ⚑ |

#### 6.5 Provoked seizures and mimics

**Provoked (acute symptomatic):** hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, uraemia, alcohol withdrawal (typically 6–48 h after the last drink), benzodiazepine withdrawal, drugs lowering the threshold (tramadol, bupropion, theophylline, quinolones, clozapine), sepsis, eclampsia, meningitis/encephalitis, stroke, head injury, hypoxia.

**Febrile convulsions** — 6 months to 5 years, with fever, no CNS infection. **Simple** (generalised, <15 min, no recurrence in 24 h) carries a small increased risk of later epilepsy; **complex** (focal, >15 min, or recurrent within 24 h) more so.

**Mimics:** vasovagal syncope, cardiac syncope, functional/dissociative seizures, migraine with aura, TIA, panic attacks with hyperventilation, parasomnias, hypnic jerks, transient global amnesia, paroxysmal movement disorders.

#### 6.6 Antiseizure medication choice (NICE NG217) ⚑

**The short version**

- **Lamotrigine or levetiracetam** is the default answer for focal seizures ⚑ — carbamazepine has been demoted to add-on/alternative.
- Generalised tonic–clonic, tonic/atonic and idiopathic generalised epilepsy: lamotrigine, levetiracetam **or sodium valproate** (valproate restricted — 6.7).
- Two type-specific answers: **absence → ethosuximide**; **myoclonic → levetiracetam or valproate**.
- The "never in myoclonic/absence" list: **carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, pregabalin, tiagabine** — they worsen myoclonus and absences.

| Seizure type | First line | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Focal** | **Lamotrigine or levetiracetam** | ⚑ Carbamazepine is now an add-on/alternative, not first-line monotherapy |
| **Generalised tonic–clonic** | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam **or sodium valproate** | Valproate restricted (6.7) |
| **Absence** | **Ethosuximide** | Then valproate; lamotrigine/levetiracetam if valproate unsuitable |
| **Myoclonic** | **Levetiracetam or sodium valproate** | Avoid carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, pregabalin, tiagabine (**they worsen myoclonus and absences**) |
| **Tonic or atonic** | Lamotrigine or sodium valproate | Often in syndromic epilepsy |
| **Idiopathic generalised epilepsy** | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam or sodium valproate | |

**Principles:** monotherapy at the lowest effective dose, titrate slowly (especially lamotrigine — rapid escalation risks **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**), only add a second drug once monotherapy at maximum tolerated dose has failed, and remember **drug-resistant epilepsy = failure of two appropriate, well-tolerated drugs** → refer to a specialist centre for surgical/vagal nerve stimulator/ketogenic assessment.

#### 6.7 Pregnancy and the valproate rules ⚑

**Valproate is the most teratogenic of the common antiseizure medications:** ~10% risk of major congenital malformation (neural tube defects, cardiac, facial clefts) and ~30–40% risk of neurodevelopmental disorder in exposed children.

**MHRA/NICE position (2023–2024):** ⚑
- Valproate must **not be started in anyone under 55 years** (male or female) unless **two specialists independently document** that there is no other effective or tolerated option.
- Anyone of childbearing potential taking it must be in a **Pregnancy Prevention Programme** with annual specialist review and a signed risk acknowledgement form.
- ⚑ New: warnings extend to **men** — possible increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in children fathered while taking valproate; effective contraception is advised for men and for 3 months after stopping.
- Topiramate: similar Pregnancy Prevention Programme requirements ⚑ (teratogenicity and neurodevelopmental risk).

**Practical pregnancy advice:** **5 mg folic acid daily** pre-conception and through the first trimester; do not stop antiseizure medication abruptly — an uncontrolled tonic–clonic seizure is more dangerous than most drugs; **lamotrigine and levetiracetam have the best pregnancy safety data**; lamotrigine levels fall in pregnancy (oestrogen induces glucuronidation) so doses often need increasing and reducing again postpartum; enzyme-inducing drugs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, topiramate >200 mg) reduce the efficacy of hormonal contraception; breastfeeding is generally encouraged; UK Epilepsy and Pregnancy Register enrolment.

#### 6.8 SUDEP, refractory epilepsy and surgery

**SUDEP** (sudden unexpected death in epilepsy) — incidence roughly **1 in 1000 patient-years** overall, higher in refractory epilepsy. Risk factors: frequent generalised tonic–clonic seizures (the strongest), nocturnal seizures, poor adherence, polytherapy, sleeping alone, young adult male. Risk reduction: optimise seizure control and adherence; NICE requires SUDEP to be discussed with every patient.

**Refractory options:** epilepsy surgery (anterior temporal lobectomy for hippocampal sclerosis has the best evidence — many become seizure-free), lesionectomy, corpus callosotomy, **vagus nerve stimulation**, responsive neurostimulation/deep brain stimulation, **ketogenic diet** (particularly in children).

#### 6.9 Functional (dissociative) seizures

**The short version**

- Duration and pattern: epileptic events are **1–2 min, stereotyped and synchronous**; functional events are **prolonged (>5 min), waxing and waning, asynchronous** with pelvic thrusting and side-to-side head shaking.
- The eyes are the quickest discriminator — **open in epileptic seizures, forcefully closed and resisting opening in functional ones**.
- Supporting evidence: **lateral tongue bite** and post-ictal confusion with abnormal ictal EEG and possible prolactin rise favour epilepsy; normal ictal EEG and prolactin with rapid recovery (or unresponsiveness with normal vitals) favour functional.
- Diagnosis is **positive**, not by exclusion; management is psychological (explanation without accusation, CBT) — and **both can coexist**.

| Feature | Epileptic seizure | **Functional/dissociative** |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | 1–2 min | Often **prolonged (>5 min), waxing and waning** |
| Motor pattern | Stereotyped, synchronous | **Asynchronous** thrashing, **pelvic thrusting, side-to-side head shaking** |
| Eyes | **Open** | **Forcefully closed, resisting opening** |
| Vocalisation | Early cry, then quiet | Emotional crying, talking during the event |
| Injury/tongue bite | Lateral tongue bite | Tip of tongue, if any |
| Post-ictal | Confusion, prolonged | Rapid recovery or prolonged unresponsiveness with normal vitals |
| Investigations | Ictal EEG abnormal; **prolactin may rise** | Ictal EEG normal; prolactin normal |

Diagnosis is made on **positive** features, not by exclusion; management is psychological (explanation without accusation, CBT), not antiseizure medication. Note that both can coexist in the same patient.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators — Section 6

**The short version**

- Recognition rows cluster by semiology and EEG: temporal aura, frontal nocturnal events, Todd's paresis, **3 Hz spike-and-wave**, **hypsarrhythmia**, early-morning jerks.
- Drug rows are the ones to over-learn ⚑: focal = **lamotrigine/levetiracetam**, absence = **ethosuximide**, myoclonic = **levetiracetam/valproate**, plus the myoclonus-worsening list.
- The numbers get asked directly: **two** seizures for diagnosis, **two** failed drugs for drug-resistance, **5 mg** folic acid, **6/12 months** driving, **~1 in 1000** patient-years for SUDEP.

Split into two passes: recognition and first-line choices, then prescribing, pregnancy and safety.
