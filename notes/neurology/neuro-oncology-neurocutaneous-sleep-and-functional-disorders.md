## Neuro-oncology, Neurocutaneous, Sleep and Functional Disorders


### Neurology Master Notes — Neuro-oncology, Neurocutaneous, Sleep and Functional Disorders

*Sources: WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System, 5th edition (2021); NICE NG99 (brain tumours), NG234 (spinal metastases); Endocrine Society and UK pituitary guidance; International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3-TR); Stone et al. on functional neurological disorder; Kumar & Clark; Merck Manual Professional; StatPearls. ⚑ = changed from older textbooks.*

#### 11.1 Brain tumours

**How they present:** progressive focal deficit, **new-onset seizure in an adult** (the commonest presentation of a low-grade glioma), features of raised ICP (morning headache worse on lying flat/coughing, vomiting, papilloedema), personality or cognitive change, and — importantly — **any new focal neurology that evolves over weeks**.

**Metastases are more common than primary brain tumours.** Commonest primaries: **lung, breast, melanoma, renal, colorectal**. Typically **multiple, at the grey–white junction, with disproportionate vasogenic oedema and ring enhancement**.

**The short version**

- **Metastases outnumber primaries** (lung, breast, melanoma, renal, colorectal) — multiple, grey–white junction, disproportionate oedema, ring enhancement.
- Commonest **malignant primary in adults = glioblastoma** (butterfly, crosses the corpus callosum); commonest **primary overall = meningioma** (extra-axial, dural tail).
- ⚑ Adult diffuse gliomas are now classified **molecularly**: **IDH mutation**, plus **1p/19q co-deletion** for oligodendroglioma (better prognosis, chemosensitive).
- Two traps: **primary CNS lymphoma** — biopsy **before** steroids ("ghost tumour"); **bilateral vestibular schwannomas = NF2**.

| Tumour | Features |
|---|---|
| **Glioblastoma (IDH-wildtype, WHO grade 4)** | Commonest malignant primary brain tumour in adults<br>• rapid course<br>• **irregular thick ring enhancement with central necrosis, crossing the corpus callosum ("butterfly glioma")**<br>• median survival ~12–15 months with maximal treatment |
| **Diffuse astrocytoma / oligodendroglioma (IDH-mutant)** ⚑ | The 2021 WHO classification is **molecular**: adult diffuse gliomas are split by **IDH mutation** and, for oligodendroglioma, **1p/19q co-deletion** — which predicts better prognosis and chemosensitivity |
| **Meningioma** | Commonest primary intracranial tumour overall<br>• **extra-axial, dural-based, homogeneous enhancement with a "dural tail"**, may calcify<br>• usually benign<br>• more common in women, grows in pregnancy (progesterone receptors)<br>• often incidental and observed |
| **Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)** | **Cerebellopontine angle**: progressive unilateral sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, imbalance, then V (corneal reflex loss) and VII involvement; **bilateral = NF2** |
| **Pituitary adenoma** | See 11.2 |
| **Primary CNS lymphoma** | Immunosuppression (HIV) or immunocompetent; periventricular, homogeneously enhancing, **exquisitely steroid-responsive ("ghost tumour" — do not give steroids before biopsy)** |
| **Medulloblastoma / pilocytic astrocytoma / ependymoma** | Childhood posterior fossa tumours — morning headache, vomiting, ataxia, hydrocephalus |

**Management principles:** MRI with contrast; **dexamethasone for symptomatic vasogenic oedema** (with PPI and glucose monitoring); antiseizure medication if seizures have occurred (not prophylactically); tissue diagnosis; then surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy (**temozolomide with radiotherapy for glioblastoma**, with **MGMT promoter methylation** predicting benefit ⚑). Discuss driving restrictions. Early palliative care involvement for high-grade disease.

#### 11.2 Pituitary tumours and apoplexy; other skull base lesions

**Pituitary adenoma:** presents with **bitemporal hemianopia** (chiasmal compression from below, upper quadrants first), headache, hormonal excess (**prolactinoma** — galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, low libido; **acromegaly**; **Cushing's disease**) or hypopituitarism.

- **Prolactinoma is treated medically first** — **cabergoline** or bromocriptine — not surgically, which is a favourite exam point.
- Non-functioning macroadenomas and GH/ACTH-secreting tumours: **trans-sphenoidal surgery**.
- Always check **visual fields** and a full pituitary hormone profile.

**Pituitary apoplexy:** haemorrhage or infarction into an adenoma → **sudden severe headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia (III, IV, VI in the cavernous sinus), reduced consciousness and acute hypopituitarism**. **Give hydrocortisone immediately**, urgent MRI, endocrine and neurosurgical input. Precipitants include pregnancy (Sheehan's is the postpartum pituitary infarction variant), anticoagulation, surgery, dynamic pituitary testing.

**Cavernous sinus syndrome:** CN III, IV, V1/V2 and VI plus sympathetic fibres → ophthalmoplegia, facial sensory loss and a **postganglionic Horner's**, often with proptosis and chemosis. Causes: cavernous sinus thrombosis (from facial/sinus infection), tumour, carotid–cavernous fistula, aneurysm.

#### 11.3 Metastatic cord compression and paraneoplastic disease

**Metastatic spinal cord compression** — see Section 5.5 for the emergency management. Remember the **pain precedes the neurology by weeks**: progressive thoracic back pain, worse lying flat and on coughing, in someone with a cancer history is an indication for **MRI whole spine within 24 hours**, not analgesia and review.

**Paraneoplastic neurological syndromes** — see the antibody table in Section 8.10. The practical rules: (1) the neurological syndrome usually **precedes the cancer diagnosis**; (2) subacute onset in a smoker or someone losing weight should trigger antibody testing and CT chest/abdomen/pelvis ± **FDG-PET**; (3) treating the tumour is the most effective treatment; (4) classic syndromes are limbic encephalitis, cerebellar degeneration (anti-Yo, breast/ovary), LEMS (SCLC), sensory neuronopathy (anti-Hu), opsoclonus–myoclonus and stiff person syndrome.

#### 11.4 Neurocutaneous syndromes

**The short version**

- All AD except **Sturge–Weber** (sporadic *GNAQ* mosaic). Chromosomes: **NF1 = 17**, **NF2 = 22**, TSC1 9q / TSC2 16p, VHL 3p.
- Skin sign identifies the syndrome: café-au-lait + axillary freckling + **Lisch nodules** = NF1; **ash-leaf macules, shagreen patch, angiofibromas** = tuberous sclerosis; **port-wine stain in V1** = Sturge–Weber.
- Tumour sign identifies the rest: **bilateral vestibular schwannomas** = NF2; **cerebellar/retinal haemangioblastoma + RCC + phaeochromocytoma** = von Hippel–Lindau.
- Epilepsy is the shared complication — **infantile spasms** in tuberous sclerosis, seizures with **tram-track gyriform calcification** in Sturge–Weber.

| Syndrome | Inheritance/gene | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Neurofibromatosis type 1** | AD, *NF1* (chromosome **17** — "von Recklinghausen, 17 letters") | **≥6 café-au-lait macules**, axillary/inguinal freckling, **≥2 neurofibromas or 1 plexiform**, **Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas)**, **optic pathway glioma**, bony dysplasia, first-degree relative; complications: learning difficulty, phaeochromocytoma, hypertension, scoliosis, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour |
| **Neurofibromatosis type 2** | AD, *NF2* (chromosome **22**) | **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas**, meningiomas, ependymomas, juvenile cataract; far fewer skin signs |
| **Tuberous sclerosis** | AD, *TSC1* (hamartin, 9q) / *TSC2* (tuberin, 16p) | **Ash-leaf macules (Wood's lamp), shagreen patch, facial angiofibromas, periungual fibromas**<br>• cortical tubers and subependymal nodules<br>• **infantile spasms**, epilepsy, learning difficulty<br>• **renal angiomyolipoma**, cardiac rhabdomyoma, lymphangioleiomyomatosis<br>• mTOR inhibitors (everolimus) are used ⚑ |
| **von Hippel–Lindau** | AD, *VHL* (3p) | **Cerebellar and retinal haemangioblastomas**, **renal cell carcinoma**, phaeochromocytoma, pancreatic and renal cysts |
| **Sturge–Weber** | Sporadic, *GNAQ* mosaic | **Port-wine stain in the V1 distribution**, leptomeningeal angioma, **"tram-track" gyriform calcification**, epilepsy, hemiparesis, glaucoma, learning difficulty |

#### 11.5 Sleep disorders

**The short version**

- **OSA** — snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence (Epworth), obesity, hypertension, AF; polysomnography, then weight loss and **CPAP** plus driving advice.
- **Narcolepsy type 1** — excessive daytime sleepiness + **cataplexy** (pathognomonic), sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations; loss of **orexin/hypocretin**; modafinil/solriamfetol/pitolisant ⚑, sodium oxybate for cataplexy.
- **REM sleep behaviour disorder** — acting out dreams is a **strong prodrome of synucleinopathy** (PD, DLB, MSA); melatonin, clonazepam, safety measures.
- **Restless legs** — check **ferritin (aim >75 µg/L)**; ⚑ **α2δ ligands first-line over dopamine agonists** because of **augmentation**.

| Disorder | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Obstructive sleep apnoea** | Snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence (Epworth score), obesity, hypertension, AF; diagnose with polysomnography/oximetry | Weight loss, **CPAP**, mandibular advancement; driving advice |
| **Narcolepsy** | **Excessive daytime sleepiness** plus **cataplexy** (sudden loss of tone triggered by emotion — pathognomonic of type 1), **sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations**; loss of hypothalamic **orexin/hypocretin** neurones, HLA-DQB1*06:02 | Diagnose with multiple sleep latency test ± CSF hypocretin; **modafinil/solriamfetol/pitolisant** for sleepiness ⚑, sodium oxybate for cataplexy and disturbed nights, scheduled naps |
| **REM sleep behaviour disorder** | Acting out dreams, injuries to self or partner; loss of REM atonia | **A strong prodrome of synucleinopathy** (PD, DLB, MSA — most convert within 10–15 years); safety measures, melatonin, clonazepam |
| **Restless legs syndrome** | Urge to move the legs, worse at rest and in the evening, relieved by movement; check **ferritin (aim >75 µg/L)**, renal function, pregnancy, drugs | Iron replacement, **α2δ ligands (gabapentin/pregabalin) preferred first-line over dopamine agonists** ⚑ because of **augmentation** |
| Parasomnias (non-REM) | Sleepwalking, night terrors, confusional arousals in slow-wave sleep, usually children | Safety, sleep hygiene |
| Circadian disorders | Shift work, delayed sleep phase | Light therapy, melatonin timing |

#### 11.6 Functional neurological disorder

**FND is a positive diagnosis based on internal inconsistency and incongruence, not a diagnosis of exclusion** ⚑ — this is a genuine change from older teaching, and it matters because "no organic cause found" both fails patients and gets marks deducted.

**Positive signs:**

**The short version**

- Every sign in the table demonstrates the same thing — **internal inconsistency**: the movement is present in one context and absent in another.
- The two to be able to name and perform: **Hoover's sign** (weak hip extension normalising when the opposite hip flexes against resistance) and **tremor entrainment**.
- Gait and seizure phenotypes count as positive signs too: **dragging monoplegic gait** (hip rotated, not pyramidal) and **prolonged, waxing-and-waning events with closed eyes**.

| Sign | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Hoover's sign** | Weak hip **extension** that normalises when the opposite hip is flexed against resistance |
| Hip abductor sign | Similar principle in hip abduction |
| **Give-way / collapsing weakness** | Inconsistent effort rather than fixed weakness |
| **Tremor entrainment** | The tremor takes on the frequency of a voluntarily tapped rhythm, or stops |
| Dragging monoplegic gait | Leg dragged with the hip internally or externally rotated — not seen in pyramidal weakness |
| Functional seizure features | See Section 6.9 — prolonged, waxing and waning, closed eyes |
| Inconsistency over time | Signs vary with distraction and observation |

**Context:** symptoms are real and disabling, not feigned (unlike factitious disorder or malingering). Risk factors include prior physical injury or illness, migraine, chronic pain, fatigue, anxiety and depression, and adverse life events — but a psychological trigger is **not required** for the diagnosis.

**Management:** an explicit, positive explanation ("your nervous system is not working properly rather than being damaged — here is the sign that shows me that"), demonstration of the positive sign to the patient, written information, **physiotherapy with an FND-informed approach for motor symptoms**, **CBT for functional seizures**, treating comorbid depression, anxiety and pain, and avoiding repeated investigation, which entrenches illness beliefs.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators — Section 11

**The short version**

- Imaging descriptors carry most of the marks: **butterfly/crossing corpus callosum** = GBM, **dural tail** = meningioma, **periventricular + steroid-responsive** = CNS lymphoma.
- Three "do this first" answers: **hydrocortisone** in pituitary apoplexy, **cabergoline** (not surgery) for prolactinoma, **biopsy before steroids** in CNS lymphoma.
- ⚑ Three changed answers to hold: molecular glioma classification (IDH, 1p/19q), α2δ ligands first-line in restless legs, and **FND is a positive diagnosis, not one of exclusion**.

Two passes: tumours and pituitary first, then neurocutaneous, sleep and functional.

### Neurology (ANU) — Paediatric tumours — Neuro-oncology, Neurocutaneous, Sleep and Functional Disorders

**The short version**

- Histology is the discriminator here: **Rosenthal fibres** = pilocytic astrocytoma; **Homer-Wright rosettes + small blue cells** = medulloblastoma; **perivascular rosettes + blepharoblasts** = ependymoma; **squamous nesting** = craniopharyngioma.
- Posterior fossa siting explains the presentation — medulloblastoma and ependymoma compress the **4th ventricle → obstructive hydrocephalus**.
- **Craniopharyngioma** is the supratentorial outlier: Rathke pouch remnant, **calcification of the sella**, **bitemporal hemianopia**, short stature, high recurrence after resection.
- Prognosis splits the group: good for pilocytic astrocytoma and craniopharyngioma, **poor for ependymoma** and for medulloblastoma in children &lt;3 years (which also seeds "drop metastases").

| Pilocytic astrocytoma (Grade I- low grade) | Definition: derived from astrocytes (glial cell)<br>• Location: most commonly in children, suprotentorial in adults<br>• Features: Benign slow-growing tumour that usually arises in the posterior fossa of the cerebellum, forming single/multiple cysts<br>• Histology: biphasic (cell rich and cell poor cystic areas), Rosenthal fibres (eosinophilic, corkscrew fibres)<br>• Prognosis: good |
| --- | --- |
| Craniopharyngioma | Definition: derived from remnants of the Rathke pouch<br>• Features: most common supratentorial tumour that is generally benign with ↑ recurrence after resection; calcification of sella(tooth enamel-like); bitemporal hemianopia<br>• Presentation: short stature<br>• Histology: nesting of squamous epithelium bordered by radially arranged cells<br>• Prognosis: good |
| Medulloblastoma | Definition: originates from immature or embryonal cells at their earliest stage of development<br>• Features: highly malignant, compress 4th ventricle (→ hydrocephalus), seeds arachnoid space → “drop metastases” to spinal cord<br>• Histology:  Homer-Wright rosettes (differentiated tumour cells surrounding neuropil), small blue cells<br>• Prognosis: dependent on transcriptional profiling but poor prognosis for children &lt;3 years old<br>• Treatment: resection, radiation for mets |
| Ependymoma | Definition: arises from the ependymal, the thin epithelium-like lining of the ventricular system<br>• Features: commonly 4th ventricle, obstructive hydrocephalus (child better in fetal position)<br>• Histology: perivascular rosettes, blepharoblasts (basal ciliary bodies) near nucleus<br>• Prognosis: poor<br>• Treatment: resection |

### Neurology (ANU) — adult tumours — Neuro-oncology, Neurocutaneous, Sleep and Functional Disorders

**The short version**

- Histology one-liners again: **pleomorphic cells around necrosis/haemorrhage** = GBM; **whorls + psammoma bodies** = meningioma; **"fried egg" cells + chicken-wire capillaries** = oligodendroglioma; **thin-walled capillaries** = haemangioblastoma.
- **GBM** is the aggressive one — only tumour that crosses the corpus callosum ("butterfly glioma", ring-enhancing), ~1-year median survival.
- **Haemangioblastoma + retinal angiomas = von Hippel-Lindau**; **bilateral acoustic neuroma (schwannoma) = NF-2**.
- **Pituitary adenoma**: micro &lt;1 cm vs macro &gt;1 cm; mass effect = bitemporal hemianopia + headache; commonest is **prolactinoma** (infertility, amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea, ↓libido) — treat medically with a DA agonist, surgery is trans-sphenoidal. **Apoplexy** = sudden headache, visual disturbance, ↓mental status, panhypopituitarism.

#### seizures/epilepsy

**The short version**

- Definitions to quote: **seizure** = transient excessive/abnormal cortical neuronal activity; **epilepsy** = ≥2 unprovoked seizures; **status epilepticus** = ≥5 min without remission, or successive seizures without return to baseline.
- Aetiology screen is the same list every time — infection, neoplasia, **vascular (stroke is the commonest cause of late-onset seizures)**, metabolic (↓Na⁺, ↓glucose, ↓Ca²⁺, uraemia), trauma, drugs/withdrawal, neurodegeneration, psychogenic.
- Mechanism: **↑excitatory (glutamate, aspartate) and ↓inhibitory (GABA)** transmission; the semiology depends on where it starts and how far it spreads.
- Workup is history from a **witness** plus bloods, **ECG (exclude arrhythmia)**, CT acutely for haemorrhage/mass, MRI for aetiology, LP if fever/meningism, EEG (variable sensitivity).

##### Anticonvulsants

**The short version**

- Almost all of the first-tier drugs are **Na⁺-channel blockers**; valproate adds **↑cerebral GABA**, which is why it is the broad-spectrum agent.
- Uses: **phenytoin** for status epilepticus (IV), **sodium valproate** first-line in generalised epilepsy, **carbamazepine** for focal + generalised (but **may worsen generalised seizures**), **lamotrigine** broad-spectrum including JME.
- Side-effect flags that get examined: phenytoin **gum hypertrophy** and cerebellar degeneration; valproate **teratogenic (NTD)**, weight gain, thrombocytopenia; carbamazepine **Stevens-Johnson in Han Chinese** and agranulocytosis; lamotrigine **Stevens-Johnson**.

**Second-tier and add-on agents**

- Broad-spectrum alternatives: **topiramate** (also migraine prophylaxis) and **levetiracetam** (renally excreted, minimal interactions).
- **Clonazepam/clobazam** are short-term cover while titrating; **phenobarbitone** has a narrow therapeutic index and withdrawal seizures; **lacosamide** is a narrow-spectrum add-on.
