## Neuropharmacology and Key Medications


### Neurology Master Notes — Neuropharmacology and Key Medications

*Sources: BNF and Australian Medicines Handbook; NICE NG217 (epilepsy), NG128 (stroke), NG71 (Parkinson's), NG97 (dementia), NG220 (MS), CG173/NG193 (neuropathic pain); MHRA Drug Safety Updates; Rang & Dale *Pharmacology*; Merck Manual Professional; StatPearls. Scope: the consolidated drug section — mechanisms, the side effects that get asked about, interactions and monitoring. ⚑ = changed from older textbooks.*

---

##### 12.1 Antiseizure medications

| Drug | Mechanism | Key adverse effects | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sodium valproate** | Multiple — ↑GABA, Na⁺ channels, T-type Ca²⁺ | **Teratogenicity (neural tube, ~10% malformation, 30–40% neurodevelopmental)**, weight gain, tremor, hair loss, **hepatotoxicity**, pancreatitis, thrombocytopenia, **hyperammonaemic encephalopathy** | ⚑ Not to be started under 55 years unless two specialists agree; Pregnancy Prevention Programme; **enzyme inhibitor** — raises lamotrigine levels (halve the lamotrigine dose) |
| **Lamotrigine** | Na⁺ channel blockade | **Rash — Stevens–Johnson syndrome (titrate slowly)**, headache, insomnia, can worsen myoclonus | Levels **fall in pregnancy** (increase the dose, reduce postpartum); valproate doubles the level; oestrogen-containing contraception lowers it |
| **Levetiracetam** | SV2A binding | **Behavioural change — irritability, aggression, depression** (the main limiting effect); somnolence | No significant interactions; renally cleared; easy IV/oral switch |
| **Carbamazepine** | Na⁺ channel blockade | **Hyponatraemia (SIADH)**, rash, **HLA-B\*1502 → SJS in Han Chinese/Southeast Asian ancestry**, agranulocytosis, ataxia, diplopia; **worsens myoclonic and absence seizures** | **Potent enzyme inducer** — reduces the efficacy of the combined pill, DOACs, warfarin, and auto-induces its own metabolism |
| **Phenytoin** | Na⁺ channel blockade | **Zero-order (saturable) kinetics — small dose changes cause toxicity**; nystagmus → ataxia → confusion with rising levels; **gum hypertrophy, hirsutism, coarse facies**, cerebellar atrophy, megaloblastic anaemia (folate), osteomalacia, purple glove syndrome | Enzyme inducer; needs level monitoring (adjust for albumin); IV must be given slowly with cardiac monitoring |
| **Topiramate** | Multiple | **Weight loss, paraesthesia, word-finding difficulty ("dopamax"), renal stones, acute angle-closure glaucoma, metabolic acidosis, oligohidrosis**; teratogenic ⚑ | Pregnancy Prevention Programme required ⚑; reduces oral contraceptive efficacy >200 mg/day |
| **Ethosuximide** | T-type Ca²⁺ channels | GI upset, drowsiness, rarely SJS/blood dyscrasias | First-line for **absence** seizures only |
| **Sodium-channel add-ons** (lacosamide, oxcarbazepine, eslicarbazepine) | Slow Na⁺ inactivation | PR prolongation (lacosamide), hyponatraemia (oxcarbazepine) | Useful in focal epilepsy |
| **Gabapentin / pregabalin** | α2δ subunit of Ca²⁺ channels | Sedation, weight gain, oedema, **misuse potential (Class C controlled drugs in the UK)** ⚑, respiratory depression with opioids | Mainly used for neuropathic pain; **worsen myoclonus and absences** |
| **Perampanel, brivaracetam, cenobamate, zonisamide, clobazam, vigabatrin, rufinamide, cannabidiol, stiripentol, fenfluramine** | Various | Vigabatrin — **irreversible visual field constriction** (reserved for infantile spasms); perampanel — psychiatric effects | Specialist add-ons |

**Class effects to remember:** all can cause sedation, dizziness and rash; **enzyme inducers (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone, topiramate at higher doses) reduce contraceptive efficacy and lower vitamin D**; long-term use warrants attention to bone health; abrupt withdrawal precipitates seizures.

##### 12.2 Stroke and antithrombotic pharmacology

| Drug/class | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Alteplase / tenecteplase** | Recombinant tissue plasminogen activators — convert plasminogen to plasmin | Tenecteplase is fibrin-specific with a longer half-life → **single bolus** ⚑; risks: intracranial haemorrhage, angio-oedema (worse with ACE inhibitors) |
| **Aspirin** | Irreversible COX-1 inhibition → ↓thromboxane A₂ | 300 mg acutely for 2 weeks, then long-term antithrombotic |
| **Clopidogrel** | P2Y12 receptor antagonist (prodrug — activated by CYP2C19) | **First-line long-term after ischaemic stroke/TIA**; reduced effect in CYP2C19 poor metabolisers; PPIs (omeprazole) may reduce activation |
| **Dipyridamole** | Phosphodiesterase inhibitor | With aspirin if clopidogrel is not tolerated; headache is common |
| **DOACs** (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran) | Factor Xa inhibitors; dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor | Preferred over warfarin in **non-valvular AF**; renally dosed; reversal — **idarucizumab** (dabigatran), **andexanet alfa** (Xa inhibitors) |
| **Warfarin** | Vitamin K epoxide reductase inhibition (factors II, VII, IX, X, protein C/S) | For mechanical valves and moderate–severe mitral stenosis; INR monitoring; huge interaction list |
| **Statins** | HMG-CoA reductase inhibition | High-intensity after ischaemic stroke; myalgia/myopathy, deranged LFTs; **avoid routinely after intracerebral haemorrhage unless the indication is strong** |
| **Nimodipine** | Dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker with cerebral selectivity | **Oral 60 mg 4-hourly for 21 days after aneurysmal SAH** — reduces delayed cerebral ischaemia |

##### 12.3 Headache pharmacology

| Class | Examples | Mechanism | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Triptans** | Sumatriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan | **5-HT₁B/₁D agonists** — cranial vasoconstriction and inhibition of CGRP release | **Contraindicated in ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, previous stroke, hemiplegic/basilar migraine**; ≤10 days/month to avoid overuse headache |
| **Gepants** ⚑ | Rimegepant, atogepant, ubrogepant | **CGRP receptor antagonists** | No vasoconstriction → usable in cardiovascular disease; rimegepant and atogepant also used for prevention |
| **Ditans** ⚑ | Lasmiditan | 5-HT₁F agonist | Sedation, driving restriction after dosing |
| **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** ⚑ | Erenumab (receptor); fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab (ligand) | Block CGRP signalling | Monthly/quarterly injections; NICE after ≥3 failed preventives; constipation and hypertension with erenumab |
| **Preventives** | Propranolol, amitriptyline, topiramate, candesartan | Various | Propranolol — avoid in asthma; amitriptyline — anticholinergic, sedating; topiramate — teratogenic |
| Botulinum toxin A | Presynaptic SNARE cleavage | Licensed for **chronic migraine** (≥15 headache days/month) |
| Cluster-specific | High-flow oxygen, subcutaneous sumatriptan, **verapamil** (preventive, with ECG monitoring), short steroid course, greater occipital nerve block | | |
| Antiemetics | Metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, domperidone | D2 antagonists — also improve absorption | **Metoclopramide/prochlorperazine cause acute dystonic reactions** and are contraindicated in Parkinson's disease |

##### 12.4 Parkinson's disease pharmacology

**Principle:** dopaminergic drugs treat motor symptoms; they do not slow the disease. **Never stop them abruptly** — the risk is an akinetic crisis or neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome (a common inpatient error when patients are nil by mouth; use a patch or nasogastric route).

| Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Levodopa + peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor** | Co-careldopa, co-beneldopa | **Most effective for motor symptoms**, best for function and quality of life; **first-line for most patients whose motor symptoms affect quality of life** (NICE NG71). Adverse effects: nausea, postural hypotension, hallucinations, and — long-term — **motor fluctuations ("wearing off", "on–off") and dyskinesias**, which occur earlier in younger patients |
| **Dopamine agonists** | Ropinirole, pramipexole, rotigotine patch, apomorphine (rescue/infusion) | Fewer dyskinesias but **more impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality, binge eating — must be asked about at every review)**, hallucinations, somnolence with sudden-onset sleep (driving advice), and oedema. Ergot-derived agonists (cabergoline, pergolide) risk fibrotic reactions — largely abandoned ⚑ |
| **MAO-B inhibitors** | Selegiline, rasagiline, safinamide | Mild benefit; useful early or as adjunct; interaction risk with SSRIs/SNRIs (serotonin syndrome) |
| **COMT inhibitors** | Entacapone, opicapone, tolcapone | Extend the levodopa "on" time; harmless orange urine; tolcapone is hepatotoxic |
| **Amantadine** | NMDA antagonist + dopaminergic | Mainly for **levodopa-induced dyskinesia**; livedo reticularis, confusion, ankle oedema |
| **Anticholinergics** | Trihexyphenidyl, procyclidine | Only for tremor in younger patients; **avoid in the elderly (confusion)**; useful for acute drug-induced dystonia |
| **Device-assisted therapy** ⚑ | Apomorphine infusion, **levodopa–carbidopa intestinal gel**, **subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa**, **deep brain stimulation (subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus interna)** | For refractory fluctuations; DBS requires good levodopa responsiveness and no significant dementia |

**Non-motor prescribing:** orthostatic hypotension — fludrocortisone or midodrine; psychosis — **quetiapine or clozapine only** (never haloperidol/risperidone); dementia — **rivastigmine**; drooling — glycopyrronium; REM sleep behaviour disorder — melatonin or clonazepam; constipation — laxatives; depression — SSRIs with awareness of the MAO-B interaction.

**Drugs that cause or worsen parkinsonism:** typical and atypical antipsychotics, **metoclopramide and prochlorperazine**, sodium valproate, lithium, tetrabenazine, calcium channel blockers (flunarizine, cinnarizine).

##### 12.5 MS disease-modifying therapies

(Full table in Section 8.2.) **The examinable pattern is drug → signature risk:**

| Drug | Signature risk |
|---|---|
| **Natalizumab** | **Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy** (JC virus antibody status stratifies risk) |
| **Fingolimod / S1P modulators** | **First-dose bradycardia**, macular oedema, VZV reactivation, **rebound on withdrawal** |
| **Alemtuzumab** | **Secondary autoimmunity** — thyroid, ITP, anti-GBM disease; 5 years of monitoring |
| **Dimethyl fumarate** | Flushing, lymphopenia (→ PML) |
| **Teriflunomide** | Teratogenicity, hepatotoxicity — needs cholestyramine washout |
| **Anti-CD20 (ocrelizumab, ofatumumab)** | Infusion reactions, hypogammaglobulinaemia, infections; **the only class licensed for primary progressive MS** |
| **Cladribine** | Lymphopenia, zoster |
| **Interferon beta / glatiramer** | Flu-like symptoms, injection-site reactions; safest in pregnancy |

**Relapse:** methylprednisolone 500 mg oral for 5 days (or IV 1 g for 3 days). **Symptomatic:** baclofen/tizanidine/nabiximols for spasticity, fampridine for walking, gabapentinoids or duloxetine for pain, anticholinergics or intermittent self-catheterisation for bladder.

##### 12.6 Dementia pharmacology

| Drug | Mechanism | Indication and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine** | Acetylcholinesterase inhibition | Mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's; also **DLB and Parkinson's disease dementia** (rivastigmine has the best evidence). Adverse effects: **bradycardia and syncope (check pulse/ECG), GI upset, vivid dreams, urinary symptoms**; caution with asthma/COPD and peptic ulcer |
| **Memantine** | NMDA receptor antagonist | Moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's, or intolerance of cholinesterase inhibitors; better tolerated |
| **Lecanemab, donanemab** ⚑ | Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies | Early symptomatic Alzheimer's with confirmed amyloid; **ARIA (oedema and haemorrhage) requiring serial MRI**; higher risk in **ApoE ε4 homozygotes**. MHRA licensed but **NICE found them not cost-effective for the NHS**; **TGA registered lecanemab in September 2025 for ApoE ε4 non-carriers and heterozygotes only** |
| Antipsychotics | D2 blockade | Only for severe distress or risk; increase stroke and mortality; **contraindicated in DLB** |

**Not indicated:** cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine in **frontotemporal dementia** or pure vascular dementia. Review and reduce **anticholinergic burden** (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, older antihistamines) in every patient with cognitive impairment.

##### 12.7 Neuromuscular pharmacology

| Drug | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pyridostigmine** | Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (does not cross the BBB) | Symptomatic in MG; muscarinic effects — cramps, diarrhoea, salivation (can be blunted with propantheline); **overdose → cholinergic crisis** |
| **Prednisolone** | Immunosuppression | Backbone of MG treatment; **start low and titrate — rapid initiation can transiently worsen weakness** |
| **Azathioprine** | Purine synthesis inhibition | **Check TPMT before starting**; monitor FBC/LFT; **never with allopurinol** (fatal marrow suppression) |
| Mycophenolate, methotrexate, ciclosporin, tacrolimus | Various | Steroid-sparing alternatives |
| **Rituximab** | Anti-CD20 | Especially effective in **MuSK-positive** MG |
| **Complement inhibitors** ⚑ | Eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan | AChR-positive refractory generalised MG; **meningococcal vaccination mandatory** |
| **FcRn blockers** ⚑ | Efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab | Accelerate IgG degradation → rapid, cyclical benefit in generalised MG |
| **IVIg** | Immunomodulation | GBS, CIDP, MG crisis; risks — thrombosis, renal impairment, aseptic meningitis, **check IgA to avoid anaphylaxis in IgA deficiency** |
| **Plasma exchange** | Removes circulating antibody | Equivalent to IVIg in GBS and MG crisis; line and haemodynamic risks |
| **3,4-diaminopyridine (amifampridine)** | Blocks presynaptic K⁺ channels → prolonged Ca²⁺ entry | LEMS; can lower the seizure threshold |
| **Riluzole** | Glutamate release inhibition | MND — ~3 months' survival benefit; monitor LFTs |

##### 12.8 Neuropathic pain

**First-line (NICE NG193/CG173):** **amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin** — try each in turn as monotherapy; **carbamazepine is first-line specifically for trigeminal neuralgia**. **Tramadol only as rescue**, not long-term. **Capsaicin cream** for localised neuropathic pain. **Do not use conventional opioids, and do not use gabapentinoids for sciatica** ⚑.

Practical points: start low and titrate; explain a realistic goal (30–50% pain reduction, not abolition); gabapentinoids need renal dose adjustment and are controlled drugs in the UK because of misuse ⚑; duloxetine is preferred in diabetic neuropathy; amitriptyline helps sleep but is anticholinergic; address function, mood and sleep alongside the drug.

##### 12.9 Immunotherapy in neurology

| Modality | Where it is used | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **High-dose corticosteroids** | MS relapse, GCA, CIDP, myositis, autoimmune encephalitis, vasculitis, cord compression oedema | Hyperglycaemia, mood change and psychosis, insomnia, infection, osteoporosis (bone protection), gastric protection, adrenal suppression — **never stop long-term steroids abruptly** |
| **IVIg** | GBS, CIDP, MG, autoimmune encephalitis, dermatomyositis | Thrombosis, renal failure, aseptic meningitis, IgA deficiency |
| **Plasma exchange** | GBS, MG crisis, NMOSD relapse, severe steroid-refractory MS relapse | Hypotension, coagulopathy, line sepsis |
| **Rituximab and biologics** | NMOSD, MG, autoimmune encephalitis, vasculitis | Hepatitis B reactivation (screen), hypogammaglobulinaemia, infection |
| **Tocilizumab** | Giant cell arteritis (steroid-sparing) | Masks CRP; GI perforation risk |

##### 12.10 Drug-induced neurological disease — a checklist to run in every unexplained case

| Presentation | Culprits |
|---|---|
| **Parkinsonism** | Antipsychotics, **metoclopramide, prochlorperazine**, valproate, lithium, cinnarizine |
| **Acute dystonia / oculogyric crisis** | Metoclopramide, antipsychotics (treat with procyclidine) |
| **Tardive dyskinesia** | Long-term dopamine antagonists |
| **Tremor** | Salbutamol, lithium, valproate, SSRIs, caffeine, thyroxine |
| **Seizures (lowered threshold)** | Tramadol, bupropion, theophylline, quinolones, clozapine, TCAs, abrupt benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal |
| **Peripheral neuropathy** | **Isoniazid (give pyridoxine)**, metronidazole, nitrofurantoin, amiodarone, **vincristine, platinum agents, taxanes**, phenytoin, **excess pyridoxine**, dapsone, linezolid |
| **Myopathy/rhabdomyolysis** | Statins (worse with fibrates, macrolides, ciclosporin), steroids, colchicine, alcohol |
| **Worsening myasthenia** | Aminoglycosides, macrolides, quinolones, beta-blockers, magnesium, phenytoin, penicillamine |
| **Idiopathic intracranial hypertension** | Tetracyclines, retinoids/vitamin A, **steroid withdrawal**, nitrofurantoin |
| **Encephalopathy** | Valproate (hyperammonaemia), lithium, ciclosporin/tacrolimus (PRES), ifosfamide, cefepime |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, **tramadol**, linezolid, triptans in combination, St John's wort |
| **Optic neuropathy** | **Ethambutol**, amiodarone, isoniazid |
| **Ototoxicity/vestibulotoxicity** | Gentamicin, furosemide, cisplatin |

#### Part A — By presenting syndrome

##### A1. Sudden focal neurological deficit

| Additional feature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Leg weaker than arm, abulia, incontinence | ACA territory infarct |
| Face and arm weaker than leg + aphasia (dominant) or neglect (non-dominant) | MCA territory infarct |
| Isolated homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing | PCA (occipital) infarct |
| Dense equal face/arm/leg weakness with normal higher function | Lacunar infarct, internal capsule |
| Crossed signs — cranial nerve one side, limbs the other | Brainstem infarct |
| Sudden severe headache **then** the deficit | Haemorrhage — ICH or SAH |
| Deficit + neck pain + Horner's in a young adult | Carotid or vertebral dissection |
| Deficit that resolves fully in <1 hour, imaging normal | TIA (tissue-based definition) |
| Deficit after a seizure, resolving over hours | Todd's paresis |
| Deficit preceded by a slow visual/sensory march over 20–30 min | Migraine with aura |
| Weakness crossing arterial territories with a haemorrhagic infarct | Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis |
| "Man-in-a-barrel" after cardiac arrest | Watershed infarction |

##### A2. Headache

| Additional feature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Maximal within a minute, "worst ever" | SAH until excluded |
| Recurrent thunderclaps over days, postpartum or on serotonergic drugs | Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome |
| Over 50, scalp tenderness, **jaw claudication**, raised ESR | Giant cell arteritis |
| Unilateral, throbbing, photophobia + nausea, lies still in the dark | Migraine |
| Excruciating retro-orbital pain with lacrimation, **pacing**, nightly bouts | Cluster headache |
| Severe unilateral attacks abolished by indometacin | Paroxysmal hemicrania |
| Worse lying flat, morning, vomiting, papilloedema | Raised intracranial pressure |
| Worse standing, better lying flat | Low CSF pressure / leak |
| Obese young woman, pulsatile tinnitus, papilloedema, sixth nerve palsy | Idiopathic intracranial hypertension |
| ≥15 headache days/month with regular analgesic use | Medication-overuse headache |
| Headache + fever + neck stiffness ± rash | Meningitis |
| Headache + fever + confusion/behaviour change + seizure | Encephalitis |
| Headache + visual loss + ophthalmoplegia in a known pituitary mass | Pituitary apoplexy |

##### A3. Acute weakness

| Additional feature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Ascending, symmetrical, areflexic, over days, back pain | Guillain–Barré syndrome |
| Ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + areflexia | Miller Fisher variant |
| Fatigable, ocular/bulbar, pupils spared | Myasthenia gravis |
| Descending, bulbar-onset, **fixed dilated pupils**, dry mouth | Botulism |
| Proximal legs first, dry mouth, reflexes return after exercise | Lambert–Eaton (think small cell lung cancer) |
| Paraparesis with a sensory level and sphincter loss | Spinal cord compression — MRI now |
| Asymmetric flaccid legs, saddle anaesthesia, retention | Cauda equina syndrome |
| Hemiparesis, sudden | Stroke |
| Proximal symmetrical, raised CK, no sensory loss | Inflammatory myopathy |
| Wasting + fasciculations + brisk reflexes, no sensory loss | Motor neurone disease |
| Episodic weakness with abnormal potassium | Periodic paralysis |

##### A4. Altered consciousness / confusion

| Additional feature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Fluctuating attention over hours–days, worse at night | Delirium |
| Progressive over months–years, attention preserved early | Dementia |
| Pinpoint reactive pupils | Pontine haemorrhage or opioids |
| Unilateral fixed dilated pupil | Uncal herniation |
| Reactive pupils, roving eyes, asterixis | Metabolic/toxic coma |
| Confusion + ophthalmoplegia + ataxia | Wernicke's encephalopathy |
| Not waking >30 min after a convulsion | Non-convulsive status epilepticus |
| Fever + confusion + temporal changes on MRI | HSV encephalitis |
| Young woman, psychosis → seizures → orofacial dyskinesias | Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis |
| Aware but unable to move except vertical gaze and blinking | Locked-in syndrome |

##### A5. Gait and balance disturbance

| Gait/feature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Small shuffling steps, reduced arm swing, festination | Parkinsonism |
| Broad-based, falls towards the lesion, nystagmus | Cerebellar ataxia |
| Stamping, watches the feet, **Romberg positive** | Sensory (dorsal column) ataxia |
| Circumduction with flexed arm | Hemiplegic (stroke) |
| Scissoring, stiff, adducted | Spastic paraparesis (cord) |
| High-stepping | Foot drop |
| Waddling, Trendelenburg | Proximal myopathy |
| Magnetic, feet "stuck", with incontinence and cognitive decline | Normal pressure hydrocephalus |
| Inconsistent, improves with distraction | Functional gait disorder |

##### A6. Vertigo and dizziness

| Duration and trigger | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Seconds, on rolling over or looking up | BPPV — Dix–Hallpike, then Epley |
| Minutes–hours, episodic, with fluctuating hearing loss and aural fullness | Ménière's disease |
| Days, continuous, after a viral illness, hearing intact | Vestibular neuritis |
| Days, continuous, **with** hearing loss | Labyrinthitis (or AICA stroke) |
| Sudden, continuous, **cannot stand or walk**, normal head impulse test | Posterior circulation stroke |
| Recurrent with migraine features | Vestibular migraine |
| Chronic, progressive unilateral hearing loss and tinnitus | Vestibular schwannoma |

##### A7. Sensory disturbance

| Pattern | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Glove and stocking, symmetrical | Length-dependent polyneuropathy |
| Dermatomal with a lost reflex | Radiculopathy |
| Individual nerve territory | Mononeuropathy |
| Several nerves, asymmetric, painful | Mononeuritis multiplex — think vasculitis |
| Sensory level on the trunk | Cord lesion |
| Ipsilateral proprioception loss + contralateral pain/temperature loss | Brown-Séquard |
| "Cape" loss of pain and temperature only | Syringomyelia |
| Vibration/proprioception loss with extensor plantars | Subacute combined degeneration (B12) |
| Pain/temperature loss with preserved vibration, sudden and painful | Anterior spinal artery infarction |
| Face and body dissociated (crossed) | Lateral medullary syndrome |

##### A8. Transient episodes

| Feature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Positive phenomena, stereotyped, seconds, post-ictal confusion, lateral tongue bite | Seizure |
| Prodromal nausea/pallor/sweating, brief, rapid full recovery | Syncope |
| Slow visual march over 20–30 min, then headache | Migraine aura |
| Sudden negative deficit lasting minutes, vascular risk factors | TIA |
| Painless "curtain" over one eye | Amaurosis fugax |
| Prolonged, waxing and waning, eyes forcibly closed | Functional/dissociative seizure |
| Sudden loss of tone with laughter, consciousness preserved | Cataplexy (narcolepsy) |
| Isolated dense amnesia for hours with repetitive questioning | Transient global amnesia |

#### Part B — Signature lookup tables

##### B1. Pathognomonic and near-pathognomonic clues

| Clue | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Dawson's fingers on FLAIR | Multiple sclerosis |
| Bilateral internuclear ophthalmoplegia in a young adult | Multiple sclerosis |
| Longitudinally extensive myelitis + intractable hiccups | NMOSD (AQP4) |

##### B3. CSF patterns

| Pattern | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| >1000 neutrophils, protein >1 g/L, glucose ratio <0.4 | Bacterial meningitis |
| Lymphocytes, normal glucose, normal/mildly raised protein | Viral meningitis |
| Lymphocytes, **very high** protein, **low** glucose, subacute | TB (or fungal) meningitis |
| High protein, normal cell count | Albuminocytological dissociation — GBS/CIDP |
| Unmatched oligoclonal bands | Multiple sclerosis |
| Xanthochromia, constant red cells across bottles | Subarachnoid haemorrhage |
| Red cells falling across bottles, no xanthochromia | Traumatic tap |
| Very high opening pressure with cryptococcal antigen | Cryptococcal meningitis |
| Normal constituents with opening pressure >25 cmH₂O | Idiopathic intracranial hypertension |
| 14-3-3 / RT-QuIC positive | Prion disease |

##### B6. Localising signs at a glance

| Sign | Localisation |
|---|---|
| Forehead spared in facial weakness | UMN (contralateral cortex/capsule) |
| Whole hemiface weak | LMN (CN VII) |
| Tongue deviates towards the weak side | Ipsilateral CN XII |
| Uvula deviates away from the lesion | Ipsilateral CN X |
| Jaw deviates towards the weak side | Ipsilateral CN V motor |
| Nystagmus fast phase towards the lesion | Cerebellar hemisphere |
| Eyes deviate towards the lesion | Cortical (frontal eye field) — away in seizure |
| Failure of adduction + abducting nystagmus | MLF (internuclear ophthalmoplegia) |
| Bitemporal hemianopia | Optic chiasm |
| Superior quadrantanopia | Temporal (Meyer's loop) |
| Inferior quadrantanopia | Parietal radiation |

#### Part C — Confusable pairs, reduced to one separating feature

| Pair | The one thing that separates them |
|---|---|
| **Ischaemic vs haemorrhagic stroke** | Imaging only — clinically indistinguishable (headache and vomiting *hint* at haemorrhage) |
| **Stroke vs Bell's palsy** | **Forehead**: spared = stroke, involved = Bell's |
| **Seizure vs syncope** | Lateral tongue bite and prolonged post-ictal confusion = seizure |
| **Epileptic vs functional seizure** | Eyes forcibly closed, waxing–waning, >5 min = functional |
| **Migraine aura vs TIA** | Aura marches over minutes with **positive** phenomena; TIA is instant and **negative** |
| **Migraine vs cluster** | Migraine lies still; cluster paces |
| **Cluster vs paroxysmal hemicrania** | Indometacin abolishes paroxysmal hemicrania |
| **SAH vs traumatic tap** | Xanthochromia and constant red-cell count = SAH |
| **Extradural vs subdural** | Biconvex, does not cross sutures, lucid interval = extradural |
| **Delirium vs dementia** | Attention: impaired and fluctuating = delirium |
| **DLB vs Parkinson's disease dementia** | The 1-year rule |
| **Parkinson's vs essential tremor** | Rest and asymmetric with bradykinesia = Parkinson's; postural, symmetric, alcohol-responsive = essential |
| **Parkinson's vs PSP** | Early falls **backwards** and down-gaze palsy = PSP |
| **Parkinson's vs MSA** | Early severe autonomic failure = MSA |
| **Parkinson's vs vascular parkinsonism** | Lower-body predominance with normal arms = vascular |
| **Parkinson's vs drug-induced** | Symmetry and a drug history = drug-induced |
| **MS vs NMOSD** | Longitudinally extensive myelitis and severe bilateral optic neuritis = NMOSD (check AQP4) |
| **Optic neuritis vs GCA-related visual loss** | Age, pain on eye movement and recovery = optic neuritis; painless sudden loss over 50 with jaw claudication = GCA |
| **Myasthenia vs Lambert–Eaton** | Fatigues with use = MG; briefly improves with use, hyporeflexic, dry mouth = LEMS |
| **Myasthenia vs botulism** | Pupils: spared in MG, **dilated** in botulism |
| **Myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis** | Miosis, salivation, bradycardia, fasciculations = cholinergic |
| **GBS vs CIDP** | Nadir ≤4 weeks = GBS; progression >8 weeks = CIDP (and CIDP responds to steroids) |
| **GBS vs transverse myelitis** | Sensory level and extensor plantars = cord; areflexia throughout = GBS |
| **Common peroneal palsy vs L5 radiculopathy** | Inversion and hip abduction weak = L5 root |
| **Ulnar lesion at the elbow vs the wrist** | Dorsal hand sensory loss = elbow |
| **Conus medullaris vs cauda equina** | Symmetrical with early sphincter loss = conus |
| **Cord compression vs cord infarction** | Sudden and painful with spared vibration = anterior spinal artery infarction |
| **Central cord vs anterior cord syndrome** | Arms worse than legs = central cord |
| **Myopathy vs neuropathy** | Proximal + no sensory loss + raised CK = myopathy |
| **Polymyositis vs inclusion body myositis** | Asymmetric finger flexor/quadriceps weakness, steroid-resistant, over 50 = IBM |
| **Peripheral vs central vertigo** | HINTS: a **normal** head impulse test with new vertigo = central |
| **BPPV vs vestibular neuritis** | Seconds and positional = BPPV; days and continuous = neuritis |
| **Meningitis vs encephalitis** | Altered brain function (behaviour, cognition, seizures) = encephalitis |
| **Bacterial vs TB meningitis** | Neutrophils and hours = bacterial; lymphocytes, very high protein and weeks = TB |
| **Toxoplasmosis vs CNS lymphoma in HIV** | Multiple basal ganglia lesions responding to anti-toxoplasma therapy = toxoplasmosis; single periventricular lesion with EBV in CSF = lymphoma |
| **NMS vs serotonin syndrome** | Lead-pipe rigidity and days = NMS; clonus, hyperreflexia and hours = serotonin syndrome |
| **Raised vs low CSF pressure headache** | Worse lying flat = raised; worse standing = low |
| **IIH vs venous sinus thrombosis** | Venography — IIH requires it to be normal |
| **Absence seizure vs focal impaired-awareness seizure** | Absence: no aura, no post-ictal confusion, seconds, 3 Hz spike-and-wave |
| **Alzheimer's vs frontotemporal dementia** | Memory first vs behaviour/language first, and age at onset |
| **Alzheimer's vs NPH** | Gait disturbance **before** cognitive decline = NPH |
| **MND vs cervical myelopathy** | Sensory signs and a sensory level = myelopathy (and MND spares the sphincters) |
| **MND vs multifocal motor neuropathy** | Conduction block and anti-GM1, no UMN signs = MMN (treatable) |
