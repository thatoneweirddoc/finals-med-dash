## Headache and Facial Pain


### Neurology Master Notes — Headache and Facial Pain

#### 7.1 Approach and red flags

Most headache is primary (migraine, tension-type, cluster). The job is to spot the small number that are secondary.

**SNOOP4 red flags:** **S**ystemic symptoms or secondary risk factors (fever, weight loss, cancer, HIV/immunosuppression, pregnancy); **N**eurological signs or reduced consciousness; **O**nset sudden (thunderclap); **O**lder — new headache after **50** (giant cell arteritis, tumour); **P**apilloedema; **P**ositional (worse lying flat = raised ICP; worse upright = low pressure); **P**recipitated by Valsalva/exertion; **P**rogressive or a change in an established pattern.

#### 7.2 Migraine

**Diagnosis (ICHD-3):** ≥5 attacks lasting 4–72 hours, with **≥2 of** unilateral, pulsating, moderate-severe, aggravated by routine activity, **and ≥1 of** nausea/vomiting or photophobia **and** phonophobia.

**Aura** (in ~25%): fully reversible, develops over **≥5 minutes**, lasts **5–60 minutes**, usually visual (**fortification spectra, scintillating scotoma spreading across the field**), sometimes sensory (marching paraesthesia), rarely dysphasic. **Aura that lasts >60 minutes, is purely negative, or is always on the same side warrants imaging.**

**Phases:** prodrome (yawning, mood change, food craving, hours to days), aura, headache, postdrome.

**Triggers:** stress and stress-relief, sleep change, missed meals, dehydration, alcohol (especially red wine), hormonal (menstrual migraine), specific foods, weather. A headache diary is diagnostic and therapeutic.

**Acute treatment:**
- **Simple analgesia**: aspirin 900 mg or ibuprofen/naproxen, taken **early and at full dose**; add an antiemetic (metoclopramide or prochlorperazine — also helps gastric stasis and absorption).
- **Triptans** (e.g. sumatriptan 50–100 mg oral, or subcutaneous/nasal if vomiting) — 5-HT₁B/₁D agonists. **Contraindicated in ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, previous stroke, and in hemiplegic/basilar migraine.**
- ⚑ **Gepants** (small-molecule CGRP receptor antagonists — rimegepant, ubrogepant) and the ditan **lasmiditan** (5-HT₁F agonist) are alternatives where triptans fail or are contraindicated; they lack vasoconstrictor effects. Rimegepant is licensed both for acute treatment and prevention.
- **Avoid opioids and codeine-containing analgesics** — they promote medication-overuse headache.

**Prevention** (offer if ≥4 debilitating days/month, or acute treatment is failing):
- **Propranolol** (avoid in asthma) — good evidence and first-line for many.
- **Amitriptyline** (helpful with poor sleep or coexisting tension-type headache).
- **Topiramate** — effective but teratogenic: ⚑ Pregnancy Prevention Programme required, and it reduces oral contraceptive efficacy at higher doses.
- **Candesartan** as an alternative.
- ⚑ **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** — erenumab (receptor), fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab (ligand) — monthly/quarterly injections; NICE approves after **3 previous preventives have failed**. **Atogepant/rimegepant** (oral gepants) are also now approved preventives ⚑.
- **Botulinum toxin A** for **chronic migraine** (≥15 headache days/month) after 3 preventives have failed.
- Menstrual migraine: perimenstrual naproxen or frovatriptan/zolmitriptan.
- Non-drug: regular sleep, meals and exercise; riboflavin 400 mg; magnesium; treat medication overuse first.

**Migraine and the combined oral contraceptive:** ⚑ **migraine with aura is an absolute contraindication to combined hormonal contraception** (UKMEC 4) — the stroke risk is multiplicative. Migraine without aura is UKMEC 2–3.

#### 7.3 Tension-type and medication-overuse headache

**Tension-type headache:** bilateral, pressing/tightening ("band around the head"), mild-moderate, not aggravated by activity, no vomiting; may have photophobia *or* phonophobia but not both. Manage with simple analgesia used sparingly, stress and posture management, amitriptyline if chronic.

**Medication-overuse headache:** headache on **≥15 days/month** in someone using acute treatment regularly for >3 months — **≥15 days/month** for simple analgesics, **≥10 days/month** for triptans, opioids, ergots or combination analgesics. Treatment is **withdrawal of the overused drug** (abrupt for simple analgesics and triptans; opioids need tapering), with warning that headache worsens for 1–2 weeks first, plus a preventive.

#### 7.5 Thunderclap headache

Maximal intensity within **1 minute** (usually seconds). Assume **subarachnoid haemorrhage** until excluded (see Section 4.7): immediate non-contrast CT, LP at ≥12 hours if CT is negative.

**Differentials:** SAH, **reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome** (recurrent thunderclaps over days, often postpartum or with serotonergic/vasoactive drugs; "sausage-string" vessels on angiography; treat with nimodipine and remove triggers), cervical artery dissection, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, **pituitary apoplexy** (headache + visual loss + ophthalmoplegia + hypopituitarism — needs hydrocortisone and urgent MRI), hypertensive emergency/PRES, spontaneous intracranial hypotension, third ventricular colloid cyst, primary cough/exertional/sexual headache (a diagnosis of exclusion).

#### 7.6 Giant cell arteritis

**Suspect in anyone over 50** with new headache. Features: temporal headache and scalp tenderness (pain combing hair), **jaw claudication (the most specific symptom)**, visual symptoms (amaurosis fugax, diplopia, sudden painless visual loss from anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy), thickened non-pulsatile temporal artery, systemic upset, and polymyalgia rheumatica in ~40–50%.

**Investigations:** **ESR and CRP** (usually markedly raised; a normal ESR does not exclude it), **temporal artery ultrasound** looking for the **halo sign** ⚑ (now a first-line test in many centres and recommended in the BSR 2020 GCA guideline), **temporal artery biopsy** (skip lesions mean a negative biopsy does not exclude; remains positive for ~2 weeks after starting steroids), consider large-vessel imaging (PET-CT/MRA) for extracranial involvement.

**Treatment — do not wait for tests:**
- **No visual symptoms: prednisolone 40–60 mg daily immediately.**
- **Visual symptoms or jaw claudication: IV methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg daily for up to 3 days**, then high-dose oral, and same-day ophthalmology review.
- **Tocilizumab** ⚑ as a steroid-sparing agent in relapsing or steroid-intolerant disease (NICE-approved).
- Add bone protection, PPI, and aspirin where not contraindicated; taper steroids slowly over 12–24 months.

**The point of urgency:** visual loss in GCA is usually irreversible, and the fellow eye is at risk within days.

#### 7.7 Idiopathic intracranial hypertension

**Typical patient:** a young woman with obesity, or recent weight gain.

**Features:** daily headache worse lying flat and on waking, **pulsatile tinnitus**, transient visual obscurations, **papilloedema**, **sixth nerve palsy** (false localising), enlarging blind spot and constricted fields; **visual acuity is preserved until late** — fields, not acuity, are what to monitor.

**Drug causes to always ask about:** ⚑ **tetracyclines (doxycycline, minocycline), vitamin A and retinoids (isotretinoin), and — importantly — corticosteroid withdrawal**; also nitrofurantoin, lithium, levothyroxine in children, growth hormone.

**Diagnosis (modified Dandy/Friedman criteria):** papilloedema, normal neurological examination except cranial nerve palsies, **normal neuroimaging including MR venography (to exclude venous sinus thrombosis)**, **CSF opening pressure >25 cmH₂O with normal CSF constituents**.

**Management:** weight loss (the most effective long-term measure, including consideration of bariatric surgery ⚑ — the IIH:WT trial), **acetazolamide** (first-line drug; topiramate as an alternative with the bonus of weight loss), stop the causative drug, serial visual fields and OCT; for threatened vision — **optic nerve sheath fenestration** or **CSF shunting**, with venous sinus stenting in selected cases with stenosis. Repeated LPs are a temporising measure only.

#### 7.8 Low-pressure (CSF leak) headache

**Orthostatic headache — worse within minutes of standing, relieved by lying down.** Causes: post-lumbar-puncture (highest with larger, cutting needles; use atraumatic pencil-point needles), spontaneous (connective tissue disorders, disc osteophyte tears), trauma or surgery.

MRI shows **diffuse smooth pachymeningeal enhancement, brain sagging, subdural collections**. Treatment: conservative (fluids, caffeine, lying flat, analgesia) for 24–48 hours, then **epidural blood patch** — highly effective for post-dural-puncture headache. Investigate for a spinal leak (CT/MR myelography) if spontaneous and persistent.

#### 7.9 Trigeminal neuralgia and other facial pain

**Trigeminal neuralgia:** paroxysms of **severe, electric-shock-like, unilateral facial pain lasting seconds**, in the **V2/V3 distribution**, triggered by light touch, chewing, cold wind or shaving; refractory periods between attacks; **no sensory loss on examination**.

**Cause:** usually neurovascular compression by a loop of the superior cerebellar artery at the root entry zone. **MRI is indicated** to exclude a secondary cause — **bilateral trigeminal neuralgia, onset under 40, sensory loss, or other cranial nerve signs suggest MS or a cerebellopontine angle tumour** and change the diagnosis.

**Treatment:** **carbamazepine is first-line** (monitor sodium — hyponatraemia — and FBC/LFTs; watch for the HLA-B*1502 association with SJS in patients of Han Chinese/Southeast Asian ancestry). Alternatives: oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin. Refractory cases: **microvascular decompression** (best long-term durability), stereotactic radiosurgery, percutaneous rhizotomy/balloon compression.

**Other facial pain:** post-herpetic neuralgia (after shingles; amitriptyline, gabapentinoids, topical lidocaine), persistent idiopathic facial pain, temporomandibular disorder, dental and sinus causes, **Ramsay Hunt** (see Section 10), and **cluster headache** — which is frequently misdiagnosed as dental or sinus disease.
