## Cerebrovascular Disease


### Neurology Master Notes — Cerebrovascular Disease

*Sources: AHA/ASA 2026 Guideline for the Early Management of Patients with Acute Ischemic Stroke; NICE NG128 (stroke and TIA); European Stroke Organisation guidelines; AHA/ASA spontaneous ICH guideline (2022); AHA/ASA aneurysmal SAH guideline (2023); Kumar & Clark; Merck Manual Professional; StatPearls. Scope: the highest-yield acute section. ⚑ marks guidance that has moved since older textbooks.*

---

#### 4.1 Definitions and risk factors

**Stroke** — an acute focal neurological deficit of vascular origin lasting >24 hours or with imaging evidence of infarction/haemorrhage. **TIA** — transient focal neurological dysfunction of vascular origin **without infarction on imaging**; ⚑ the definition is now *tissue-based*, not the old "resolves within 24 hours" time-based one.

Roughly **85% ischaemic, 15% haemorrhagic** (of which about two-thirds intracerebral and one-third subarachnoid).

**Modifiable risk factors:** hypertension (the single biggest), atrial fibrillation, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidaemia, obesity, alcohol, obstructive sleep apnoea, physical inactivity, combined oral contraceptive with migraine with aura. **Non-modifiable:** age, male sex, family history, prior stroke/TIA, ethnicity (higher risk in South Asian, African and Caribbean populations).

#### 4.2 TIA

Symptoms usually last minutes (typically <1 hour). Distinguish from mimics: migraine aura (positive symptoms, slow march), focal seizure (positive symptoms, seconds, post-ictal), syncope (global, not focal), hypoglycaemia, functional.

**Management (NICE NG128):**

- Give **aspirin 300 mg immediately** unless contraindicated.
- **Specialist assessment within 24 hours** for anyone with a suspected TIA in the last 7 days. ⚑ NICE has **abandoned the ABCD² score** for deciding urgency — everyone gets seen within 24 hours.
- Do **not** do CT routinely; MRI (with diffusion-weighted imaging) is the test of choice, ideally on the day of assessment.
- Carotid imaging urgently if the patient is a candidate for carotid intervention.
- Start secondary prevention immediately (see 4.9). **Crescendo TIAs** (≥2 in a week) or TIA on anticoagulation → admit.
- Driving: in the UK, no driving for **1 month** after a TIA or stroke (Group 1 licence), longer and with notification for Group 2.

**Amaurosis fugax** — painless transient monocular visual loss, classically "curtain descending"; think ipsilateral carotid disease or cardiac embolus, and treat as a TIA.

#### 4.3 Ischaemic stroke — mechanisms and classification

**TOAST aetiologies:** large-artery atherosclerosis; cardioembolism (AF, endocarditis, recent MI, prosthetic valve, patent foramen ovale); small-vessel occlusion (lacunar); other determined (dissection, vasculitis, thrombophilia, sickle cell, CADASIL, moyamoya); undetermined (cryptogenic).

**Bamford / Oxford (OCSP) classification** — clinical, done at the bedside, predicts prognosis.

| Class | Criteria | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **TACS** — total anterior circulation | **All three**: (1) higher cortical dysfunction (aphasia/neglect), (2) homonymous hemianopia, (3) motor and/or sensory deficit of ≥2 of face/arm/leg | Worst prognosis; large MCA ± ACA |
| **PACS** — partial anterior circulation | **Two of the three**, or higher cortical dysfunction alone, or a limited motor/sensory deficit | Highest early recurrence risk |
| **LACS** — lacunar | **No** cortical signs, no visual field defect: pure motor, pure sensory, sensorimotor, or ataxic hemiparesis | Best prognosis; small perforator |
| **POCS** — posterior circulation | Cranial nerve palsy with contralateral deficit, bilateral deficits, disordered conjugate eye movement, cerebellar signs, **isolated homonymous hemianopia** | Easily missed |

#### 4.4 Vascular syndromes worth recognising instantly

| Presentation | Territory |
|---|---|
| Leg > arm weakness, abulia, incontinence | ACA |
| Face/arm > leg, aphasia or neglect, gaze towards lesion | MCA |
| Isolated homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing | PCA |
| Dense equal hemiparesis, no cortical signs, no field defect | Lacunar (internal capsule/pons) |
| Vertigo, ataxia, crossed sensory loss, Horner's, hoarseness | Lateral medullary — PICA/vertebral |
| Sudden reduced consciousness, quadriparesis, pinpoint pupils | Basilar occlusion / pontine |
| Preserved awareness, quadriplegia, vertical eye movement only | Locked-in (ventral pons) |
| Young patient, neck pain, Horner's, after neck trauma or chiropractic manipulation | **Carotid or vertebral dissection** |
| Young patient, migraine, recurrent subcortical strokes, family history, dementia | **CADASIL** (NOTCH3) |
| Stroke in pregnancy/puerperium, headache, seizures | Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, eclampsia, RCVS |

**Five lacunar syndromes:** pure motor hemiparesis (posterior limb of internal capsule — commonest), pure sensory stroke (VPL thalamus), sensorimotor stroke, ataxic hemiparesis (pons/internal capsule), clumsy-hand dysarthria.

#### 4.5 Hyperacute management ⚑

**Sequence:** ABC and glucose → NIHSS → **immediate non-contrast CT head** → decide reperfusion → CT angiography (± perfusion) if large-vessel occlusion possible → stroke unit.

**Thrombolysis**

- Agents: ⚑ the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline endorses **either alteplase or tenecteplase** within the 4.5-hour window; tenecteplase is a single bolus (0.25 mg/kg, max 25 mg) and is increasingly first-line, alteplase is 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg, 10% bolus then infusion over 1 hour). Older textbooks list alteplase only.
- **Window: within 4.5 hours of onset (or last known well)**, for disabling deficits ⚑ **regardless of NIHSS score**.
- **Extended/unknown-onset window:** selected patients **4.5–9 hours**, or wake-up stroke, using advanced imaging — perfusion core/penumbra mismatch (EXTEND) or **DWI–FLAIR mismatch** (WAKE-UP). ⚑ This did not exist in older texts.
- ⚑ For **non-disabling** deficits within 4.5 hours, dual antiplatelet therapy is now preferred over thrombolysis.
- **BP must be <185/110** before thrombolysis and kept **<180/105 for 24 hours** afterwards.
- **Absolute contraindications:** intracranial haemorrhage on imaging, previous ICH, active bleeding, known AVM/aneurysm/intracranial neoplasm at risk, recent significant head injury or intracranial/spinal surgery (<3 months), suspected SAH, GI haemorrhage <21 days, platelets <100, INR >1.7 or therapeutic anticoagulation, DOAC within 48 hours (unless assays normal), **glucose <2.8 mmol/L uncorrected**, uncontrolled BP above the threshold.
- **Complications:** symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage (~2–6%), angio-oedema (especially with ACE inhibitors), systemic bleeding.

**Mechanical thrombectomy**

- For **large-vessel occlusion** (intracranial ICA, M1, and selected M2/basilar).
- **Within 6 hours** on standard criteria; **6–24 hours** with perfusion/clinical mismatch selection (DAWN, DEFUSE-3).
- ⚑ Now also offered to patients with **large established ischaemic cores** (SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT, RESCUE-Japan LIMIT) — the old rule "ASPECTS <6 excludes thrombectomy" no longer holds.
- ⚑ **Basilar artery occlusion: thrombectomy within 24 hours with NIHSS ≥10** (ATTENTION, BAOCHE) — a strong recommendation in the 2026 guideline.
- Thrombolysis and thrombectomy are not alternatives: if eligible for both, give thrombolysis and proceed to thrombectomy without waiting for a response.

**Other acute measures**

- **Aspirin 300 mg** as soon as haemorrhage is excluded, or at **24 hours** if thrombolysed (after repeat imaging); continue 300 mg for 2 weeks then switch to long-term antithrombotic.
- **Do not lower BP acutely** in ischaemic stroke unless thrombolysing, or BP >220/120, or another indication (aortic dissection, heart failure, hypertensive encephalopathy). ⚑ Intensive lowering to <140 systolic is **not** recommended even after successful reperfusion.
- Glucose 4–11 mmol/L; treat fever; nil by mouth until swallow screened; VTE prophylaxis with **intermittent pneumatic compression** (not routine heparin); early mobilisation; stroke unit care (the single intervention with the best evidence overall).
- **Decompressive hemicraniectomy within 48 hours** for malignant MCA infarction in selected patients (age criteria vary; benefit on survival clear, on functional independence less so).

#### 4.6 Intracerebral haemorrhage

**Causes:** hypertension (deep — basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum), **cerebral amyloid angiopathy** (lobar, elderly, recurrent, microbleeds on SWI), anticoagulation, AVM/aneurysm, tumour, sympathomimetic drugs (cocaine, amphetamine), venous thrombosis.

**Management:**

- ⚑ **Rapid BP lowering to a systolic target of 130–140 mmHg within the first hour**, sustained — as part of a care bundle (INTERACT3, which showed reduced mortality/disability). Avoid dropping >70 mmHg from baseline.
- **Reverse anticoagulation urgently:** warfarin → **IV vitamin K + prothrombin complex concentrate**; dabigatran → **idarucizumab**; factor Xa inhibitors → **andexanet alfa** (or PCC where unavailable) ⚑.
- Neurosurgery for **cerebellar haemorrhage >3 cm** or with brainstem compression/hydrocephalus (this one is exam-standard), for hydrocephalus (EVD), and for selected lobar clot evacuation (minimally invasive approaches increasingly used ⚑).
- Reverse the reversible: glucose, temperature, seizures (treat, do not prophylax), head up 30°.
- Stop antithrombotics; the timing of restarting is individualised (often ~4–8 weeks if a strong indication such as a mechanical valve or high-risk AF).

#### 4.7 Subarachnoid haemorrhage

**Presentation:** **thunderclap headache** — maximal within seconds, "worst ever", often occipital; ± vomiting, neck stiffness, photophobia, reduced consciousness, seizure, **subhyaloid/vitreous (Terson's) haemorrhage** on fundoscopy, third nerve palsy (PCom aneurysm), sudden death.

**Causes:** ruptured **saccular (berry) aneurysm** (~85%) — commonest sites: anterior communicating artery, posterior communicating artery, MCA bifurcation; perimesencephalic non-aneurysmal (benign, ~10%); AVM, dissection, trauma.

**Associations:** autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, Ehlers–Danlos type IV, coarctation, family history, smoking, hypertension, alcohol.

**Diagnosis:** immediate **non-contrast CT** — sensitivity is >98% within 6 hours of onset and falls thereafter; if negative and the story is convincing, **LP at ≥12 hours** for **xanthochromia** (spectrophotometry). Then **CT angiography** to define the aneurysm.

**Management:**

- Neurosurgical/neuroradiology referral; **endovascular coiling is preferred to clipping** where the aneurysm is suitable (ISAT), ideally within 24–72 hours.
- **Nimodipine 60 mg orally 4-hourly for 21 days** — reduces poor outcome from delayed cerebral ischaemia. (Note: it is neuroprotective; it does not simply "prevent vasospasm on angiography".)
- Bed rest, analgesia, laxatives, careful BP control (avoid hypotension), euvolaemia, VTE prophylaxis mechanical initially.

**Complications and their timing:**

| Complication | Timing | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| Rebleeding | First 24–48 h (highest risk) | Sudden deterioration — secure the aneurysm early |
| **Delayed cerebral ischaemia / vasospasm** | **Days 4–14 (peak 7–10)** | New focal deficit or reduced GCS; treat with induced euvolaemia/hypertension, nimodipine, angioplasty |
| Hydrocephalus | Any time; early or late | Deteriorating consciousness; EVD/shunt |
| **Hyponatraemia** | Days | SIADH vs **cerebral salt wasting** (hypovolaemic — treat with salt and fluid, **not** fluid restriction) |
| Seizures | Any | Treat, don't prophylax |
| Neurogenic pulmonary oedema, stunned myocardium | Early | ECG changes mimicking ischaemia |

**Grading:** Hunt and Hess or WFNS (based on GCS and focal deficit) clinically; **modified Fisher** on CT (blood load predicts vasospasm).

#### 4.8 Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis

**Suspect when:** headache (often progressive over days, worse lying flat or on Valsalva), papilloedema, seizures, focal deficits **crossing arterial territories**, haemorrhagic infarcts in unusual places (parasagittal, temporal), or isolated intracranial hypertension.

**Risk factors:** pregnancy and puerperium, combined oral contraceptive, thrombophilia, dehydration, malignancy, local sepsis (mastoiditis, sinusitis), head injury, nephrotic syndrome, and (rarely) vaccine-induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia.

**Diagnosis:** **CT or MR venography**. Plain CT may show a dense cord/delta sign but is often normal. D-dimer is unhelpful if negative in isolated headache.

**Treatment:** ⚑ **anticoagulate — with low-molecular-weight heparin — even in the presence of haemorrhagic infarction**, then oral anticoagulation for 3–12 months. Treat raised ICP; decompressive surgery in severe cases. Warfarin remains standard; DOACs are a reasonable alternative in stable patients (RE-SPECT CVT, ACTION-CVT) ⚑ but not where VITT or antiphospholipid syndrome is present.

#### 4.9 Secondary prevention

| Setting | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Ischaemic stroke or TIA, non-cardioembolic | **Clopidogrel 75 mg daily** long term (aspirin + dipyridamole if intolerant) |
| **Minor stroke (NIHSS ≤3–5) or high-risk TIA** | ⚑ **Dual antiplatelet (aspirin + clopidogrel) for 21 days**, then clopidogrel alone (CHANCE, POINT, THALES). Loading doses used; do not continue DAPT long term — bleeding risk |
| Atrial fibrillation | **DOAC** (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran); warfarin if mechanical valve or moderate–severe mitral stenosis. Timing: ⚑ **early initiation (within days) is safe and is now supported** by ELAN/OPTIMAS; the old "1-3-6-12 day rule" is being superseded |
| Lipids | High-intensity statin (e.g. atorvastatin 80 mg) — target LDL <1.8 mmol/L (some guidance <1.4) |
| Blood pressure | Long-term target <130/80 in most; start once stable (usually after ~2 weeks) |
| **Symptomatic carotid stenosis 50–99% (NASCET)** | **Carotid endarterectomy within 2 weeks** of the event — benefit falls sharply with delay. Stenting in selected younger patients or high surgical risk |
| Asymptomatic carotid stenosis | Best medical therapy; surgery only in selected cases |
| **Patent foramen ovale** in cryptogenic stroke, age <60, high-risk PFO | ⚑ **PFO closure** (CLOSE, REDUCE, RESPECT) plus antiplatelet |
| Lifestyle | Smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, exercise, diabetes control, AF screening |

#### 4.10 Complications and rehabilitation

**Acute complications:** aspiration pneumonia (swallow screen before anything by mouth), malignant cerebral oedema (day 2–5 after large MCA infarct), haemorrhagic transformation, seizures, DVT/PE, pressure ulcers, depression, urinary retention/infection, malnutrition.

**Rehabilitation** is multidisciplinary from day one: physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy (both for aphasia and swallowing), dietitian, psychology, stroke nurse specialists. **Post-stroke complications to actively look for:** spasticity (physiotherapy, botulinum toxin), shoulder subluxation and pain, central post-stroke (thalamic) pain, emotional lability, post-stroke depression (common, treatable), fatigue, cognitive impairment/vascular dementia.

**Prognostic notes:** TACS carries the worst outcome (~60% dead or dependent at 1 year); LACS the best. Most functional recovery occurs in the first 3 months.

### Neurology (ANU) — stroke — Cerebrovascular Disease

- DEFINITION: sudden neurological deficit of a vascular basis with CNS tissue infarction, confirmed by neuroimaging
- RISK FACTORS: non-modifiable (↑age &gt; 65 y/o, F&gt;M, ethnicity e.g. Indigenous, familial), modifiable (TIA, HTN, smoking, cholesterol, ↓ diet, ↓ exercise, obesity, DM, EtOH, AF ↑ risk of thrombosis, coagulopathy in chronic liver disease)
- PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

ISCHEMIC STROKE (80%)

- TOAST Criteria

- LARGE-ARTERY ATHEROSCLEROSIS (embolus/thrombusis)- large artery &gt; 50% occlusion, infarct &gt; 1.5 cm
- CARDIOEMBOLISM- AF, valve disease, ventricular thrombi, cardiomyopathy
- SMALL-VESSEL OCCLUSION (LACUNAR)- a/w HTN, DM and ↑ age 🡪 lipohyalinosis, affecting small penetrating arteries/arterioles that originate from the base of the brain and supply basal ganglia, thalamus, deep white matter and brain-stem; infarct &lt; 1.5cm
- STROKE OF OTHER DETERMINED AETIOLOGY e.g. drugs, arterial dissection, thrombophilia, arteritis, anti-phospholipid syndrome, complex migraine, watershed infarcts
- STROKE OF UNDETERMINED AETIOLOGY- two/more causes, negative/incomplete evaluation

- Hypoxia 🡪 Necrosis at centre of ischemic damage becomes leaky 🡪 K+ and ATP 🡪 further inflammation + oedema of surrounding ischemic penumbra (initially vulnerable tissue salvageable) 🡪 widespread infarction 🡪 liquefaction necrosis 🡪 removed by macrophages 🡪 parenchymal volume loss

HAEMORRHAGIC (20%)

INTRACEREBRAL HAEMORRHAGE (ICH)- BLEEDING DIRECTLY INTO THE BRAIN PARENCHYMA

- Causes: HTN (most common), trauma, small microaneurysm rupture (Charcot-Bouchard), bleeding diatheses, amyloid angiopathy, drugs (amphetamine, cocaine), vasculitis; Sx may not be as sudden like embolism or SAH

SUBARACHNOID HAEMORRHAGE (SAH)- BLEEDING INTO THE CSF WITHIN THE SUBARACHNOID SPACE

- Causes: trauma (common), ruptured aneurysms, coagulopathies, vasculitides, tumour, cerebral artery dissections
- DIFFERENTIALS: drug intoxication, infections, migraines, metabolic, seizures, tumours
- PRESENTATION
- Sudden onset due to sudden ↑ICP, with worsening of symptoms as infarction progresses
- ↑ICP 🡪 headache (worse in morning + bending), N/V, mass effects, focal signs, herniation syndrome, HTN, afebrile
- General- ↓ LOC, altered mental status, confusion, neglect
- Language- dysarthria, aphasia, swallowing difficulty
- Vision- diplopia, eye deviation, asymmetric pupils, visual field defect
- ![](notes/images/neurology-anu/img011.jpg)
Coordination- ataxia, intention tremor, ↓ coordination
- Motor- UMN Sx
- Sensation- ↓ sensation
- Dominant hemisphere: aphasia, contralateral hemiparesis, dysarthria, reading, writing, calculation
- Non-dominant hemisphere: neglect, apraxia (dressing, constructional)
- However, constellation of neurological deficits can point to a certain vascular territory (in table below)
- Anterior circulation ischemia caused by occlusion to one of the carotid arteries/MCA (most common)
- Posterior circulation ischaemia more challenging as motor/sensory limb signs are absent
- Facial droop, Arm weakness (contralateral), Speech slurring, Time is crucial

- Anti-platelet- Aspirin 81 mg oral 24 hours after tPA or other anti-platelets if aspirin intolerant (e.g. clopidogrel)
- Anti-coagulant- if rtPA not received; IV heparin recommended or ensure INR b/w 2-3 if on warfarin already
- Intra-arterial thrombectomy by interventional radiology within 6 hours

HEMORRHAGIC:

- Anti-hypertensives: no conclusive BP target ranges but guidelines suggest that target sBP 140-160 systolic with IV anti-hypertensives is safe and appropriate management
- ICP lowering medical management
- Surgical

BOTH:

- NBM, possible intubation/ventilation, elevation of bed (for ↑ICP)
- IVF (avoid over-hydration to worsen cerebral oedema and under-hydration to further ischaemia)
- Glycemic control (BSL &lt;6.5)
- Control of BP (only treat ↑↑HTN)
- ↓ temp in febrile
- DVT prophylaxis (calf compressors)
- LONG-TERM:
- Reverse cause (AF; carotid endarcteomy for stenosis)
- Physiotherapy and speech pathology
- PREVENTION
- A: anti-platelets (aspirin)
- B: BP
- C: cessation of smoking, cholesterol
- D: diet, diabetes
- E: exercise
