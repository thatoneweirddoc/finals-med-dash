## Neurological Emergencies and Disorders of Consciousness


### Neurology Master Notes — Neurological Emergencies and Disorders of Consciousness

*Sources: Brain Trauma Foundation Guidelines (4th ed); NICE NG232 (head injury), NG234 (spinal metastases and metastatic spinal cord compression); Neurocritical Care Society status epilepticus guideline; Academy of Medical Royal Colleges code of practice for the diagnosis of death; Kumar & Clark; Merck Manual Professional; StatPearls. Scope: the conditions where minutes change outcome.*

---

#### 5.1 Raised intracranial pressure and herniation

Normal ICP in adults: **5–15 mmHg**. Treat sustained ICP **>22 mmHg** ⚑ (Brain Trauma Foundation 4th edition; older texts say >20). Remember **CPP = MAP − ICP**, target **60–70 mmHg**.

**Causes:** mass lesion (tumour, haematoma, abscess), oedema (trauma, large infarct, hepatic failure), hydrocephalus, venous outflow obstruction, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, hypercapnia.

**Clinical features:** headache worse in the morning / on lying flat / on coughing and straining, vomiting (often without nausea), **papilloedema**, visual obscurations, diplopia from a **false-localising sixth nerve palsy**, reduced consciousness. **Cushing's triad** — **hypertension (widened pulse pressure), bradycardia, irregular breathing** — is a late and ominous sign of brainstem compression.

**Herniation syndromes:**

| Syndrome | Anatomy | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Uncal (transtentorial)** | Medial temporal lobe over tentorium | **Ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil** (CN III), then contralateral hemiparesis; **Kernohan's notch** phenomenon can give *ipsilateral* (false-localising) hemiparesis |
| Central | Downward diencephalic displacement | Progressive reduced consciousness, small reactive then midposition pupils, Cheyne–Stokes → hyperventilation |
| Subfalcine (cingulate) | Under falx | Often silent; may compress ACA → leg weakness |
| **Tonsillar (coning)** | Cerebellar tonsils through foramen magnum | Neck stiffness, **respiratory arrest**, death — the reason not to LP with raised ICP |

**Management ladder:**
1. Airway, ventilation (**normocapnia PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa**), oxygenation, avoid hypotension.
2. **Head up 30°**, head midline, loosen collars/ties (venous drainage), treat pain, fever and seizures, avoid hypotonic fluids.
3. **Osmotherapy** — mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg or **hypertonic saline**; hypertonic saline is increasingly preferred, especially if hypovolaemic or hypotensive ⚑.
4. Sedation ± neuromuscular blockade; consider ICP monitoring.
5. **Short-term hyperventilation** to PaCO₂ ~4.0–4.5 kPa only as a bridge to definitive treatment — prolonged use causes ischaemia.
6. **Dexamethasone works for vasogenic oedema around tumour and abscess; it does not help traumatic or ischaemic oedema** (CRASH trial showed harm in head injury).
7. Definitive: evacuate the haematoma, resect the mass, EVD for hydrocephalus, decompressive craniectomy.

#### 5.2 The comatose patient

Coma requires **bilateral hemispheric dysfunction, a brainstem/RAS lesion, or a diffuse metabolic-toxic insult**.

**Structured approach:** ABC, glucose (and empirical thiamine before glucose in anyone at risk), naloxone if opioid features, temperature; then GCS, pupils, eye movements, motor response, respiratory pattern, fundoscopy, meningism, skin/needle marks, breath; then targeted investigation.

| Feature | Metabolic/toxic | Structural |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Gradual | Sudden |
| Pupils | Preserved reactivity | Asymmetric, fixed, or level-specific |
| Focal signs | Usually absent | Present |
| Eye movements | Roving, conjugate | Gaze deviation, disconjugate |
| Tone/movement | Tremor, asterixis, myoclonus | Posturing, focal |

**Causes to work through:** hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hypercapnia, sepsis, uraemia, hepatic failure, hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hypothyroidism (myxoedema coma), adrenal crisis, drugs/alcohol/opioids, Wernicke's, non-convulsive status, stroke/haemorrhage/SAH, trauma, meningitis/encephalitis, tumour, hydrocephalus, hypothermia.

**Locked-in syndrome, vegetative state and minimally conscious state are not coma** — a locked-in patient is fully aware, and the way to detect it is to ask them to look up and blink.

#### 5.3 Traumatic brain injury

**NICE NG232 — CT head within 1 hour if:** GCS <13 on initial assessment or <15 at 2 hours after injury; suspected open/depressed skull fracture; any sign of basal skull fracture (**haemotympanum, "panda" periorbital eyes, CSF otorrhoea/rhinorrhoea, Battle's sign**); post-traumatic seizure; focal neurological deficit; more than one episode of vomiting. **CT within 8 hours** for loss of consciousness/amnesia plus age ≥65, coagulopathy/anticoagulation, dangerous mechanism, or >30 min retrograde amnesia. ⚑ NG232 also advises CT within 8 hours for anyone on an anticoagulant even without other risk factors.

| | **Extradural haematoma** | **Subdural haematoma** |
|---|---|---|
| Vessel | **Middle meningeal artery** (pterion fracture) | **Bridging veins** |
| Population | Young, trauma | Elderly, alcohol, anticoagulated; minor or no trauma |
| CT shape | **Biconvex/lentiform**, does not cross sutures | **Crescentic**, crosses sutures, not midline |
| Course | **Lucid interval** then rapid deterioration | Acute, subacute or chronic (weeks of confusion, headache, fluctuation) |
| Treatment | Urgent craniotomy | Burr hole/craniotomy if symptomatic; chronic SDH often burr-hole drainage ± middle meningeal artery embolisation ⚑ |

**Diffuse axonal injury** — deceleration shear injury; CT may look near-normal while the patient is deeply unconscious; MRI shows petechial haemorrhages at the grey–white junction, corpus callosum and brainstem.

**Concussion / mild TBI:** transient neurological dysfunction without structural injury; graded return to activity; **second impact syndrome** is the rationale for not returning to sport while symptomatic. Post-concussion syndrome — headache, dizziness, poor concentration, irritability, for weeks to months.

**Other TBI principles:** avoid hypotension and hypoxia (each markedly worsens outcome); **tranexamic acid within 3 hours** reduces head-injury-related death in mild-moderate TBI (CRASH-3) ⚑; seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam/phenytoin for 7 days in severe TBI; **steroids are contraindicated** (CRASH).

#### 5.4 Status epilepticus

**Definition (ILAE operational):** ⚑ a generalised convulsive seizure lasting **≥5 minutes**, or repeated seizures without recovery of consciousness between them. (The older definition of 30 minutes describes the point at which neuronal injury becomes likely — **t₂** — not the point at which to start treating — **t₁ = 5 min**.)

**Timed ladder:**

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–5 min | ABC, oxygen, IV access, **check glucose**, thiamine if malnourished/alcohol, monitoring |
| 5 min | **Benzodiazepine**: IV lorazepam 4 mg (0.1 mg/kg), or buccal midazolam 10 mg / rectal diazepam if no access |
| 10 min | **Repeat the benzodiazepine once** (maximum two doses total — further doses mainly cause respiratory depression) |
| 15–20 min | **Second-line IV antiseizure medication**: ⚑ levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (max 4.5 g), sodium valproate 40 mg/kg (max 3 g), or phenytoin/fosphenytoin 20 mg/kg. **ESETT showed these three are equally effective (~50% each)** — levetiracetam is usually preferred for ease and safety; avoid valproate in women of childbearing potential |
| 30–60 min (refractory) | **General anaesthesia** — propofol, midazolam or thiopentone infusion, intubation, ICU, continuous EEG |
| >24 h despite anaesthesia | **Super-refractory** — ketamine, immunotherapy, ketogenic diet, treat the underlying cause |

**Always ask why:** poor adherence or withdrawal of antiseizure medication (commonest in known epilepsy), alcohol withdrawal, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, infection (meningitis/encephalitis), stroke or haemorrhage, tumour, eclampsia (**give magnesium sulfate**), drug toxicity, autoimmune encephalitis.

**Non-convulsive status** — unexplained persistent confusion or reduced consciousness, especially after a convulsion that "stopped": diagnose with **EEG**. Consider it whenever a post-ictal patient fails to wake up within 20–30 minutes.

**Complications:** hypoxia, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury, hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, arrhythmia, neuronal injury.

#### 5.5 Acute cord compression and cauda equina syndrome

**Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC)** — commonest primaries: **breast, lung, prostate**, also myeloma, renal, lymphoma. Presents with **progressive back pain, often thoracic, worse on lying flat, coughing or straining**, preceding neurology by weeks — a chance to intervene early.

**Immediate management (NICE NG234):**
1. **Dexamethasone 16 mg daily** (with PPI and glucose monitoring) unless lymphoma is suspected and tissue is needed first.
2. **MRI of the whole spine within 24 hours** (sooner if neurology is progressing) — whole spine because multiple levels are common.
3. Discuss with the MSCC coordinator/oncology and spinal surgery: **surgical decompression** for a good-prognosis patient with a single level or spinal instability, otherwise **radiotherapy**.
4. Flat bed rest with log-roll until spinal stability is assessed; analgesia; VTE prophylaxis; bladder care.

**Cauda equina syndrome** — a surgical emergency, usually a large central disc prolapse (also tumour, abscess, haematoma, trauma).

**Red flags:** bilateral sciatica, **saddle anaesthesia**, **urinary retention with overflow incontinence** (or loss of bladder sensation — an earlier sign than retention), faecal incontinence or loss of anal tone, progressive motor weakness, sexual dysfunction.

**Action: immediate MRI lumbosacral spine and emergency neurosurgical/spinal referral** — decompression ideally within 24–48 hours of onset and as soon as possible; delay predicts permanent sphincter dysfunction. Do not "wait until morning" and do not rely on a post-void residual alone.

| | Conus medullaris | Cauda equina |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Sudden, symmetrical | Gradual, asymmetrical |
| Pain | Less prominent | Severe radicular |
| Motor | Mixed UMN + LMN, symmetrical | **LMN only, asymmetrical** |
| Reflexes | Ankle jerks lost, knee jerks may persist, plantars ↑ | All lost |
| Sensory | Symmetrical saddle, dissociated | Asymmetrical saddle |
| Sphincter | **Early** and severe | Late |

#### 5.6 Neuromuscular respiratory failure

The trap: hypoxia and a falling oxygen saturation are **late** signs. A patient with GBS or a myasthenic crisis fatigues and then decompensates abruptly.

**Monitor forced vital capacity (FVC).** "**20/30/40 rule**": FVC <20 mL/kg, maximum inspiratory pressure less negative than −30 cmH₂O, or maximum expiratory pressure <40 cmH₂O → high risk, involve ICU. ⚑ In practice, **FVC <1–1.5 L, a fall of >30% from baseline, an inability to count to 20 in one breath, or a single-breath count of <15** should trigger ICU review; **FVC <15 mL/kg usually means intubation**.

Other warning signs: inability to lift the head from the pillow, weak cough, bulbar weakness with pooling of secretions, paradoxical abdominal breathing, tachypnoea, orthopnoea, restlessness. **Non-invasive ventilation is usually inappropriate** when there is bulbar weakness — these patients need intubation.

**Note:** in myasthenic crisis, differentiate from **cholinergic crisis** (excess anticholinesterase): the latter has muscarinic excess — miosis, salivation, lacrimation, diarrhoea, bradycardia, fasciculations. Modern practice is to withhold pyridostigmine in crisis and treat with **plasma exchange or IVIg plus steroids**.

#### 5.7 Delirium versus dementia

| Feature | **Delirium** | **Dementia** |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours–days | Months–years |
| Course | **Fluctuating**, worse at night | Slowly progressive |
| Consciousness/attention | **Impaired attention** — the cardinal feature | Clear until late |
| Hallucinations | Common, visual | Less common (except DLB) |
| Reversibility | Usually reversible | Usually not |

**Delirium causes (PINCH ME):** Pain, Infection, Nutrition, Constipation, Hydration, Medication (opioids, anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, steroids), Environment/Electrolytes. Also alcohol withdrawal, urinary retention, hypoxia, stroke.

**Management:** identify and treat the cause; non-pharmacological first (orientation, lighting, sleep hygiene, familiar faces, mobility, sensory aids, avoid catheters and restraints). Drugs only for distress or risk: **low-dose haloperidol** short term — **avoid antipsychotics in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies** (use quetiapine or clozapine if unavoidable); benzodiazepines are first-line only in alcohol withdrawal or seizure risk.

#### 5.8 Toxidromes and metabolic emergencies with neurological signatures

| Syndrome | Trigger | Signature | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** | Dopamine antagonist (or withdrawal of dopaminergic drug) | Days onset, **"lead-pipe" rigidity**, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, **very high CK**, normal or reduced reflexes | Stop the drug, cool, fluids, dantrolene ± bromocriptine |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | SSRI/SNRI/MAOI/tramadol/linezolid combinations | **Hours** onset, **clonus and hyperreflexia (legs > arms)**, agitation, mydriasis, diarrhoea, hyperthermia | Stop the drug, benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine |
| Malignant hyperthermia | Volatile anaesthetics, suxamethonium | Intra-operative rigidity, rising end-tidal CO₂, hyperthermia; RYR1 | Dantrolene |
| Anticholinergic | Antihistamines, TCAs, hyoscine | "Mad, hot, dry, red, blind" — delirium, dry skin, mydriasis, retention | Supportive; physostigmine rarely |
| **Wernicke's encephalopathy** | Thiamine deficiency (alcohol, hyperemesis, bariatric surgery, refeeding) | **Confusion, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia** (all three in <20%) | **IV thiamine (Pabrinex) before any glucose**; untreated → Korsakoff |
| Hepatic encephalopathy | Cirrhosis + precipitant | Asterixis, constructional apraxia, fetor | Lactulose, rifaximin, treat precipitant |
| **Osmotic demyelination** | Over-rapid correction of hyponatraemia | Delayed (days) quadriparesis, dysarthria, locked-in | Prevention: correct Na⁺ ≤8–10 mmol/L per 24 h |
| CO poisoning | Faulty heater, fire | Headache, confusion, cherry-red late, delayed leukoencephalopathy | High-flow O₂ ± hyperbaric |

> **Hook:** *NMS is rigid and slow; serotonin syndrome is clonic and fast.*

#### 5.9 Brain death and organ donation

Diagnosed clinically, in a patient with **known irreversible structural brain damage**, **deep apnoeic coma on no sedation**, and **excluded reversible causes** (drugs, hypothermia <34 °C, severe metabolic or endocrine derangement, neuromuscular blockade).

**Brainstem reflex testing — all must be absent:**
- Pupillary response to light (II/III)
- Corneal reflex (V/VII)
- Vestibulo-ocular (caloric) response to ice-cold water (VIII/III/VI)
- Motor response within cranial nerve distribution to supraorbital pressure (V/VII)
- Gag (IX/X) and cough on deep tracheal suction (X)
- **Apnoea test** — no respiratory effort with PaCO₂ allowed to rise above 6.65 kPa (and pH <7.40)

In the UK, testing is performed by two suitably experienced doctors (one a consultant), neither on the transplant team, on two separate occasions. Spinal reflexes may persist and do not exclude the diagnosis. Legal time of death is the time of the **first** set of tests.
