## Movement Disorders and Neurodegeneration


### Neurology Master Notes — Movement Disorders and Neurodegeneration

*Sources: MDS clinical diagnostic criteria for Parkinson's disease (2015); NICE NG71 (Parkinson's disease in adults), NG97 (dementia); consensus criteria for PSP (MDS 2017), MSA (2022), DLB (McKeith 2017); El Escorial/Gold Coast criteria and NICE NG42 (motor neurone disease); TGA and MHRA decisions on anti-amyloid antibodies; Kumar & Clark; Merck Manual Professional; StatPearls. ⚑ = changed from older textbooks.*

#### 9.1 Parkinson's disease

**Pathology:** degeneration of dopaminergic neurones in the **substantia nigra pars compacta** with intraneuronal **Lewy bodies containing α-synuclein**. Symptoms appear after ~50–70% of nigral neurones are lost. Braak staging explains the prodrome: the olfactory bulb and dorsal vagal nucleus are affected before the midbrain, which is why **anosmia, constipation, depression and REM sleep behaviour disorder can precede motor symptoms by years** ⚑.

**Cardinal features:** **bradykinesia** (obligatory) plus **rest tremor** and/or **rigidity**.

- **Bradykinesia** — slowness *with decrement in amplitude* on repetitive movement (finger tapping); micrographia, hypomimia, reduced arm swing, hypophonia.
- **Rest tremor** — 4–6 Hz, "pill-rolling", **asymmetric**, worse with distraction (counting backwards), improves on movement.
- **Rigidity** — "lead-pipe", with **cogwheeling** when tremor is superimposed; **positive Froment's manoeuvre** (activation of the other limb).
- Postural instability is a *later* feature — early falls argue against idiopathic PD.

**Diagnosis is clinical** (MDS criteria). NICE: refer untreated to a specialist; **DaTscan** is only for distinguishing PD from essential tremor when there is genuine doubt (it does **not** separate PD from parkinson-plus syndromes, which are also presynaptic).

**Non-motor features (often more disabling than motor):** anosmia, constipation, orthostatic hypotension, urinary urgency, depression and anxiety, apathy, REM sleep behaviour disorder, excessive daytime sleepiness, pain, dementia (~50% within 10 years), psychosis and hallucinations (often drug-related).

**Red flags that argue against idiopathic PD (i.e. suggest a "plus" syndrome or secondary cause):** symmetrical onset, **early falls (<3 years)**, early autonomic failure, early dementia, **early or severe bulbar dysfunction**, vertical gaze palsy, cerebellar signs, pyramidal signs, a rapid course, **poor or absent response to levodopa**, and prominent drug history.

#### 9.2 Parkinson-plus syndromes and secondary parkinsonism

| Syndrome | Discriminating features | Pathology |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)** | **Early falls, backwards**; **vertical supranuclear gaze palsy (down-gaze first)**; axial rigidity; surprised/staring facies; pseudobulbar affect; "hummingbird" midbrain on sagittal MRI | **Tau** |
| **Multiple system atrophy (MSA)** | **Early, severe autonomic failure** (postural hypotension, incontinence, impotence); MSA-P parkinsonian or MSA-C cerebellar; inspiratory stridor; **"hot cross bun" sign** in the pons | **α-synuclein** (glial cytoplasmic inclusions) |
| **Corticobasal degeneration** | **Markedly asymmetric** akinetic-rigid limb, **apraxia, alien limb phenomenon**, cortical sensory loss, myoclonus, dystonia | Tau |
| **Dementia with Lewy bodies** | **Dementia within 1 year of parkinsonism**, fluctuating cognition, **visual hallucinations**, REM sleep behaviour disorder, **severe neuroleptic sensitivity** | α-synuclein |
| **Vascular parkinsonism** | **"Lower-body parkinsonism"** — gait predominant, upper limbs spared; pyramidal signs; stepwise; vascular risk factors | Small-vessel disease |
| **Drug-induced parkinsonism** | **Symmetrical**, subacute, after antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, sodium valproate, lithium; resolves over weeks–months on withdrawal | Dopamine blockade |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** | Gait apraxia + urinary incontinence + dementia; ventriculomegaly out of proportion to atrophy | CSF dynamics |
| **Wilson's disease** | Under 50, tremor/dystonia, **Kayser–Fleischer rings**, liver disease, psychiatric change | Copper |

> **Hook:** *PSP falls backwards and can't look down; MSA faints and wets itself; CBD forgets the limb belongs to it; DLB sees things and cannot tolerate haloperidol.*

**Rule for DLB vs Parkinson's disease dementia:** the **"1-year rule"** — dementia within 1 year of (or before) the motor features = **DLB**; dementia arising well after established PD = **Parkinson's disease dementia**.

#### 9.3 Tremor

| Type | When present | Frequency | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rest** | At rest, improves with action | 4–6 Hz | Asymmetric, pill-rolling — **Parkinson's** |
| **Postural / action (essential tremor)** | Arms outstretched, on action | 6–12 Hz | **Bilateral, symmetric**, head/voice tremor, **improves with alcohol**, family history in ~60%; treat with **propranolol or primidone** |
| **Intention** | Worsens approaching a target | — | Cerebellar disease; with past-pointing and dysmetria |
| **Physiological/enhanced** | Postural | Fine, fast | Anxiety, caffeine, **thyrotoxicosis**, salbutamol, lithium, alcohol withdrawal |
| **Dystonic** | Position-dependent, jerky | Irregular | With dystonic posturing |
| **Functional** | Variable | **Entrainable and distractible** | Sudden onset, variable frequency |

**Essential tremor vs Parkinson's disease** is the classic pairing: essential tremor is **bilateral, postural/kinetic, faster, with head and voice involvement, improved by alcohol, and without bradykinesia**; parkinsonian tremor is **asymmetric, at rest, slower, with bradykinesia and rigidity**.

#### 9.4 Chorea, Huntington's and Wilson's

**Chorea** — brief, irregular, flowing, non-stereotyped involuntary movements that flit from one body part to another; **motor impersistence** (milkmaid grip, inability to keep the tongue protruded).

**Causes:** Huntington's disease, **Sydenham's chorea** (post-streptococcal, a major Jones criterion), chorea gravidarum, SLE and antiphospholipid syndrome, thyrotoxicosis, polycythaemia, drugs (levodopa, antipsychotic-induced **tardive dyskinesia**, cocaine, oral contraceptives), Wilson's disease, neuroacanthocytosis, hyperglycaemia (non-ketotic hyperosmolar → hemichorea).

**Huntington's disease:** autosomal dominant, **CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in *HTT* on chromosome 4** (≥40 repeats fully penetrant), with **anticipation** (worse and earlier in successive generations, especially with paternal transmission). Presents in mid-adulthood with the triad of **chorea, cognitive decline (subcortical dementia) and psychiatric disturbance** (depression, irritability, high suicide risk). MRI: **caudate atrophy with box-car ventricles**. No disease-modifying treatment; chorea managed with **tetrabenazine** (or antipsychotics); genetic counselling before predictive testing is mandatory.

**Wilson's disease:** autosomal recessive *ATP7B* mutation → impaired biliary copper excretion. **Always consider in anyone under 50 with a movement disorder, especially with liver disease or psychiatric symptoms.** Features: tremor (classically "wing-beating"), dystonia, dysarthria, parkinsonism, ataxia, **Kayser–Fleischer rings** (near-universal in the neurological form), hepatitis/cirrhosis, haemolysis, psychiatric change. Investigations: **low serum caeruloplasmin, low serum copper, high 24-hour urinary copper**, slit-lamp examination, liver biopsy copper; MRI **"face of the giant panda"** in the midbrain. Treatment: **penicillamine or trientine** (chelation), zinc for maintenance, and liver transplantation in fulminant disease.

#### 9.5 Dystonia, myoclonus and tics

**Dystonia** — sustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing twisting postures; often relieved by a **sensory trick (geste antagoniste)**, e.g. touching the chin in cervical dystonia.

- Focal: **cervical dystonia (torticollis)**, blepharospasm, writer's cramp, laryngeal dystonia. Treat with **botulinum toxin**.
- Generalised: **DYT1 (childhood-onset)**; **dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa)** — diurnal variation, dramatic and sustained response to small doses of levodopa, **so every young-onset dystonia deserves a levodopa trial**.
- Acute dystonic reaction: hours after a dopamine antagonist (metoclopramide, antipsychotics) — oculogyric crisis, torticollis; treat with **procyclidine or benztropine**.
- Secondary: Wilson's, cerebral palsy, drugs, stroke.

**Myoclonus** — brief shock-like jerks. Physiological (hypnic jerks, hiccup), essential, epileptic (JME), or symptomatic: **metabolic (uraemia, hepatic — asterixis is negative myoclonus), post-hypoxic (Lance–Adams), CJD, Alzheimer's late stage, drugs (opioids, gabapentinoids in renal failure, SSRIs)**.

**Tics** — brief, stereotyped, **suppressible with rising inner urge and relief on release** — the feature that separates them from chorea and myoclonus. **Tourette syndrome:** multiple motor tics plus ≥1 vocal tic for >1 year with onset before 18; comorbid OCD and ADHD are the rule. Treat behaviourally first (habit reversal/CBIT); drugs (clonidine, aripiprazole, tetrabenazine) if disabling.

#### 9.6 Ataxias

**Acquired:** alcohol (with thiamine deficiency — vermis), stroke, MS, posterior fossa tumour, hypothyroidism, **phenytoin** and lithium toxicity, coeliac disease, paraneoplastic (anti-Yo), vitamin E deficiency, Wernicke's, hydrocephalus.

**Inherited:**

| Disorder | Inheritance/genetics | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| **Friedreich's ataxia** | **Autosomal recessive**, GAA repeat in *FXN* (frataxin) | Onset <25 y; ataxia, **absent reflexes with extensor plantars**, loss of proprioception, **pes cavus, scoliosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, diabetes**; the commonest inherited ataxia |
| **Ataxia telangiectasia** | Autosomal recessive, *ATM* | Childhood ataxia, oculocutaneous telangiectasia, immunodeficiency, malignancy risk, raised AFP |
| **Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCA1–>40)** | **Autosomal dominant**, CAG repeats | Adult onset, anticipation; SCA3 (Machado–Joseph) commonest worldwide |
| **Episodic ataxia** | Autosomal dominant channelopathies | EA2 responds to acetazolamide |

#### 9.7 Dementia syndromes

**Definition:** acquired, progressive, global cognitive decline affecting function, with **clear consciousness** (the discriminator from delirium).

| Type | Proportion | Key features | Pathology |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alzheimer's disease** | ~60% | **Insidious episodic memory loss first**, then language and visuospatial; temporoparietal atrophy, medial temporal lobe atrophy on MRI | **Extracellular β-amyloid plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau neurofibrillary tangles** |
| **Vascular dementia** | ~20% | **Stepwise** decline, focal signs, vascular risk factors, executive/processing-speed deficits; white matter disease and infarcts | Ischaemia |
| **Dementia with Lewy bodies** | ~10–15% | **Fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder**; neuroleptic sensitivity; reduced uptake on DaTscan | α-synuclein |
| **Frontotemporal dementia** | ~5% (but commonest under 65 alongside AD) | **Onset 45–65**; behavioural variant — disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, hyperorality, compulsions, **memory relatively spared early**; or primary progressive aphasia (semantic/non-fluent) | Tau or **TDP-43**; overlap with MND; *C9orf72*, *MAPT*, *GRN* |

**Other causes:** Parkinson's disease dementia, Huntington's, prion disease, alcohol-related brain damage/Korsakoff, HIV, neurosyphilis, chronic subdural haematoma, tumour, **B12 or thyroid deficiency**, normal pressure hydrocephalus, depression ("pseudodementia").

**Assessment:** collateral history, cognitive testing (**MoCA, ACE-III, MMSE**), bloods to exclude reversible causes (FBC, U&E, calcium, glucose/HbA1c, LFT, TFT, **B12 and folate**, ± HIV/syphilis), **structural imaging (CT or MRI)**, and specialist tests where the diagnosis is unclear (**FDG-PET, DaTscan for DLB, MIBG cardiac scintigraphy, CSF amyloid/tau, or plasma p-tau217** ⚑ — blood biomarkers are entering practice).

**Management (NICE NG97):**
- **Non-pharmacological first**: cognitive stimulation therapy, exercise, occupational therapy, carer support and education, advance care planning, driving and financial capacity discussions, and **Power of Attorney early**.
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's, DLB and Parkinson's disease dementia. **Memantine** (NMDA antagonist) for moderate-to-severe AD, or if cholinesterase inhibitors are contraindicated/not tolerated. **Neither is indicated in frontotemporal dementia or pure vascular dementia** — in vascular dementia, treat the vascular risk factors.
- **Behavioural and psychological symptoms:** look for pain, infection, constipation and environmental triggers before drugs. Antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk, at the lowest dose for the shortest time — they increase stroke and mortality risk — and **avoid entirely in DLB** (use quetiapine or clozapine if unavoidable).
- ⚑ **Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies** (lecanemab, donanemab) for early symptomatic Alzheimer's with confirmed amyloid: they slow decline modestly (around 25–35% relative slowing on CDR-SB over 18 months) at the cost of **ARIA — amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (oedema ARIA-E and haemorrhage ARIA-H)**, requiring serial MRI, and increased risk in **ApoE ε4 homozygotes**. Regulatory position differs by country and is worth knowing: **MHRA licensed both, but NICE concluded they are not cost-effective for NHS use**; in **Australia the TGA registered lecanemab in September 2025, restricted to ApoE ε4 non-carriers and heterozygotes** (homozygotes excluded because of ARIA risk). Expect exams to ask about the mechanism, ARIA, and the ApoE ε4 issue rather than the funding decision.

#### 9.8 Normal pressure hydrocephalus and reversible dementias

**NPH triad — "wet, wacky and wobbly":** **gait apraxia first** (magnetic, wide-based, shuffling), then urinary incontinence, then a subcortical dementia. Communicating hydrocephalus with **ventriculomegaly out of proportion to sulcal atrophy** (Evans index >0.3) and a normal or high-normal opening pressure.

**The key test is a large-volume ("tap") lumbar puncture removing 30–50 mL of CSF, or extended lumbar drainage, with objective gait timing before and after.** Improvement predicts benefit from a **ventriculoperitoneal shunt**. Gait improves most; cognition least — so the earlier the shunt, the better.

**Always exclude the treatable:** B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, chronic subdural haematoma, depression, medication (anticholinergic burden, benzodiazepines, opioids), alcohol, sleep apnoea, neurosyphilis, HIV.

#### 9.9 Motor neurone disease

**Degeneration of upper and lower motor neurones, with no sensory involvement.** Median age of onset ~60; median survival **2–5 years** from symptom onset. ~10% familial (*C9orf72* is the commonest, and links MND with frontotemporal dementia; also *SOD1*, *TARDBP*, *FUS*).

**Phenotypes:**

| Variant | Features | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| **Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (~70%)** | Mixed UMN + LMN in the same region — **wasted, fasciculating limbs with brisk reflexes** | Typical |
| **Progressive bulbar palsy (~20%)** | Dysarthria, dysphagia, wasted fasciculating tongue, mixed bulbar/pseudobulbar signs | **Worst** |
| Progressive muscular atrophy | LMN only | Slower |
| Primary lateral sclerosis | UMN only | Slowest |

**Recognition:** progressive, painless, **asymmetric** weakness; wasting; **fasciculations**; cramps; **brisk reflexes in a wasted limb** (the single most useful sign); split hand; foot drop; bulbar symptoms; emotional lability (pseudobulbar affect); weight loss; up to 50% have cognitive change and ~15% frank FTD. **No sensory loss, no sphincter disturbance, no eye movement abnormality** — features that, if present, should make you doubt the diagnosis.

**Investigations:** clinical diagnosis supported by **EMG (widespread denervation and fasciculation potentials in ≥2–3 body regions)**, normal nerve conduction sensory studies, MRI brain and cervical spine to exclude compressive myelopathy, and bloods to exclude mimics (CK, anti-GM1 for **multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block** — a treatable mimic — B12, thyroid, paraprotein, HIV, Lyme).

**Management (NICE NG42):** multidisciplinary clinic.
- **Riluzole** — glutamate antagonist; extends survival by ~3 months; monitor LFTs.
- ⚑ **Newer agents** are limited and region-specific: **edaravone** is licensed in some countries; **tofersen** is approved for the rare *SOD1* form; sodium phenylbutyrate–taurursodiol was withdrawn after its confirmatory trial failed ⚑ — a useful reminder to check current status rather than an old textbook.
- **Non-invasive ventilation** — the intervention with the biggest quality-of-life and survival benefit; monitor FVC/SNIP and symptoms of nocturnal hypoventilation.
- **Gastrostomy (PEG/RIG) placed early**, before FVC falls below ~50% predicted.
- Symptom control: **riluzole aside, most of the work is symptomatic** — sialorrhoea (glycopyrronium, hyoscine, botulinum toxin), spasticity (baclofen), cramps (quinine), pseudobulbar affect (amitriptyline, dextromethorphan/quinidine), communication aids, palliative care involvement early, and advance care planning.

| After the internal capsule, the neurones pass through the crus cerebri of the midbrain, the pons and into the medulla.
