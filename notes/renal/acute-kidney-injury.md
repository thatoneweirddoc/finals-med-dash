## Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

### Finals-study-2.pdf — Renal Notes, Section 2: AKI (pages 24-31)

Cross-checked against KDIGO 2012 AKI guideline, NICE AKI guidance, Merck Manual

Professional, StatPearls, and Kidney360 (2023). AKI is the single highest-yield acute renal topic in finals — it appears in written papers, data interpretation, prescribing, and OSCE emergency stations.

#### 2.1 Definitions and Staging

##### KDIGO definition of AKI — any one of:

- 1. **↑ Creatinine ≥26.5 μmol/L (≥0.3 mg/dL) within 48 hours,** OR
- 2. **↑ Creatinine to ≥1.5 × baseline,** known or presumed to have occurred **within the prior**

**7 days,** OR
- 3. **Urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr for ≥6 hours**

Note the two different time windows: **48 hours** for the absolute rise, **7 days** for the relative rise.

Examiners test this.

##### KDIGO staging

| Stag e | Serum creatinine | Urine output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1.5–1.9 × baseline OR ↑ ≥26.5 μmol/L (≥0.3 mg/dL) | <0.5 mL/kg/hr for 6–12 hrs |
| 2 | 2.0–2.9 × baseline | <0.5 mL/kg/hr for ≥12 hrs |
| 3 | ≥3.0 × baseline OR creatinine ≥353.6 μmol/L (≥4.0 mg/dL) OR initiation of RRT OR (if <18 yrs) eGFR <35 | <0.3 mL/kg/hr for ≥24 hrs OR anuria ≥12 hrs |

###### Three rules that generate exam questions:

- **"Worse-of" rule** — stage by whichever criterion (creatinine or urine output) gives the **higher** stage.
- **RRT override** — starting dialysis = **Stage 3,** regardless of the numbers.

- **Urine output criteria are unreliable** in patients on **diuretics** or with **cirrhosis,** and really require ICU-level monitoring to apply properly.

Prognosis: even Stage 1 AKI roughly doubles long-term CKD risk. Mortality climbs steeply with stage (~9% stage 1 → 30–50% stage 3 requiring RRT).

##### Distinguishing AKI from CKD (and acute-on-chronic)

| Favours CKD | Favours AKI |
| --- | --- |
| Small, echogenic kidneys with poor corticomedullary differentiation on US | Normal-sized kidneys |
| Anaemia (EPO deficiency) | Normal Hb |
| Hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, ↑PTH (established CKD–MBD) | Normal bone profile |
| Previous blood results showing chronically raised creatinine | Previously normal creatinine |
| Renal osteodystrophy on imaging | — |

- **The single most useful investigation is old bloods.** Always look for a baseline creatinine.
- **Exceptions — CKD with normal/large kidneys:** diabetic nephropathy (early), amyloid,

HIV-associated nephropathy, myeloma, ADPKD, infiltration.
- **Acute-on-chronic** is common and behaves like AKI for management purposes — treat the acute insult.

#### 2.2 Classification by Cause

The pre-renal / intrinsic / post-renal framework is the backbone of every AKI answer. State it explicitly in exams — the structure earns marks.

##### Pre-renal (~60–70% of AKI — the commonest)

Kidney is structurally normal; the problem is perfusion. **Reversible if corrected promptly** — but if prolonged, progresses to ATN.
- **True hypovolaemia:** haemorrhage, GI losses (vomiting/diarrhoea), burns, diuretics, poor intake

- **Reduced effective circulating volume** (despite fluid overload!): **cardiorenal** **syndrome** (heart failure), **hepatorenal syndrome** (cirrhosis), nephrotic syndrome
- **Sepsis / distributive shock** — vasodilatation → renal hypoperfusion
- **Drug-induced haemodynamic AKI** (no structural damage — purely altered glomerular haemodynamics):
  - **NSAIDs** → block prostaglandins → **afferent arteriolar constriction** → ↓GFR
  - **ACEi/ARB** → block angiotensin II → **efferent arteriolar dilatation** → ↓GFR (dangerous in bilateral renal artery stenosis)
  - **Calcineurin inhibitors** (ciclosporin, tacrolimus) → afferent vasoconstriction
  - **The "triple whammy": NSAID + ACEi/ARB + diuretic.** Classic exam vignette.
- **Renal artery stenosis** with ACEi
- **Abdominal compartment syndrome** — see below

##### Intrinsic (renal)

Structural damage. Sub-classify by the compartment injured — this is how a good answer is organised. **Post-renal (obstruction)**

Always exclude — it's **reversible** and missing it is negligent. Requires **bilateral** obstruction (or unilateral in a single functioning kidney) to cause AKI.
- **Intraluminal:** stones, clot, sloughed papillae
- **Intramural:** ureteric stricture, tumour
- **Extramural: prostatic hypertrophy or cancer** (the commonest cause in older men), pelvic/cervical malignancy, **retroperitoneal fibrosis,** aortic aneurysm
- **First test: renal ultrasound** (within 24 hours if cause unclear)
- **Trap:** hydronephrosis may be **absent** in early obstruction, volume depletion, or **retroperitoneal fibrosis/encasing tumour** (ureters trapped, can't dilate)

#### 2.3 Specific AKI Syndromes

##### Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)

- **Ischaemic** (prolonged pre-renal state, hypotension, sepsis, surgery) vs **nephrotoxic** (aminoglycosides, contrast, amphotericin, cisplatin, myoglobin, light chains)
- **Urinalysis: muddy-brown granular casts,** renal tubular epithelial cells; **FeNa >1–2%;**

###### urine osm ~300 (isosthenuric)

- **Three phases** — worth knowing because the diuretic phase is a management trap:
  - 1. **Oliguric phase** (~1–2 weeks) — risk of fluid overload, hyperkalaemia
  - 2. **Polyuric/diuretic phase** — tubules recover GFR before they recover concentrating ability → **massive urine output, risk of hypovolaemia,** **hypokalaemia, hypernatraemia.** Must replace fluids and monitor electrolytes.

- 3. **Recovery phase**
- Usually **reversible over days–weeks** if the insult is removed — but often needs supportive dialysis in the interim

##### Rhabdomyolysis and pigment nephropathy

- **Causes:** crush injury, prolonged immobility ("long lie"), seizures, extreme exertion, **statins** (esp. with fibrates or macrolides), alcohol, illicit drugs, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, malignant hyperthermia
- **Diagnosis: CK >5× upper limit of normal** (i.e. typically **>1000 U/L); AKI risk rises**

###### steeply with CK >15,000–20,000 U/L

- **Classic triad** (myalgia, weakness, tea-coloured urine) is present in **<10%** — don't rely on it
- **Biochemistry — the pattern is very examinable:**
  - ↑↑ **CK,** ↑ **potassium,** ↑ **phosphate,** ↑ **urate, ↓ calcium** (early — deposits in damaged muscle), ↑ AST/ALT/LDH
  - **Late rebound hypercalcaemia** during recovery (calcium released from muscle)

— don't over-treat the early hypocalcaemia
  - **Dipstick blood positive, but NO red cells on microscopy** (it's myoglobin) — the mismatch clue
- **Management: early, aggressive IV fluid resuscitation** (ideally within 6 hours) — the single intervention with clear benefit; treat hyperkalaemia; stop the cause; monitor for compartment syndrome. Urinary alkalinisation and mannitol are traditional but evidence is weak — don't lead with them.

##### Contrast-associated AKI

- Creatinine typically rises at **48–72 hours,** peaks at 3–5 days, usually recovers
- **Risk factors:** pre-existing CKD (the biggest), diabetes, heart failure, hypovolaemia, high contrast volume, age, myeloma
- **Prevention: IV isotonic crystalloid (sodium chloride 0.9%) pre- and post-procedure** is the mainstay; withhold nephrotoxics; use the lowest contrast volume. N-acetylcysteine and bicarbonate have largely fallen out of favour (PRESERVE trial showed no benefit) — worth knowing this has changed.
- **Note the shift in thinking:** the true attributable risk is now believed to be **smaller than** **historically taught** — never withhold a genuinely indicated CT for fear of contrast nephropathy.

##### Tumour lysis syndrome (TLS)

- **When:** after starting chemotherapy in **bulky, chemosensitive tumours** (high-grade lymphoma, ALL, leukaemias); can occur spontaneously
- **The biochemistry — memorise the direction of each:**
  - ↑ **Potassium,** ↑ **phosphate,** ↑ **urate, ↓ calcium** (binds the excess phosphate)

- Everything up except calcium.
- **Mechanism of AKI:** uric acid and calcium phosphate crystal deposition in tubules
- **Prevention/management: IV fluids** (the mainstay), **allopurinol** (xanthine oxidase inhibitor — prevents urate formation, use prophylactically) vs **rasburicase** (recombinant urate oxidase — breaks down existing urate, used for high-risk/established TLS).

**Rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency** (causes haemolysis).
- Treat hyperkalaemia; dialysis if refractory

##### Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS)

- **A diagnosis of exclusion:** AKI in advanced cirrhosis/portal hypertension with **no other** **cause, no improvement after volume expansion (albumin),** no shock, no nephrotoxins, and a **bland urinalysis with very low urine sodium (<10 mmol/L)** and

FeNa <1%
- **Mechanism:** splanchnic vasodilatation → ↓ effective circulating volume → intense **renal** **vasoconstriction.** The kidneys are structurally normal (they work if transplanted into someone else — a favourite viva point).
- **Management: terlipressin (splanchnic vasoconstrictor) + IV albumin;** treat precipitants (SBP, GI bleed, over-diuresis, large-volume paracentesis without albumin cover). **Definitive treatment is liver transplantation.**

##### Cardiorenal syndrome

- Bidirectional: heart failure → renal hypoperfusion **and venous congestion** (raised renal venous pressure is now recognised as a major driver — not just "forward failure")
- The therapeutic dilemma: diuresis worsens creatinine short-term but is often the right thing — **a modest creatinine rise during effective decongestion is acceptable**

##### Abdominal compartment syndrome

- **Intra-abdominal pressure >20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction**
- Causes: massive fluid resuscitation, pancreatitis, trauma, bowel obstruction, ascites
- Compresses renal veins and reduces perfusion pressure → oliguric AKI
- **Measure via bladder pressure;** management is decompression (± laparotomy)

#### 2.4 Management

##### The structured approach (use this framework in any AKI question)

- 1. **Assess and resuscitate — is this patient sick?**
- A–E; treat shock, hyperkalaemia, pulmonary oedema, sepsis first

- 2. **Determine the cause — pre-renal / intrinsic / post-renal**
- History (drugs! fluid losses! systemic symptoms!), fluid status examination, urinalysis, bloods, **ultrasound to exclude obstruction**
- **Immunology screen if intrinsic renal disease suspected** (ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, complement, myeloma screen) — don't miss the treatable, time-critical GNs
- 3. **Fluid management**
- **Hypovolaemic → fluid resuscitate.** Balanced crystalloid (Hartmann's/Plasma-Lyte) or

0.9% saline in **boluses (e.g. 250–500 mL) with reassessment after each.**
  - Avoid large-volume 0.9% saline — hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis. Avoid

Hartmann's if hyperkalaemic (contains K⁺), though this is somewhat theoretical.
  - **Avoid starches** (increase AKI and mortality)
- **Fluid overloaded → diurese or dialyse.** Do not reflexively give fluid to every AKI —

###### fluid overload is itself harmful and increases mortality.

- **There is no role for "renal-dose dopamine" or routine furosemide to convert** **oliguric to non-oliguric AKI** — both are ineffective. Classic exam distractor.
- 4. **Stop the nephrotoxics; review every drug**
- **STOP:** NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, aminoglycosides, diuretics (if hypovolaemic), contrast
- **DOSE-ADJUST:** opioids, gabapentin, metformin (stop — lactic acidosis risk), DOACs,

LMWH, digoxin, many antibiotics
- **Sick day rules** — SADMANS-type medication holds during acute illness
- 5. **Treat the cause**
- Sepsis → antibiotics; obstruction → **catheter / nephrostomy;** GN → immunosuppression; hypercalcaemia/myeloma → specific treatment
- 6. **Monitor and support**
- Daily U&Es, fluid balance, **daily weights,** catheterise if urine output monitoring is critical
- Treat complications: hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload, anaemia, bleeding
- Nutrition; avoid hypoglycaemia

##### Indications for urgent dialysis — "AEIOU"

| Letter | Indication | Typical trigger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Acidosis | Severe metabolic acidosis, pH <7.1–7.2, refractory to medical therapy |
| E | Electrolytes | Refractory hyperkalaemia (K⁺ >6.5 mmol/L, or lower with ECG changes) not responding to medical management |
| I | Ingestion / Intoxication | Dialysable toxins: lithium, salicylates, methanol, ethylene glycol, valproate, metformin (severe lactic acidosis) |
| O | Overload | Pulmonary oedema refractory to diuretics |
| U | Uraemia | Symptomatic: uraemic pericarditis, uraemic encephalopathy, uraemic bleeding (platelet dysfunction), intractable nausea/vomiting |

Memory aid for dialysable toxins: **"I STUMBLED"** or simply remember small, water-soluble, low-protein-bound, low-volume-of-distribution drugs are dialysable — **lithium, methanol,** **ethylene glycol, salicylates** are the four to name.

**Key principle:** dialysis is indicated for **refractory** complications and **symptomatic** uraemia — not for an absolute creatinine number. Trials of early vs delayed RRT have not shown clear benefit for starting early in the absence of these indications.

##### Complications and prognosis

- **Acute:** hyperkalaemia (the immediate killer), acidosis, fluid overload/pulmonary oedema, uraemic complications, bleeding, infection
- **Recovery:** watch for the **polyuric phase** of recovering ATN — hypovolaemia and electrolyte losses
- **Long-term: AKI is not a benign, fully reversible event.** Even a single episode substantially increases the risk of:
  - **Progression to CKD** and end-stage kidney disease
  - **Cardiovascular events** and death
  - Recurrent AKI
- **All patients with AKI need follow-up creatinine and a medication review** — this is a commonly missed exam/SAQ point.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

###### Question Answer

| Fastest way to exclude a reversible cause? | Renal ultrasound — obstruction |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-renal or ATN? | FeNa <1%, urine osm >500, bland urine = pre-renal. FeNa >1–2%, urine osm ~300, muddy-brown casts = ATN. If on diuretics, use FeUrea <35% |
| Dipstick blood +++, no RBCs on microscopy? | Rhabdomyolysis — check CK |
| Everything up except calcium, post-chemo? | Tumour lysis syndrome |
| AKI + cirrhosis + urine Na <10 + no response to albumin? | Hepatorenal syndrome — terlipressin + albumin |
| AKI + haemoptysis? | Pulmonary–renal syndrome — anti-GBM or ANCA vasculitis. Urgent immunology + biopsy. |
| AKI + new drug (PPI/NSAID/antibiotic) + rash + eosinophilia? | Acute interstitial nephritis — stop the drug, consider steroids |
| AKI post-angiogram + livedo + eosinophilia? | Cholesterol embolisation |
| When to dialyse urgently? | AEIOU |
| Does "renal-dose dopamine" work? | No. Neither does routine furosemide to boost urine output |
