#### 8.1 Renal Stones

##### Types and risk factors

| Stone type | ~Frequency | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcium oxalate | ~80% (commonest) | Radio-opaque; hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia; low fluid intake |
| Calcium phosphate |  | Radio-opaque; associated with distal (type 1) RTA, alkaline urine |
| Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) | ~10% | Infection stones (urease-producing organisms — Proteus, Klebsiella); form staghorn calculi; alkaline urine |
| Uric acid | ~5–10% | Radiolucent (won't show on plain film); acidic urine, gout, high purine, tumour lysis |
| Cystine | rare | Inherited cystinuria; hexagonal crystals; from childhood |

##### Presentation and imaging

- **Renal colic:** sudden severe **loin-to-groin pain,** colicky, restless patient (can't get comfortable — contrast with peritonitis where they lie still), nausea, haematuria.
- **Imaging: non-contrast CT KUB is the gold standard** (detects nearly all stones including radiolucent uric acid; ultrasound for pregnancy/children to avoid radiation).

##### Management

- **Analgesia: NSAIDs (e.g. IM/PR diclofenac) are first-line** and superior to opioids for renal colic (caution in AKI/CKD).

- **Most small stones (<5 mm) pass spontaneously; medical expulsive therapy** **(tamsulosin)** may help stones 5–10 mm in the distal ureter.
- **Indications for intervention:** stone unlikely to pass, uncontrolled pain, **obstruction** **with infection (emergency),** AKI, solitary kidney.
- **Modalities: shockwave lithotripsy (ESWL), ureteroscopy + laser, percutaneous** **nephrolithotomy (PCNL)** for large/staghorn stones.
- ⚠ **Obstructed + infected kidney = urological emergency** → **urgent decompression** (nephrostomy or ureteric stent) + antibiotics. Do not delay for stone removal.
- **Prevention:** high fluid intake (>2.5 L/day), reduce salt/animal protein, normal dietary calcium (not low), thiazides for hypercalciuria, potassium citrate, allopurinol for uric acid.

#### 8.2 Infection

| Entity | Features | Management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower UTI (cystitis) | Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain; no systemic upset; leucocytes/nitrites | Short-course antibiotics (nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim per local guidance) |
| Pyelonephritis | Fever, rigors, loin pain, nausea/vomiting + LUTS; white cell casts | Broader/longer antibiotics; admit if unwell; image if not improving |
| Complicated UTI | Structural/functional abnormality, stones, catheter, pregnancy, immunosuppression, male | Longer courses; investigate underlying cause |
| Catheter-associate d UTI | Only treat if symptomatic; don't treat colonisation | Remove/change catheter; antibiotics only if symptoms |
| Renal/perinephric abscess | Persistent fever despite antibiotics, flank mass | CT; drainage + antibiotics |

- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria — when to treat:** generally **do NOT treat,** EXCEPT in **pregnancy** (risk of pyelonephritis/preterm labour) and **before urological procedures** with anticipated mucosal breach. Treating otherwise drives resistance. Common exam point.

#### 8.3 Obstructive Uropathy

- **Causes:** luminal (stone, clot), mural (stricture, tumour), extrinsic **(BPH/prostate cancer**

— commonest in older men, pelvic malignancy, retroperitoneal fibrosis, AAA).
- **Hydronephrosis** on ultrasound (may be absent early or if ureters encased).
- **Post-obstructive diuresis:** after relieving chronic bilateral obstruction, a **massive** **diuresis** can follow (accumulated solute/urea, tubular dysfunction, volume overload correction) → **monitor fluid balance and electrolytes closely,** replace losses.
- **Benign prostatic hyperplasia:** LUTS (hesitancy, weak stream, nocturia, incomplete emptying); can cause **chronic retention → obstructive AKI.** Treat with **α-blockers** **(tamsulosin)** ± **5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride);** catheterise acute retention.

#### 8.4 Cystic Kidney Disease

##### Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD)

- **Genetics: PKD1 (chromosome 16, ~85%, more severe)** or **PKD2 (chromosome 4,** **milder);** autosomal dominant.
- **Renal:** bilateral enlarging cysts → **hypertension** (early), haematuria, cyst infection/haemorrhage, stones, progressive CKD → ESKD.
- **Extra-renal manifestations (examinable): berry (intracranial) aneurysms →** **subarachnoid haemorrhage, hepatic cysts,** mitral valve prolapse, **diverticular** **disease,** abdominal wall hernias.
- **Management:** BP control **(ACEi/ARB),** treat complications; **tolvaptan (V2 receptor** **antagonist)** ⚑ slows cyst growth and eGFR decline in rapidly progressing ADPKD — a relatively recent disease-modifying option (monitor LFTs, causes marked aquaresis/thirst). Screen family; consider MRA screening for aneurysms if family history.

##### Other cystic disease

- **Simple cysts** — very common, benign, incidental; use the **Bosniak classification** to grade malignant potential of complex cysts.
- **Acquired cystic disease** — in long-standing dialysis patients; increased RCC risk.
- **Medullary sponge kidney** — cystic dilatation of collecting ducts; recurrent stones and infection, usually benign.
- (Autosomal recessive PKD — infantile, rarer, severe.)

#### 8.5 Renal and Urological Malignancy

##### Renal cell carcinoma (RCC)

- Arises from **proximal tubular epithelium; clear cell** commonest. Risk: smoking, obesity, **VHL disease,** dialysis-acquired cysts.
- **Classic triad (haematuria + loin pain + flank mass)** is late and uncommon (<10%); most found incidentally on imaging.
- **Paraneoplastic syndromes (a favourite): polycythaemia (EPO), hypercalcaemia**

###### (PTHrP), hypertension (renin), Stauffer syndrome (non-metastatic hepatic

**dysfunction).**
- **Spread:** into **renal vein/IVC** (can cause a **left varicocele** if it obstructs the left gonadal vein), lungs ("cannonball" metastases).
- Management: **surgery (partial/radical nephrectomy);** tyrosine kinase inhibitors/immunotherapy for advanced disease (relatively chemo/radio-resistant).

##### Urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma

- Bladder/ureter/renal pelvis; **painless visible haematuria;** risk: **smoking, aromatic** **amines (dye/rubber industry),** cyclophosphamide.
- Diagnosis: **cystoscopy + CT urogram + urine cytology.**

##### Prostate cancer (renal relevance)

- Can cause **bladder outflow / ureteric obstruction → post-renal AKI.** Presents with

LUTS, bone pain (sclerotic mets), raised PSA.

##### Haematuria — the diagnostic pathway

- **Visible (macroscopic) haematuria** → always investigate: **cystoscopy + upper-tract** **imaging (CT urogram),** especially **painless visible haematuria in an older smoker =** **malignancy until excluded.**
- **Non-visible (microscopic):** confirm it's real (not myoglobin/haemoglobin — check microscopy for RBCs), exclude UTI, then assess with imaging/cystoscopy if persistent or with risk factors.
- **Glomerular vs urological bleeding: dysmorphic RBCs, red cell casts, proteinuria**

**→ glomerular (nephrology); isomorphic RBCs, clots, no casts → urological** **(urology).**

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Clue | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Loin-to-groin colic, restless patient | Renal stone — CT KUB |
| Radiolucent stone (not on plain film/CT easily) | Uric acid stone |
| Staghorn calculus + urease organism (Proteus) | Struvite (infection) stone |
| Fever + obstructed kidney | Urological emergency — decompress (nephrostomy/stent) + antibiotics |
| Asymptomatic bacteriuria — treat when? | Only in pregnancy / pre-urological procedure |
| Massive diuresis after relieving obstruction | Post-obstructive diuresis — replace fluids, monitor electrolytes |
| ADPKD + sudden severe headache | Ruptured berry aneurysm (SAH) |
| Drug that slows ADPKD progression | Tolvaptan |
| RCC + polycythaemia/hypercalcaemia | Paraneoplastic syndrome |
| Painless visible haematuria, older smoker | Urothelial/bladder cancer until excluded — cystoscopy + CT urogram |
| Dysmorphic RBCs + casts vs isomorphic RBCs + clots | Glomerular (nephrology) vs urological (urology) |

### Nephrology-and-Urology-ANU.docx — Developmental pathologies

#### developmental pathologies

- POTTER SYNDROME: in bilateral renal agenesis or obstructive uropathy (Pulmonary hypoplasia, Oligohydramnios – trigger, Twisted face, Twisted skin, Extremity defects, Renal failure- in utero)

- HORSESHOE KIDNEY: inferior kidney poles fuse and the ureters course medially and anteriorly over joined renal isthmus  can cause hydronephrosis, uteropelvic joint obstruction, renal stones, infection, chromosomal aneuploidy syndromes (Turner syndrome, trisomies 13/18/21), renal cancer

- CONGENITAL SOLITARY FUNCTIONING KIDNEY: one functioning kidney; majority asymptomatic

- UNILATERAL RENAL AGENESIS: complete absence of kidney/ureter

- MULTICYSTIC DYSPLASTIC KIDNEY: non-functional kidney with cysts + connective tissue (bilateral=POTTER)

- DUPLEX COLLECTING SYSTEM: associated with vesicoureteral reflux and/or ureteral obstruction,  risk for UTIs

- POSTERIOR URETHRAL VALVES:  urethral obstruction and bladder outlet obstruction in male infants, and can be diagnosed prenatally by hydronephrosis and dilated/thick-walled bladder on U/S

- RENAL ECTOPIA: Abnormal renal location usually results when a kidney fails to ascend from its origin in the true pelvis. It can cause ureteropelvic junction obstruction, vesicoureteral reflux and multicystic renal dysplasia

- RENAL HYPOPLASIA:  ureteral bud branching; underdeveloped kidney with histologically normal nephrons

- VESICOURETERAL REFLUX DISEASE: most common congenital abnormality and is discovered in utero through a morphology scan at 18 weeks. High grades are associated with renal failure and sclerotic renal lesions.

| Treatment | Underlying cause Catherization: Immediate transurethral Foley in acute retention, and intermittent in chronic retention Suprapubic catheter if above not possible (e.g. urethral stricture, ↑prostate, contraindications e.g. trauma) Post-operative: encourage ambulation, a-blockers to relax bladder neck outlet |
| Complications | Hydronephrosis Definition: distention/dilation of renal pelvis and calyces with proximal dilatation Aetiology: mechanical (congenital, renal stones, BPH, cervical cancer, ureteral trauma), functional (neuropathic, pharmacological e.g. a-adrenergic, hormonal e.g. pregnancy), VUR Complications: compression, atrophy of renal cortex and medulla |

### Nephrology-and-Urology-ANU.docx — Prostate (anatomy, physiology, BPH, prostatitis, prostate cancer)

#### prostate

#### anatomy

- Location: between the bladder and the penis; just in front of the rectum

- Anatomy: urethra runs through the centre of the prostate

- Three zones:

- Peripheral (70% volume):  fibromuscular stroma

- Central (25% volume):  contains secretory ducts

- Transitional (5% volume): proximal to urethra

- Blood supply: inferior vesical artery and middle rectal arteries

- Nerves: parasympathetic (S2-4) and sympathetic (L1-2)

#### PHYSIOLOGY

- 70% glandular and 30% stromal tissue

- Secretes fluid that nourishes and protects sperm – alkaline to neutralize vaginal acidity, proteolytic enzymes, prostatic acid phosphatase, PSA

- During ejaculation, the prostate squeezes this fluid into the urethra, and it’s expelled with sperm as semen

- The vasa deferentia bring sperm from the testes to the seminal vesicles. The seminal vesicles contribute fluid to semen during ejaculation.

#### benign prostatic hyperplasia

| Definition | Benign stromal and glandular hyperplasia of the transitional zone of the prostate |
|---|---|
| Epidemiology | Prevalence increases with age (~50% of 50 y/o, ~80% of 80 y/o) |
| Aetiology | DHT: converted from testosterone by 5-α reductase and is a potent prostatic growth factor Gene amplification of androgen receptors: present in glandular epithelial and stromal cells  ↑ androgen receptor sensitivity to androgens  prostatic hyperplasia Androgen-oestrogen imbalance: ↑ oestrogen: testosterone (with ↑ age) Stem cell proliferation and longevity |
| Pathophysiology | Alpha-adrenergic receptors in prostate capsule, stroma and bladder neck which produce DHT Glandular/stromal hyperplasia  prostatic urethral compression  bladder outlet obstruction In bladder outlet obstruction, ↑ detrusor activity (irritative Sx), weakening of bladder wall (UTIs, retention, bladder stones), detrusor muscle hypertrophy (trabeculation/pseudodiverticula) |
| Risk Factors | Cigarette smoking, metabolic syndrome, genetic predisposition |
| AUA BPH Symptom Score | FUNWISE Frequency, Urgency, Nocturia, Weak stream, Intermittency, Straining, Emptying incomplete feeling 0-7: Mildly symptomatic 8-19: Moderately symptomatic 20-35: Severely symptomatic Note: dysuria not included in score but is commonly associated with BPH Initial alpha-adrenergic antagonist monotherapy for <20, combination therapy for >20 |
| Presentation | LUTS: irritative (frequency, urgency, urge incontinence, dysuria) and obstructive symptoms (hesitance, straining, poor/intermittent stream, dribbling, sensation of incomplete voiding) Gross haematuria DRE: symmetrically enlarged, smooth (no nodules), firm, non-tender, rubbery/elastic text |
| Investigations | Urinalysis and urine culture: UTI, haematuria Bloods: PSA (co-existent prostate cancer), renal (BUN, Cr, EUC) Abdominal U/S: ↑ total prostate valud, ↑ post-void residual urine volume (> 100 mL) Transrectal U/S: if prostate cancer suspected from DRE/PSA Uroflowmetry: normal maximal urinary flow rate ↓ and is <15 mL in bladder outlet obstruction and <10 mL is an indication for surgery CTAP: hydronephrosis, mass Cystoscopy/biopsy if indicated (suspected malignancy, surgical intervention) |
| Differentials | Prostate cancer, prostatitis, prostatic abscess, urethral strictures, posterior urethral valves |
| Management | Lifestyle:  limit fluids, bladder training, monitoring Medical therapy: indicated in mild BPH, discomfort from symptoms Alpha-blockers (e.g. tamsulosin, doxazosin. terazosin)  relaxation of smooth muscle of bladder neck and urethra  ↓ resistance to urinary outflow  symptom improvement 5-α reductase inhibitor (e.g. finasteride, dutasteride)  blocks conversion of testosterone to DHT  ↓ prostatic growth, ↑ prostatic apoptosis/involution SE: sexual/erectile/ejaculatory dysfunction, ↓ libido Parasympatholytics/anticholinergics (e.g. oxybutynin, darifenacin) Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors Surgical intervention: indicated in severe BPH Sx, complications Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP): resection of the hyperplastic prostatic tissue under cystoscopy guidance, using a cautery resectoscope Laser ablation: microwave thermotherapy Open/laparoscopic/robotic prostatectomy: in > 75g prostates Transurethral incision of the prostate (TUIP): indicated in patients with small prostates with obstructive symptoms or those at high risk for surgical complications Almost all surgical interventions lead to retrograde ejaculation into the bladder because of anatomical changes |
|  | Recurrent UTIs, urinary retention, overflow incontinence, hydronephrosis, chronic kidney disease, gross haematuria, bladder calculi, erectile dysfunction |

#### acute prostatitis

| Definition | Inflammation of prostate gland from infectious/non-infectious origin |
|---|---|
| Epidemiology | Most common urologic diagnosis in men < 50 y/o |
| Aetiology | Bacterial: 80% due to E. coli, 5-10% from enterococcus Chronic pelvic pain syndrome: no detectable causative organism, unknown mechanism |
| Risk factor for bacterial | Infections (urethritis, cystitis, epididymitis), indwelling catheter, transurethral surgery, protstate biopsy, voiding dysfunction, bladder outlet obstruction |
| Pathophysiology | Reflux of infected urine  reflux of bacteria from urethra to prostate A causative pathogen is detected by routine methods in only 5 – 10% of cases ↑ serum IgG/IgA acutely and fall to normal levels after treatment (?immunological role) |
| Presentation | Acute: spiking fevers/chills/malaise, irritative Sx, cloudy urine, severe perineal pain with defecation that radiates to scrotum/testes/penis/suprapubic/lower back, tender/boggy/swollen prostate Chronic: ↓ grade fever, irritative Sx, erectile dysfunction, bloody semen, mild pain, prostate often normal/enlarged |
| Investigations | Urinalysis: ↑WBC, gram stain, leukocytes, nitrates Urine MC&S Bloods: ↑ inflammatory markers, ↑PSA, leucocytosis U/S: residual volume Uroflowmetry/cystoscopy if indicated |
| Management | Antibiotic (fluoroquinolones e.g. ciprofloxacin first line or trimethoprim second line) NSAID Suprapubic catheterisation: in acute urinary retention/persistent fever |

#### prostate cancer

| Definition | Malignant tumour of glandular origin, situated in the prostate |
|---|---|
| Epidemiology | Most prevalent cancer in males, 75% diagnosed between 60-85 |
| Risk factors | Advanced age, family history, African-American race, genetic (BRCA-2, Lynch Syndrome), obesity, dietary fat (Note: BPH is NOT a risk factor) |
| Pathophysiology | High-grade prostatic intra-epithelial neoplasia is the most likely precursor of invasive cancer  can spread up to bladder neck or ejaculatory ducts and seminal vesicles 60-70% nodules in the peripheral zone, 10-20%  in the transition zone, 5-10% arise in central zone Adenocarcinoma: > 95%, expresses PSA, multifocal Other types: urothelial, endometrial Method of spread: local invasion, lymphatic (obturator > iliac > presacral/para-aortic), hematogenous dissemination occurs early |
| Presentation | Early-stage: asymptomatic (usually found during screening tests) Advanced-stage: Constitutional Urinary: obstructive, irritative retention, haematuria (terminal), incontinence Hydronephrosis Erectile dysfunction Metastatic disease Bone pain: bone metastasis, especially lumbosacral spine) Neurological deficits due to spinal cord compression Lymphedema: usually causes swelling, pain, and redness in the legs |
| Investigations | DRE: asymmetrical, nodular prostate that is smooth, non-firm and painless (pain in adv stage) PSA: Glycoprotein produced by epithelial cells of the prostate gland that leaks into circulation in the setting of disrupted glandular architecture Total PSA > 4 ng/mL suggests malignancy but may be ↑ in BPH, UTI, prostatitis, trauma and manipulation of prostate gland. Normal PSA values do not exclude prostate cancer! ↑ alkaline phosphatase in bone metastases Renal function: normal, except for locally advanced disease causing obstruction Prostate biopsy: Transurectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided needle biopsy confirms diagnosis and aids grading. ∼ 12 prostate samples are biopsied from different areas of the prostate Bone scan (technetium-99m) and CT scan for osteoblastic lesions and asses extra-prostatic extension, liver mets and urinary obstruction |
| Gleason Score | Sum of two scales scored 1 – 5 Grade ranges from 1–5, with grade 1 being the most and grade 5 the least differentiated Grade 1: well-differentiated, microscopically uniform glands without invasion into adjacent healthy prostate tissue; Grade 5: undifferentiated cancer cells with no glandular differentiation) |
| Management | Localised disease:  watchful waiting in elderly with comorbidity and life expectancy < 10 years) Definite treatment: Radiation therapy: external beam radiation and brachytherapy (Iodine-125 radioactive seeds implanted into the prostate) Radical prostatectomy: in localised disease to remove entire prostate gland (including prostatic capsule, seminal vesicles, vas deferens → infertility, pelvic lymph nodes) Anti-androgen therapy: GnRH agonist(e.g. leuprolide)/antagonist (e.g. degarelix) with antiandrogen (e.g. flutamide, bicalutamide). The aim is to ↓ serum testosterone to ↓ prostate cancer growth Surgical castration: bilateral orchictomy Disseminated disease: Anti-androgen therapy Chemotherapy with Docetaxel (mitotic inhibitor) Osteoclast inhibitors (bisphosphonates, denosumab) in bone metastases Treatment based on stage T1/2 (localised, low risk): watchful waiting T1/2 (intermediate, high-risk): definite therapy T3/4: EBRT, androgen deprivation therapy or RP N>0 or M>0: Hormonal therapy/palliative radiotherapy for metastases Bilateral orchiectomy which removes 90% of testosterone GnRH agonists (e.g. leuprolide, goserelin) or antagonist (e.g. degarelix) Oestrogens (e.g. DES) Antiandrogens (bicalutamide) Hormone-refractory prostate cancer: chemotherapy e.g. docetaxel, cabazitaxel |
| Complications | Metastasis, surgery/RT complications e.g. erectile dysfunction, urinary incontinence, infertility |
| Prognosis | T1-T2 is comparable to normal life expectancy T3-T4, 40-70% 10 year survival rate However, morbidity resulting from treatment can be high |

#### testicular torsion

| Definition | A condition in which the spermatic cord becomes twisted within the scrotum, leading to venous engorgement, arterial compromise and irreversible damage if not resolved after 6-12 hours |
|---|---|
| Epidemiology | Peak incidence in neonatal period and puberty |
| Aetiology | Idiopathic (bell-clap deformity  mobile testes  twists itself  ischemia/toxin), iatrogenic, trauma |
| Presentation | Acute testicular pain, with swelling/oedema, local erythema Abnormal testicle position: abnormal transverse lie, scrotal elevation, possible undescended testes Absent cremasteric reflex No relief upon elevation (Prehn sign negative) Nausea, vomiting, fever, abdominal pain, urinary frequency |
| Investigations | Urine dipstick: epididymitis Urgent grey-scale/doppler ultrasound: twisting of spermatic cord, ↓ perfusion in affected testicle, testicular heterogenicity indicates necrosis |
| Differentials | Testicular appendix torsion: blue dot sign, normal cremasteric reflex Epididymitis or epididymo-orchitis: delayed onset over days, pain and swelling <35 years old: N. gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis ≥35 years old or penetrative anal intercourse: GI organisms (especially E. coli) Hydrocele: trans-illuminate with light Varicocele: painless scrotal mass Testicular cancer:  gradual onset, no pain |
| Management | Treatment should be within 6 hours Symptomatic: analgesia, anti-emetic Immediate scrotal exploration with reduction (untwisting) and bilateral orchidopexy (fixation) ± orchiectomy |
