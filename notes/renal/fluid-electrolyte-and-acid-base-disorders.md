## Fluid, Electrolyte and Acid-Base Disorders

### Finals-study-2.pdf — Renal Notes, Section 6: Fluid, Electrolyte and Acid-Base Disorders (pages 53-58)

Cross-checked against UK Kidney Association hyperkalaemia guideline (2023), European hyponatraemia guidance, and standard texts. This is the single highest-yield section for data-interpretation questions — the algorithms below are what examiners build vignettes around.

#### 6.1 Sodium and Water

Key principle: **sodium problems are water problems.** Serum sodium reflects water balance relative to sodium, not total body sodium. Volume status is assessed separately and clinically.

##### Hyponatraemia — the diagnostic algorithm

###### Step 1 — exclude the fakes:

- **Pseudohyponatraemia** (severe hyperlipidaemia/paraproteinaemia — normal measured osmolality)
- **Hyperosmolar hyponatraemia** (hyperglycaemia, mannitol — water pulled out of cells; correct sodium ~+2.4 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L glucose rise)

###### Step 2 — assess volume status (the pivot):

| Volume status | Urine Na | Causes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypovolaemic | <20 (extra-renal loss) | D&V, burns, third-spacing, pancreatitis |
|  | >20 (renal loss) | Diuretics, Addison's, salt-wasting nephropathy, cerebral salt wasting |
| Euvolaemic | Variable (often >20) | SIADH, hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency, primary polydipsia, low solute intake |
| Hypervolaemic | <20 (usually) | Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome (↓ effective circulating volume); renal failure (>20) |

**Step 3 — urine osmolality:** <100 mOsm/kg → appropriate water excretion (primary polydipsia, beer potomania/low solute); >100 → ADH acting (inappropriately if euvolaemic → SIADH).

##### SIADH

- **Diagnostic criteria:** true **euvolaemia,** hyponatraemia with **low plasma osmolality,** **inappropriately concentrated urine (osm >100), urine Na >30,** normal thyroid/adrenal function, no diuretics.
- **Causes: CNS** (stroke, haemorrhage, meningitis), **pulmonary** (pneumonia, TB, small cell lung cancer), **malignancy** (SCLC — ectopic ADH), **drugs** (SSRIs, carbamazepine, chemotherapy), post-op/pain/nausea.
- **Management: fluid restriction** first-line; treat cause; **tolvaptan** (V2 antagonist) or demeclocycline in resistant cases; hypertonic saline only for severe symptoms.

##### Correction rates & osmotic demyelination

- **Correct slowly:** no more than **~8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours** (some guidance says ≤8 in high-risk).
- **Over-rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia → osmotic demyelination** **syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis)** — delayed quadriparesis, dysarthria, "locked-in", pseudobulbar palsy. Often irreversible. **This is the exam's favourite**

###### iatrogenic catastrophe.

- **Severe symptomatic hyponatraemia** (seizures, coma): **hypertonic (3%) saline** in small boluses — the exception where you act fast, but still cap the 24-hour rise.

##### Hypernatraemia and diabetes insipidus

- Hypernatraemia = **water deficit** relative to sodium (usually inadequate water intake ± losses). Causes: dehydration, DI, osmotic diuresis, hypertonic fluids.
- **Diabetes insipidus:** polyuria + polydipsia + dilute urine.
  - **Cranial DI** (↓ADH production): trauma, tumour, surgery, infiltration → **responds**

###### to desmopressin

  - **Nephrogenic DI** (ADH resistance): lithium, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia →

###### does NOT respond to desmopressin

- **Water deprivation test:** urine fails to concentrate on deprivation; then give desmopressin — **cranial concentrates, nephrogenic doesn't.**

#### 6.2 Potassium

##### Hyperkalaemia

- **Causes:** reduced excretion **(AKI/CKD, hypoaldosteronism, ACEi/ARB/MRA, NSAIDs,** **trimethoprim, heparin),** transcellular shift **(acidosis, insulin deficiency/DKA,** **rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, digoxin toxicity, suxamethonium),** increased load, and **pseudohyperkalaemia** (haemolysed sample, thrombocytosis).
- **ECG changes (progressive — this order is examinable):**
  - 1. **Tall, tented T waves**
  - 2. Flattened/absent P waves, **PR prolongation**
  - 3. **Broad QRS**
  - 4. **"Sine wave"** → VF/asystole
- **Emergency management (K⁺ ≥6.5, or ≥6.0 with ECG changes):**
  - 1. **Protect the myocardium: IV calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL** (or calcium chloride) — onset minutes, lasts ~30–60 min, repeat if ECG changes persist.

Does **not** lower potassium.
  - 2. **Shift K⁺ into cells: insulin (10 units soluble) + 25 g glucose IV** (monitor for hypoglycaemia, continue glucose infusion if pre-treatment glucose <7); **nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg** as adjunct.
  - 3. **Remove K⁺ from body: sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC)** or patiromer (newer GI binders, now used acutely and chronically ⚑ — largely replacing calcium resonium, which is slow/ineffective acutely); **dialysis** if refractory.
- Bicarbonate is no longer recommended as a routine acute agent unless there is severe acidosis. If your notes still lead with "calcium resonium," that's outdated for acute management.

##### Hypokalaemia

- **Causes:** GI loss (D&V), renal loss **(diuretics** — the commonest, hyperaldosteronism,

RTA types 1&2, Bartter/Gitelman), transcellular shift **(insulin, β-agonists, alkalosis,** **refeeding),** poor intake.
- **ECG changes: flattened T waves, U waves,** ST depression, prolonged QT → risk of arrhythmia.
- **Magnesium: hypokalaemia is often refractory until magnesium is replaced** — low

Mg promotes renal potassium wasting. **Always check and replace magnesium.** A classic exam "why won't the potassium come up?" point.

#### 6.3 Calcium, Phosphate, Magnesium

##### Hypercalcaemia

- **Causes: primary hyperparathyroidism** and **malignancy** account for ~90% (bony metastases, PTHrP, myeloma); others: sarcoidosis (↑vitamin D), thiazides, thyrotoxicosis, immobility, milk-alkali.

- **Symptoms: "stones, bones, groans, moans"** (renal stones, bone pain, abdominal pain/constipation, psychiatric); polyuria, short QT.
- **Management: IV 0.9% saline rehydration** first-line; **bisphosphonates** for malignancy; treat cause. (Loop diuretics only once rehydrated and if overloaded — not routine.)

##### Hypocalcaemia

- **Causes:** CKD (low calcitriol), hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy), vitamin D deficiency, hypomagnesaemia, pancreatitis, rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis.
- **Signs: Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs,** perioral tingling, tetany, **prolonged QT,** seizures.
- **Management:** calcium (IV for severe/symptomatic), vitamin D; **correct magnesium.**

##### Phosphate & magnesium

- **Hyperphosphataemia:** CKD, tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis → binds calcium.
- **Hypophosphataemia:** refeeding syndrome, Fanconi, DKA treatment, alcohol.
- **Hypomagnesaemia:** diuretics, PPIs, alcohol, diarrhoea, Gitelman → causes refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia.

#### 6.4 Acid–Base

##### Systematic ABG/VBG interpretation (do it in this order)

- 1. **pH** — acidaemia (<7.35) or alkalaemia (>7.45)?
- 2. **Primary disorder** — is CO₂ or HCO₃⁻ the driver? (Respiratory if CO₂ deranged in the same direction as pH; metabolic if HCO₃⁻ is.)
- 3. **Compensation** — appropriate or is there a second disorder?
- 4. **Anion gap** (if metabolic acidosis)
- 5. **Delta ratio** (if raised anion gap)

##### Anion gap

**Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻);** normal ~8–12 mmol/L (correct for albumin — a low albumin lowers the measured gap).

| Raised anion gap metabolic acidosis (MUDPILES / KULT) | Normal anion gap (hyperchloraemic) |
| --- | --- |
| Methanol, Uraemia, DKA, Propylene glycol/paraldehyde, Iron/isoniazid, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates | Diarrhoea (GI HCO₃⁻ loss), RTA, Addison's, acetazolamide, ureteric diversion, saline overload |

##### Osmolar gap and toxic alcohols

- **Osmolar gap = measured − calculated osmolality** (calculated = 2×Na + glucose + urea). Raised (>10) → unmeasured osmoles.
- **Raised anion gap acidosis + raised osmolar gap → toxic alcohol (methanol,** **ethylene glycol)** until proven otherwise. Ethylene glycol → **calcium oxalate crystals** +

AKI. Treat with **fomepizole** (or ethanol) ± dialysis.

##### Delta ratio (in raised anion gap acidosis)

**Δ ratio = (rise in anion gap) / (fall in HCO₃⁻)**
- **<1** → additional **normal anion gap acidosis**
- **1–2** → pure high anion gap acidosis
- **>2** → coexisting **metabolic alkalosis** or pre-existing high HCO₃⁻ (e.g. chronic respiratory acidosis)

##### Metabolic alkalosis

- **Chloride-responsive (urine Cl⁻ <20): vomiting/NG loss, diuretics** — corrects with saline.
- **Chloride-resistant (urine Cl⁻ >20): hyperaldosteronism, Cushing's,**

**Bartter/Gitelman, severe hypokalaemia** — doesn't correct with saline.

##### Compensation rules (quick reference)

- **Metabolic acidosis:** expected CO₂ = **(1.5 × HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2** (Winter's formula)
- **Metabolic alkalosis:** CO₂ rises ~0.7 mmHg per 1 mmol/L rise in HCO₃⁻
- **Acute respiratory:** HCO₃⁻ changes ~1 mmol/L per 10 mmHg CO₂ change; **chronic:**

~4–5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg (renal compensation takes days)
- If compensation is outside the expected range, there is a **second (mixed) disorder.**

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Clue | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Hyponatraemia, euvolaemic, urine osm >100, urine Na >30 | SIADH (fluid restrict) |
| Delayed quadriparesis after fast Na correction | Osmotic demyelination — correct ≤8–10 mmol/L/24h |
| Polyuria, dilute urine, responds to desmopressin | Cranial DI (vs nephrogenic = no response) |
| Tall tented T waves | Hyperkalaemia — calcium first |
| Potassium won't correct | Check and replace magnesium |
| Raised anion gap + raised osmolar gap | Toxic alcohol (methanol/ethylene glycol) |
| Normal anion gap acidosis | Diarrhoea or RTA (urine anion gap separates them) |
| Metabolic alkalosis, urine Cl <20 | Chloride-responsive — vomiting/diuretics, give saline |

RENAL TUBULAR ACIDOSIS

- Type I (distal): inability to secrete H+ in collecting duct  impaired excretion of NH4+n into urine (associated with K+ and  risk of Ca3(PO4)2 stones)

- Type II (proximal): impaired HCO3- reabsorption (associated with hypoK+)

- Type III: combination of Types I and II and is extremely rare

- Type IV: defective ammoniagenesis due to aldosterone, hyporesponsiveness to aldosterone, or hyperK+

METABOLIC ACIDOSIS TREATMENT

- Underlying cause

  - Fluid resus and insulin in DKA

  - Restore tissue perfusion in lactic acidosis

  - Ethanol/fomepizole ± dialysis for methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning

  - Alkaline diuresis ± dialysis if ASA overdose

- Electrolyte disturbance of K+

- Exogenous alkali (NaHCO3-): SE: hypoK+, ECF volume overload, overshoot alkalosis

METABOLIC ALKALOSIS TREATMENT

- Underlying cause

- Replenish K+ and Mg2+ deficits

- Possible K+-sparing diuretic

- Saline sensitive metabolic: vol repletion ± carbonic anhydrase inhibitor (e.g. acetazolamide) to facilitate urinary excretion of HCO3-

- Saline resistant metabolic alkalosis: remove source of aldosterone or glucocorticoid ± spironolactone
