## Renal Physiology (Foundation)

### Finals-study-2.pdf — Renal System Notes, Part 1: Renal Physiology (pages 2-10)

Compiled from Guyton & Hall, Robbins, StatPearls, TeachMePhysiology, Deranged Physiology, and cross-checked against Merck Manual/AJKD core curriculum. Numbers given are the standard "textbook average" figures used in exams — real physiological values vary between individuals.

#### 1. Glomerular Function

##### GFR (Glomerular Filtration Rate)

- **Normal GFR ≈ 120 mL/min (~180 L/day)** in a healthy adult (StatPearls; Guyton).
- Determined by the **Starling forces** across the glomerular capillary and the glomerular filtration barrier (endothelium → basement membrane → podocyte slit diaphragms), which filter by size, shape, and charge.
- **Autoregulated** across a mean arterial pressure of ~50–150 mmHg via:
  - **Myogenic mechanism** — afferent arteriole stretch → reflex constriction.
  - **Tubuloglomerular feedback** — macula densa senses distal NaCl delivery and adjusts afferent arteriolar tone.
- GFR clearance is measured using **inulin** (gold standard, freely filtered, not reabsorbed/secreted) or estimated clinically via **creatinine clearance.**

##### Starling Forces (net filtration pressure)

Net filtration pressure = (glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure − Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure) − (glomerular capillary oncotic pressure − Bowman's space oncotic pressure)

| Force | Effect on GFR |
| --- | --- |
| ↑ Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure | ↑ GFR |
| ↑ Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure (e.g. ureteric obstruction) | ↓ GFR |
| ↑ Glomerular capillary oncotic pressure (e.g. dehydration, high plasma protein) | ↓ GFR |
| Afferent arteriole constriction | ↓ RBF and ↓ GFR (FF unchanged) |
| Efferent arteriole constriction (e.g. Angiotensin II) | ↓ RBF but GFR maintained/↑ (FF ↑) — clinically relevant with ACEi/ARB in bilateral renal artery stenosis |
| Prostaglandins | Dilate afferent arteriole, protect GFR in states of ↓ renal perfusion — hence NSAIDs (block PG) risk AKI in hypovolaemia |

##### Creatinine vs Urea

|  | Creatinine | Urea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Muscle creatine breakdown | Hepatic protein/amino acid metabolism (urea cycle) |
| Handling | Freely filtered, not reabsorbed; small amount of tubular secretion (so creatinine clearance slightly overestimates true GFR) | Freely filtered, ~40–50% passively reabsorbed (more with low tubular flow/dehydration) |
| Influenced by | Muscle mass, exercise, rhabdomyolysis, certain drugs blocking tubular secretion (e.g. trimethoprim, cimetidine — raise creatinine without truly lowering GFR) | Protein intake, GI bleeding, catabolic states, dehydration, hepatic function |
| Clinical use | Best routine marker of GFR (via eGFR equations) | Raised disproportionately to creatinine ("high urea:creatinine ratio") suggests pre-renal cause (dehydration, GI bleed) |

##### Urea Cycle

**Where it happens:** Only in **hepatocytes** (liver) — it's the only organ with the full enzyme set.

Purpose: converts toxic ammonia (from amino acid breakdown) into water-soluble, non-toxic **urea** for renal excretion.

###### Key exam points:

- **2 nitrogens** go into each urea molecule — one from free ammonia, one from aspartate.
- **CPS1/OTC deficiency** → hyperammonaemia; **OTC deficiency** is the most common urea cycle disorder and is **X-linked** (all others are autosomal recessive).
- Ornithine is regenerated, not consumed — it's a true catalytic cycle.
- Costs **4 ATP-equivalents** per urea molecule (2 in step 1, 1 in step 3 used as 2 high-energy bonds since ATP→AMP+PPi).

##### Filtration Fraction (FF)

- **FF = GFR / Renal Plasma Flow (RPF)**
- Normal RPF ≈ **600–650 mL/min;** Normal GFR ≈ **120 mL/min** → **FF ≈ 0.20 (20%)** — confirmed consistently across StatPearls, Mayo Clinic Labs, and Wikipedia/derived sources.
- Kidneys receive **20–25% of cardiac output.**
- **Efferent arteriolar constriction (Angiotensin II)** → ↓RPF, GFR maintained → **FF** **increases.** This is the physiological basis for why ACE inhibitors/ARBs can precipitate

AKI in bilateral renal artery stenosis (loss of efferent constriction → GFR falls).

#### 2. Tubular Function

##### Sodium Handling (know the % at each segment — classic exam table)

| Segment | % of filtered Na⁺ reabsorbed | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) | ~65% | Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (basolateral) + NHE3 Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger, Na⁺-glucose/amino acid cotransport (apical). Isosmotic — water follows equally, so tubular fluid remains iso-osmolar throughout. |
| Thick ascending limb (Loop of Henle) | ~25% | Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) — target of loop diuretics (furosemide). Water-impermeable segment → generates the medullary concentration gradient ("diluting segment"). |
| Distal convoluted tubule (DCT) | ~5–10% | Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) — target of thiazide diuretics. Also water-impermeable. |
| Collecting duct (principal cells) | ~2–5% | ENaC (epithelial Na channel) — aldosterone-sensitive, final fine-tuning; target of potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride blocks ENaC; spironolactone blocks the aldosterone receptor). |
| Final urine | <1% of filtered load | — |

(Figures cross-checked across Deranged Physiology, Khan Academy, and Guyton-based sources — proximal 65%, loop 25%, DCT 5%, CD 2–5% is the standard consensus.)

##### Water Handling

- **PCT reabsorbs ~65% of filtered water** (isosmotic, obligatory, follows Na⁺).
- **Descending thin limb of loop of Henle** — permeable to water only (~10% reabsorbed), impermeable to Na⁺ → concentrates tubular fluid as it descends into the hyperosmotic medulla.
- **Ascending limb (thin + thick)** — impermeable to water, reabsorbs solute only → dilutes tubular fluid ("diluting segment").
- **Countercurrent multiplication:** descending limb (water out) + ascending limb (solute out, no water) generates the corticomedullary osmotic gradient (up to ~1200 mOsm/kg at the papilla), maintained by the **vasa recta** acting as a countercurrent exchanger.

- **Collecting duct** — water permeability is **ADH-dependent** (variable, 0–~19% additional, capable of near-total recovery). Under maximal ADH, urine can be concentrated up to

~1200 mOsm/kg; without ADH, urine is dilute (~50 mOsm/kg).

##### Potassium Secretion

- Net K⁺ handling = **reabsorption in PCT (~65%) and thick ascending limb (~25%)** **regardless of intake,** then **regulated secretion in the principal cells of the late**

**DCT/collecting duct** determines final excretion.
- Principal cell secretion via **ROMK channels** (and BK channels under high flow), driven by the lumen-negative potential generated by ENaC-mediated Na⁺ reabsorption.
- **Increased by:** aldosterone, high distal tubular flow rate, high luminal Na⁺ delivery, alkalosis, hyperkalaemia itself.
- **Intercalated cells** (type A) can reabsorb K⁺ via H⁺/K⁺-ATPase in states of K⁺ depletion.
- Reciprocal relationship with H⁺: hypokalaemia → ↑H⁺ secretion (risk of metabolic alkalosis); hyperkalaemia → inhibits ammoniagenesis, impairing acid excretion (relevant to Type IV RTA).

##### Acid–Base Regulation

- **Filtered bicarbonate load ≈ 4,000–5,000 mmol/day** (GFR × plasma [HCO₃⁻]); virtually all must be reclaimed or acid-base balance collapses.
- **~85% of HCO₃⁻ reabsorption occurs in the PCT,** a further ~10–15% in the thick ascending limb.
  - 1. Mechanism: apical **NHE3** secretes H⁺ into lumen → combines with filtered HCO₃⁻

→ carbonic anhydrase → CO₂ + H₂O → CO₂ diffuses back into cell → reforms

HCO₃⁻ (via carbonic anhydrase) → exits basolaterally via **NBCe1** into blood. (Net:

HCO₃⁻ is not reabsorbed directly — it is regenerated.)
- **Generation of "new" bicarbonate** (to replace that consumed buffering fixed acids) occurs via:
  - 1. **Ammoniagenesis** in the PCT — glutamine → glutamate + NH₄⁺; glutamate → α-ketoglutarate → 2 new HCO₃⁻. NH₄⁺ is trapped in the tubule and excreted (the major adaptable urinary buffer).
  - 2. **Distal H⁺ secretion** via **H⁺-ATPase** in **Type A intercalated cells,** combining with titratable acids (mainly phosphate, HPO₄²⁻) in the lumen.
- **Type B intercalated cells** secrete HCO₃⁻ via **pendrin** during metabolic alkalosis (mirror-image of type A cells).
- Aldosterone stimulates distal H⁺ and K⁺ secretion (hyperaldosteronism → metabolic alkalosis + hypokalaemia).
- Chronic hypokalaemia stimulates ammoniagenesis and perpetuates metabolic alkalosis; hyperkalaemia inhibits ammoniagenesis, contributing to the acidosis of Type IV

RTA/hypoaldosteronism.

##### Calcium and Phosphate Regulation

- **Calcium:** ~99% of filtered load reabsorbed.
  - **PCT ~65%** (passive, paracellular, follows Na⁺/water — "solvent drag").
  - **Thick ascending limb ~20–25%** (paracellular, driven by the lumen-positive potential from NKCC2/ROMK recycling).
  - **DCT ~5–10%** — the main site of regulated, active, transcellular calcium reabsorption via **TRPV5 channels, calbindin-D28k, and the basolateral**

**Ca-ATPase/Na-Ca exchanger** — this is the segment upregulated by **PTH and**

**1,25-(OH)₂-vitamin D (calcitriol).**
- **Phosphate:** handled similarly to glucose — reabsorbed almost entirely in the **PCT** via

**NaPi-2a/NaPi-2c sodium-phosphate cotransporters.**
  - **PTH inhibits proximal tubular phosphate reabsorption** → phosphaturia.
  - **FGF-23** (from osteocytes) also inhibits NaPi cotransporters and additionally suppresses 1α-hydroxylase (opposing PTH's effect on vitamin D activation).

###### Enzyme deficiencies - Inborn errors of metabolism

- **OTC deficiency is the most common urea cycle disorder and is X-linked** — every other urea cycle disorder is autosomal recessive. This is a classic exam discriminator.
- Classic neonatal presentation of a urea cycle disorder: **poor feeding, vomiting,** **lethargy, ↑ ammonia, and respiratory alkalosis** (ammonia stimulates the respiratory centre → hyperventilation). The respiratory alkalosis is the tell — it distinguishes this from organic acidaemias, which cause a metabolic acidosis.
- **OTC deficiency → ↑ urinary orotic acid.** Reason: the block is after carbamoyl phosphate, so it accumulates, spills out of the mitochondrion, and gets diverted into **pyrimidine synthesis** → orotic acid.
- **CPS1 deficiency → normal/low orotic acid,** because the block is before carbamoyl phosphate is ever made. (Hyperammonaemia + orotic aciduria = OTC; hyperammonaemia without orotic aciduria = CPS1.)
- **Hereditary orotic aciduria (UMP synthase deficiency)** also causes orotic aciduria — but with **no hyperammonaemia,** because the urea cycle is intact. It presents instead with megaloblastic anaemia unresponsive to B12/folate.
- **ASS1 deficiency = citrullinaemia** → markedly **↑ plasma citrulline** (the substrate builds up behind the block).
- **ASL deficiency = argininosuccinic aciduria** → **↑ argininosuccinate** in blood and urine.
- General principle: **the intermediate immediately upstream of the blocked enzyme** **accumulates.** Use this to reason out any urea cycle disorder rather than memorising each.

###### Management of hyperammonaemic crisis

- **Stop all protein intake** and give **IV glucose ± lipids** — provides calories to halt catabolism (endogenous protein breakdown is itself an ammonia source).
- **Sodium benzoate** conjugates with **glycine** → **hippurate,** excreted renally.

- **Sodium phenylbutyrate** (→ phenylacetate) conjugates with **glutamine** → **phenylacetylglutamine,** excreted renally. Both create a **nitrogen escape route that** **bypasses the broken urea cycle.**
- **Arginine or citrulline supplementation** — replenishes cycle intermediates downstream of the block (arginine becomes conditionally essential when the cycle can't make it).
- **Haemodialysis** if ammonia is very high — clears ammonia far faster than peritoneal dialysis, which matters because prolonged hyperammonaemia causes irreversible neurological injury.
- Ammonia crosses the blood–brain barrier → cerebral oedema and encephalopathy; this is the same final pathway as **hepatic encephalopathy** in liver failure.

###### Links back to renal physiology

- **Urea is ~40–50% passively reabsorbed** in the tubule (more so at low tubular flow rates); **creatinine is not reabsorbed.** Hence urea has a much lower fractional excretion.
- A **disproportionately high urea:creatinine ratio** points to a **pre-renal** picture — dehydration, hypovolaemia, GI bleed, or a high catabolic state.
- Plasma urea can rise **independently of GFR** with: **GI bleeding** (blood = a large protein load), high-protein feeds, **corticosteroids,** and tissue breakdown/catabolism.
- Conversely, urea can be **falsely low** in liver failure (can't synthesise it), malnutrition/low protein intake, and pregnancy (↑ GFR + dilution).
