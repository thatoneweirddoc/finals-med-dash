## Gastroenteritis


### Paediatrics (ANU) — Gastroenteritis

#### Key leaning outcomes

- Severity of gastroenteritis
- Management of mild, moderate and severe dehydration
- List appropriate fluids and application of oral rehydration
- Discuss the management of IV fluid therapy for children with gastroenteritis
- Summarise recognition and management of hypovolemic shock
- List discharge criteria and considerations relevant to children with gastroenterit

#### Overview

- Common acute, intestinal, communicable infection causing vomiting, diarrhoea and fever. A small proportion will have severe dehydration and electrolyte disturbance, which can lead to shock/death.
- Causes: Usually viral, but sometimes bacterial/parasitic. Community outbreaks are sporadic and seasonal (winter)
- Risks: Over-hydration and/or inappropriate electrolyte replacement 🡪 death from cerebral oedema

#### differentials

Acute appendicitis, strangulated hernia, intussusception or other causes of bowel obstruction, UTI, meningitis and other types of sepsis, raised ICP, DKA, IBD, inborn errors of metabolism, hemolytic uremic syndrome

#### RED FLAGS- Consider alternative diagnosis if there is:

- Fever &gt; 39OC
- Abdominal distension
- Bilious vomiting
- Blood in vomit/stool
- Severe abdominal pain
- Vomiting without diarrhoea
- Headache
- Marked lethargy
- Breathlessness

#### Principles of Fluid Management- Medication, rehydration + Maintenance therapy

- ![](notes/images/paediatrics-clinical-practice-guidelines-anu/img004.jpg)
Fluid replacement calculations: Normal saline + 2.5% glucose
- Rehydration:

- Do not rehydrate &gt;5% in &lt;24h
- Rehydration volume (mL) = Weight (kg) x Estimated % dehydration x 10

| Body Weight | Fluid requirement mL/day | Fluid requirement mL/hour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 10 kg | 100 mL/kg | 4 mL/kg/hr |
| Second 10 kg | + 50 mL/kg | + 2 mL/kg/hr |
| Subsequent kg | + 20 mL/kg | + 1mL/kg/hr |

- Maintenance Fluid:
- Add rehydration + Maintenance volume then divide by 24 to calculate hourly rate +/- 20mmol/L KCL
- Daily Weight
- Reintroduce age-appropriate diets when vomiting settles, within the first 12-24 hours
- Hypernatraemic dehydration

- Risk of cerebral oedema, seizures, brain damage
- Correct shock- 20 mL fluid bolus of 0.9% NaCl/Hartmann’s
- Rehydrate with 0.9% NaCl + 5% glucose +/-20mmols/L KCl over 48 hours

- Hyponatraemic (&lt;130) dehydration- Use N saline and 5% glucose

**NB: **Calculations for oral rehydration therapy and nasogastric volumes are the same as IV therapy.

#### investigations

- EUC + BGL for all children with severe dehydration/commences on IVF (repeat in 6-8 hours if abnormal results initially)
- Blood culture if temperature &gt;39oC
- Urinalysis not necessary, but useful
- OBs on a regular basis

#### Discharge Criteria

- Diagnosis of gastroenteritis
- Child is rehydrated or only mildly dehydrated
- GI loss not profuse
- Child passed urine in ED or within last 4 hours
- Parent able to give ORS appropriately and take to GP R/V within 48 hours. (Parent information sheet- What is gastroenteritis, examples of appropriate fluids, how to give oral fluids, when to seek help, follow-up arrangements)

- BRONCHIOLITIS

#### Overview

- LRTI which produces small airway obstruction with air trapping and respiratory difficulty, in mostly infants &lt; 12 months
- Usually self-limiting (7-10 days), often requiring no treatment
- Aetiology: RSV (80%) most common cause, influenza, parainfluenza, adenovirus, rhinovirus

#### presentation

- PathophysiologyOedema and accumulation of mucous/cellular debris within airway lumen
- Impaired clearance
- Variable bronchiolar obstruction (patchy hyperinflation, areas of atelectasis)
- VQ mismatch (± hypoxaemia and/or hypercarbia)

- Most cases occur between late autumn and early spring, with sporadic cases any time
- Cross-infection- Common,
- Diagnosis: Clinical diagnosis but a NPA may be used to identify the virus to aid cohorting patients
- Clinical featuresNasal obstruction +/- rhinorrhoea, irritating cough
- Auscultatory signs- fine inspiratory crackles (heard early, and become coarser during recovery), expiratory wheeze (high-pitched, prolong expiration)
- After 1-3 days, increasing tachypnoea, respiratory distress, over-expanded chest
- Fever of 38.5oC (seen in 50%)
- Apnoea- usually presenting feature, especially in premature, very young or low-birthweight infants
- An infant &lt;12 months- cough, coryzal, respiratory distress, crackles, wheeze, hyperinflation, fever, feeding difficulties

- Differentials- Can be excluded via an accurate history, physical examination and a chest x-rayViral-induced asthma
- Pertussis
- Pneumonia- Complication
- CCF
- Pneumothorax
- Bronchial foreign body

- Risk Factors for Severe Disease- rapid deterioration and increased need for extra O2 and ventilator assistance. Signs of deterioration include  ↑ increasing WOB, fatigue, difficulty feeding, apnoea, and central cyanosis (late sign)Chronic lung disease (e.g. bronchopulmonary dysplasia, congenital lung abnormalities)
- &lt; 3 months old/prematurity/SGA (due to smaller airway diameter)
- Heart disease (e.g. left to right shunts)
- History of significant apnoea, dehydration, hypoxemia, central cyanosis
- Representation within 24 hours of initial assessment

#### treatment

Supplemental O2­, Adequate Fluids, Careful Observation

**Oxygen can be given by:**

- Nasal prongs (mild bronchiolitis, recovery phase)- max flow 2L/min
- Simple facemask- 6-8 L/min
- Head box- ≥ 10L/min
- Warmed, humidified high flow nasal cannula therapy
- High flow O2 1ml/kg/min
- Saturations &gt;92% in room air is acceptable if no clinical signs of respiratory distress and feeding well

#### Discharge criteria

Minimal respiratory distress, feeding well, SaO2 &gt; 92%, parental education and when to re-present, follow-up arrangements
