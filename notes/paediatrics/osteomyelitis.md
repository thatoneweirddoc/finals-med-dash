## Osteomyelitis


### Paediatrics (ANU) — Osteomyelitis

**
![](notes/images/crs-6-child-with-a-limp-anu/img003.jpg)
**

- Infection localised to bone; usually caused by microorganisms that enter bone hematogenously
- Other pathogenic mechanisms include direct inoculation (traumatic/surgical), or local invasion from a contiguous infection (e.g. cellulitis, sinusitis, periodontal disease).
- Risk Factors- open fractures requiring surgery, orthopaedic implants, complicated delivery, prematurity, skin infection urinary tract anomalies, immunodeficiency, sepsis, functional asplenism in sickle cell disease
- Most Common OrganismsChildren: Staph aureus (most common), streptococcus pneumonia and streptococcus pyogenes
- Neonates: Gram negative bacteria and group B streptococcus

- Clinical Features- usually insidious onset; acutely painful, immobile limb; focal swelling/inflammation (not usually obvious); note if adjacent joint is affected (assess risk of SA)
- Ddx- Cellulitis, subcutaneous abscess, fracture, bone tumour
- InvestigationsLaboratory: FBC (↑WCC), ESR, CRP, blood cultures (positive) and bone aspirate cultures, bone biopsy (? bone tumour)
- Radiologic: XR (rule out fracture, may see soft tissue swelling), MRI is now the most sensitive modality for detecting OM (subperiosteal pus and purulent debris in bone), bone scan (if unclear infection site)

- ManagementImmediate empiric antibiotic therapy to prevent bone necrosis, chronic infection, limb deformity, amyloidosisCefotaxime + Vancomycin (for MRSA) in &lt;3 months
- IV for a few days (7-10) followed by 4-6 weeks orally until CRP is normal

- If no significant improvement over 48-72 hours, consider draining bone of pus, surgical debridement
- Splint limb and rest
