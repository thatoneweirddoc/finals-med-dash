## Bacterial Meningitis


### Paediatrics (ANU) — Bacterial Meningitis

#### key learning outcomes

- Clinical presentations of bacterial meningitis
- Principles of assessment with suspected meningitis
- Initial management
- Significance of early diagnosis and treatment
- Use of antibiotics and steroids
- Issues to consider in admission and/or transfer of children with meningitis
- Psychosocial implications and care of the family in the care of children with bacterial meningitis

#### outline

- Acute bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency, and has a high mortality and morbidity
- Suspected in any child presenting with sepsis or altered mental status
- 90% of children with meningitis are &lt;5/y

#### management

![](notes/images/paediatrics-clinical-practice-guidelines-anu/img003.jpg)

- ABCDEFG

- Airway
- Breathing (supplemental O2)
- Circulation (Fluid resus in shock, Fluid restriction in SIADH))
- Disability (AVPU- Alert, Voice response, Pain response, Unresponsive [can give mannitol if signs of cerebral oedema])
- Exposure (rash assessment, environmental control)
- Fluid (input, output, EUCS)- Give N Saline 20ml/kg
- Glucose

- Seizures- treated immediately with a rapid injection of benzodiazepine, and phenytoin if child continues to seizure
- Lumbar puncture for CSF Analysis once meningitis is suspected and there are no contraindications

- If there are reasons to delay LP however, antibiotics should be given prior to LP.
- Antibiotics may sterilize the CSF within 1 hour in meningococcal meningitis and within 4 hours in pneumococcal meningitis

- Initiation of empiric antibiotic therapy is high priority (IV/IO/IM route).
- Steroid Therapy for children &gt; 3 months who have not been pre-treated with antibiotics

- Reduces the risk of hearing loss and neurological sequelae
- Given as a push of Dexamethasone, 0.15 mg/kg/dose (max dose 10 mg), IV, 6 hourly for 4 days

##### medical management- empiric antiobiotic therapy

- Neonates-3 monthsGBS, E.Coli, Listeria monocytogenes
- Ampicillin (/Benzylpennicilin) plus Cefotaxime (Do not use ceftriaxone)

- ≥ 3 monthsNeisseria meningitides (meningococcus), Hib (rare with vaccine)
- Cefotaxime or Ceftriaxone (don’t administer concurrently with Ca2+ containing fluids)

- If Streptococcus Pneumoniae (any age): Vancomycin is added + third generation cephalosporin
- ?HSV- add Acyclovir 20mg/kg tds IVI
- Note: in penicillin anaphylaxis 🡪 moxifloxacin (10mg/kg/dose IV 24 h, max dose 400mg) or ciprofloxacin (10mg/kg/dose IV 12 hourly, max dose 400mg) + vancomycin

##### infection control

- Use PPE
- Direct antibiotics once culture results available; duration of treatment 7-21 days, family chemoprophylaxis
- Ongoing monitoring of ABCDEFs, vitals, GCS; daily HC in infants
- If CSF results are not meningitis but child improving,  stop steroids, and continue antibiotics until negative culture confirmed
- Notification to local public health unit

#### clinical presentation and complications

- Depends onAge (~90% of bacterial meningitis occurs at age &lt; 5 years)
- Duration of illness
- Response to infection
- Infecting organism
- Vaccination history
- Drug allergies
- Prior antibiotic use- Presents with &lt;38.5oC, frequent vomiting, less alterations in LOC, decreased rate of positive CSF/Gram stain, reversed relationship between polymorphonuclear cells and lymphocytes in CSF
- Predisposing factors e.g. recent infections, sick contact, travel, head trauma, cranial surgery, maternal obstetric history in &lt;3 months (especially GBS status), Meningococcal strain B not covered by immunisation

- Presentation can be insidious (few days) or acute/fulminant (few hours)
- Meningitis should be considered in any child with: An unexplained fever
- Seizures with fevers
- Preceding history of URTI (75% of cases)
- Maculopapular, petechial or purpuric rashes in meningococcal meningitis/septicemia  (Neisseria meningitis, HIB, Strep Pneumoniae)

- 0-3 months (non-specific)- fever/hypothermia, bulging fontanelle, acute increase in HC, irritability, high pitched cry, lethargy, altered LOC, seizures, apnea, poor feeding, vomiting (Differential- HSV CNS infection)
- &gt; 3 months (more specific)-  fever (50%), neck stiffness (useful &gt; 3y/o), bulging fontanelle, acute increase in HC, Kernig’s sign, Brudzinki’s sign, Irritability, Lethargy, Altered LOC, Anorexia, Nausea, Vomiting, Photophobia, Seizures (20-30%), preceding URTI common (75%)
- Papilledema is rare and suggests complications such as venous sinus thrombosis, abscess, subdural empyema

##### Viral Meningitis

- 80-90% due to non-polio enteroviruses, mumps, HSV, EBV, VZV, measles, influenza
- Presentation: acute onset (macular rash = enterovirus) (parotid or mandibular swellings = mumps)
- Management: benign condition; symptomatic treatment only; complete recovery expected within a 7-10 days

##### Tuberculous Meningitis

- Presentation: gradual onset progressing over 1-2 weeks to drowsiness, neck stiffness, seizures 🡪 coma
- Management: isoniazid + rifampicin + pyrazinamide + steroids

#### Complications, prognosis, follow-up

- Complications: Septic shock, Disseminated intravascular coagulopathy, Purpura fulminans, Waterhouse-Friderichsen Syndrome (adrenal gland failure due to bleeding), Cerebral herniation, Neurogenic pulmonary oedema (rare), Seizures, Cerebral oedema
- Prognosis: 83% uncomplicated recovery, 2-5% mortality; 10% deafness (intellectual/behavioural disability, spasticity, seizures or hydrocephalus)
- Follow-up: hearing testing at 6-8 weeks for ALL children with meningitis
