## Croup/Laryngotracheobronchitis


### Paediatrics (ANU) — Croup/Laryngotracheobronchitis

#### KEY LEARNING OUTCOMES

- Severity of Croup
- Steroids in moderate/severe croup
- Indications for nebulised adrenaline
- Indications for O2 in management
- Monitoring requirements
- Issues to consider when discharging children with croup from ED

#### OVERVIEW

- Clinical syndrome- hoarse voice, barking cough, inspiratory stridor
- Aetiology Viral- Commonly parainfluenza (1/2)/RSV; other causes influenza A/B, rhinovirus, adenovirus, enterovirus
- Usually mild and self-limiting, usually affecting children between 6-36 months
- Viral croup- develops over days, concurrent coryzal illness, symptoms of airway obstruction disappear over 3-5 days
- Spasmodic coup- more common in atopic, older children, comes on rapidly overnight (wakes up with cough + resp distress), shorter clinical course

#### DIFFERENTIALS FOR UPPER AIRWAY OBSTRUCTION

- SupraglotticAcute tonsillar enlargement (bacterial/EBV)
- Epiglottitis (rare)
- Retropharyngeal abscess
- Foreign body
- Acute angioedema

- Laryngeal/Subglottic Viral group
- Spasmodic croup
- Laryngomalacia +/- viral infection
- Bacterial tracheitis
- Foreign body
- Diphtheria
- Thermal/chemical injury
- Intubation trauma
- Laryngospasm (neural, hypocalcemia, associated with reflux)
- Subglottic haemangioma in first 6 months- also responds to corticosteroids

- Tracheal Trauma (hematoma)
- Foreign body
- Bacterial tracheitis
- Congenital abnormality
- Tumour (anterior mediastinal lymphoma)

- Children &lt;3 months are more likely to have a structural airway problem (e.g. laryngomalacia) +/- concurrent viral infection
- Bronchial Foreign Body (1-3 years)- localised expiratory wheeze, evidence of air trapping below level of obstruction on CXR
- Inspiratory + Expiratory stridor 🡪likelihood of fixed tracheal obstruction e.g. subglottic stenosis/retropharyngeal abscess
- Toxicity (pale, febrile, poor perfusion) 🡪 unlikely croup 🡪likelihood of bacterial tracheitis (staph aureus)/epiglottitis (HiB)

#### NEED FOR TREATMENT

- General appearance- agitation, fatigue from WOB, decreasing LOC, cyanosis/extreme pallor
- Respiratory distress- stridor at rest, tracheal tug, retractions, changing RR AND HR, palpable paradox
- Oxygen desaturation &lt;90%- late sign and hence unreliable indicator of croup severity
- Factors that contribute to hospitalisationHx of severe obstruction prior to presentation
- History of previous severe croup or known structural airway anomaly
- Age &lt; 6 months
- Degree of respiratory distress
- Fluid intake
- Parental anxiety
- Home to hospital proximity
- Representation within 24 hours
- Poor response to initial treatment
- Uncertain diagnosis

- Croup MedicationsO2- In severe croup (SaO2 &lt; 92%) with central cyanosis
- Nebulised adrenaline 1:1000- 0.5 mls of 1:1000/kg, maximum 5 mls, acts within minutes and lasts 2 hours, may need a repeat dose if severe croup. Contraindicated in ventricular outflow tract obstruction (e.g. tetralogy of fallot)
- Systemic corticosteroids-- Dexamethasone (0.15-0.3 mg/kg) or Prednisolone 1mg/kg oral/IM, repeat at 12-24 hours
- Nebulised corticosteroids- Budesonide (2-4 mg nebulised, effect starts within 30 minutes)

- Frequent monitoring of ABCs- respiratory rate and effort, colour, vital signs, general responsiveness/behaviour, level of fatigue and oxygen saturation (late sign)
- Chest X-Ray- Not indicated unless uncertain about diagnosis e.g. due to additional findings like crackles/wheeze

- FEVER

#### KEY LEARNING OUTCOMES

- Principles of fever management
- Management of febrile infants &lt; 3 months, with toxicity, without focus of infection
- Admission criteria
- Discharge and Follow-up

#### Overview

- Fever- Rise in temperature, above normal, allowing for diurnal variation.
- Defined as rectal temperature &gt; 38oC (Gold Standard)Two standard deviations above the mean for infants &lt; 3 months
- Tympanic &gt; 38oC (unreliable), Oral &gt; 37.5oC, Axilla &gt; 37oC (appropriate measure)

- Serious causes- meningitis, pneumonia, pyelonephritis, sepsis (children can have no or minimal fever)
- In the assessment of children with fever- consider child’s age, signs of toxicity, focus on infection
- Prolonged fever: Fever lasting longer than expected (i.e. 10 days for viral infection), Unexplained fever &gt; 7 days duration
- Complications: febrile convulsions (infants &lt;2 years), occult bacteremia (strep pneumoniae)

#### aetiology

- Respiratory – URTI (rhinitis, pharyngitis, sinusitis), LRTI (pneumonia, bronchitis, bronchiectasis, FB)
- Bone – osteomyelitis, septic arthritis
- Urinary – cystitis, pyelonephritis
- CNS – meningitis, encephalitis
- Abdomen – abscess, appendicitis, gastroenteritis
- Other – endocarditis, otitis media
- Generalised – septicaemia, EBV, CMV, TB, toxoplasmosis, leptospirosis, Q fever, syphilis
- Autoimmune, Connective tissue disease, Drug reaction, malignant disease, metabolic diseases
- Pyrexia of unknown origin- (idiopathic fever &gt; 2 weeks
- Low grade prolonged fever common with viral infections, inflammatory/vasculitis disorders and malignancies

#### treatment

- AntipyreticsParacetamol- 15 mg/kg/dose four-hourly up to a maximum of 4 doses/24 hours
- Ibuprofen- Not recommended for children &lt; 6 months, 10 mg/kg/dose, maximum of 4 doses/ 24 hours
- Alternative them is theoretically unwise

- Empirical antibiotic therapy- reserved for children with significant toxicity, high risk of bacterial infection (lab parameters)IV Antibiotics for children 0-4 weeks

- Tepid sponging/unwrapping an over-dressed child- may be counterproductive
- Follow-up depends on diagnosis

10. HEAD INJURY (FIRST 24 HOURS)

#### key learning objectives

- Principles of assessment of children with head injury
- GCS and modified GCS in assessment
- Management according to risk
- Role and indications for CT scan
- Indicators of NAI
- D/C Criteria and Issues

#### overview

- Most frequent reason for ED visits (1-2%)
- Consider trauma (sharp/blunt/MVA) and NAI (in inconsistent histories)
- Patterns of head injuryDecreased LOC- hard to obtain in children
- Vomiting after
- Acute brain swelling- following blunt trauma
- GCS
- Seizure-Haemorrhage- head represents 18% of body’s surface area in children  high risk of life-threatening haemorrhage

#### assessment

- Glasgow Coma Scale- Modified in childrenEye opening/4
- Modified verbal response/5- assesses irritability, consolable when crying, using smiles/words
- Movement or motor response/6

- Severe head injury- GCS &lt;9, unequal pupils, unequal motor response, open head injury (CSF leak/exposed brain tissue)
