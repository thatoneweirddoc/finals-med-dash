## Metabolic Bone Disease


### MSK & Rheumatology Notes — Metabolic Bone Disease

*(MSK/fragility-fracture angle — the calcium-axis biochemistry lives in the endocrine notes.)*

#### 7.1 Osteoporosis

**Reduced bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration → fragility fractures.** Bone **quantity** falls but **quality (mineralisation) is normal** — contrast with osteomalacia below.

**A fragility fracture = a fracture from a fall from standing height or less** (classic sites: **vertebrae, hip/NOF, distal radius/wrist, proximal humerus**). ⚑ *Any fragility fracture in an older adult should trigger osteoporosis assessment, whatever the DEXA.*

**Risk factors — "SHATTERED FAMILY":** **S**teroids, **H**yperthyroid/hyperparathyroid, **A**lcohol/smoking, **T**hin (low BMI), **T**estosterone low, **E**arly menopause, **R**enal/liver failure, **E**rosive/inflammatory disease (RA), **D**iabetes/**D**ietary calcium low; plus age, female, family history, prior fracture.

**Diagnosis:** **DEXA** scan → **T-score** (SD vs young adult):

| T-score | Category |
|---|---|
| ≥ −1.0 | Normal |
| −1.0 to −2.5 | **Osteopenia** |
| **≤ −2.5** | **Osteoporosis** |
| ≤ −2.5 + fragility fracture | Established (severe) osteoporosis |

**FRAX** (or QFracture) estimates **10-year fracture probability** to guide who to treat/scan.
**Bloods are typically normal** (Ca, PO₄, ALP all normal) — abnormal bone biochemistry points elsewhere (osteomalacia, Paget's, myeloma, hyperparathyroidism).

**Management:** lifestyle (weight-bearing exercise, stop smoking, reduce alcohol, calcium/vitamin D repletion, falls prevention). **First-line pharmacological: oral bisphosphonate (alendronate/risedronate)**. ⚑ Anabolic options for severe/very-high-risk disease: **romosozumab** (anti-sclerostin, builds bone) and **teriparatide** (PTH analogue); **denosumab** (anti-RANKL) as an alternative — ⚑ **do not stop denosumab abruptly** (rebound vertebral fractures — transition to a bisphosphonate). HRT/raloxifene in selected women. (Drug detail in Section 8.3.)

#### 7.2 Osteomalacia & rickets

**Defective mineralisation of osteoid** (soft bones), usually from **vitamin D deficiency** (or hypophosphataemia). **Rickets** is the same process in the growing child (before physeal fusion).
- **Clinical:** **bone pain and tenderness, proximal myopathy (waddling gait), fragility fractures**; children → bowed legs, costochondral "rickety rosary", growth failure.
- **Biochemistry (contrast to osteoporosis):** **low vitamin D, low/normal calcium, low phosphate, HIGH ALP, high PTH (secondary hyperparathyroidism)**.
- **Radiology:** **Looser's zones (pseudofractures)** — the buzz finding.
- **Management:** vitamin D (± calcium) replacement; treat the cause.

#### 7.3 Paget's disease of bone

**Disordered, accelerated bone turnover** (overactive osteoclasts then chaotic osteoblasts) → structurally weak, enlarged, deformed bone. Older patients; often **asymptomatic, found via isolated raised ALP**.
- **Clinical:** **bone pain, deformity (bowed tibia, skull enlargement — "hat no longer fits"), warmth over bone, fractures, deafness** (VIII compression), **secondary OA**; rarely **high-output cardiac failure** and **osteosarcoma** (sudden pain increase).
- **Biochemistry:** ⚑ **isolated markedly raised ALP with NORMAL calcium and phosphate** (the classic pattern). X-ray/bone scan: lytic + sclerotic, cortical thickening.
- **Management:** **bisphosphonates** (esp. IV zoledronate) for pain/active disease; analgesia.

#### 7.4 Osteonecrosis (avascular necrosis, AVN)

**Death of bone from interrupted blood supply.** Sites/causes to know:
- **Femoral head** (after **displaced intracapsular NOF fracture**, steroids, alcohol, **sickle cell disease**, SLE, the "bends"), **scaphoid proximal pole**, **talus**, humeral head, femoral condyles.
- **Investigation:** X-ray may be normal early → **MRI is the most sensitive test**; later X-ray shows sclerosis, subchondral collapse ("crescent sign").
- ⚑ **Bisphosphonate/denosumab-related osteonecrosis of the jaw** and atypical femoral fractures — a drug-related form to be aware of when counselling.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators — Metabolic Bone

| Clue / question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Fragility fracture + T-score ≤ −2.5, **normal Ca/PO₄/ALP** | **Osteoporosis** |
| Bone pain + proximal myopathy + **low Ca/PO₄, high ALP/PTH**, Looser's zones | **Osteomalacia** |
| **Isolated raised ALP, normal Ca/PO₄**, bowed tibia, deafness | **Paget's disease** |
| Hip/scaphoid/talus pain, X-ray normal, MRI shows necrosis | **Avascular necrosis** |
| First-line osteoporosis drug / anabolic options | **Bisphosphonate** / romosozumab, teriparatide ⚑ |
| Drug you must not stop abruptly (rebound fractures) | **Denosumab** ⚑ |

### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — Embryology & Bone Growth — Metabolic Bone Disease

- Endochondral ossification occurs at embryological at week 8 of 40 and creates woven bone
- Woven bone is highly cellular, rapidly and randomly growing → gradually replaced by lamellar bone → mature skeleton
- Embryonic somatic mesoderm → mesenchyme, in week 5 there is chondrification of mesenchymal cells to chondrocytes
- Chondroblasts secrete cartilage and the perichondrium develops around the bone → cartilage proliferates → cellular hypertrophy and maturation → calcification and cartilage degeneration by osteoblasts → ossification of the primary and secondary growth centres

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — fracture healing — Metabolic Bone Disease

Fracture is the disruption of the continuity of the bone, (long bone is more common but flat bone is more severe).

Early stage: disruption of the bone and vessels 🡪 hematoma 🡪 organization of capillaries and fibroblasts 🡪 granulation tissue

Inflammation cells neutrophils and macrophages remove hematoma and tissue debris.

Intermediate: formation of a callus – new blood vessels, fibrous tissue, cartilage and woven bone.

Osteoblast cells form and secrete osteoid 🡪 mineralization 🡪 woven bone

Late stage: remodeling: completion of ossification and remodeling of dead bone by osteoclast resorption and osteoblast secretion 🡪 formation of trabecular bone.

![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img006.jpg)

#### factors that promotes healing

Growth hormone, thyroid hormone, calcitonin, insulin anabolic hormones, vitd, vitc, exercise weight bearing, youth

#### Factors that inhibit healing:

NSAIDS steroid, DM, anaemia & decreased oxygenation, vit d deficiency, excessive bone gap or motion, infection, osteoporosis, pagets, advanced age

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — Fractures — Metabolic Bone Disease

#### Integrity of skin/soft tissue:

- closed: skin/soft tissue over and near fracture is intact
- open: skin/soft tissue over and near fracture is lacerated or abraded, fracture exposed to outside environment Signs: continuous bleeding from puncture site or fat droplets in blood are suggestive of an open fracture

#### Location

- epiphyseal: end of bone, forming part of the adjacent joint
- metaphyseal: the flared portion of the bone at the ends of the shaft
- diaphyseal: the shaft of a long bone (proximal, middle, distal)
- physis: growth plate

#### Orientation/fracture pattern:

- transverse: fracture line perpendicular to long axis of bone; result of direct high energy force
- oblique: angular fracture line; result of angular or rotational force
- butterfly: fracture site fragment which looks like a butterfly
- segmental: a separate segment of bone bordered by fracture lines; result of high energy force
- spiral: complex, multi-planar fracture line; result of rotational force, low energy
- comminuted/multi-fragmentary: >2 fracture fragments
- intra-articular: fracture line crosses articular cartilage and enters joint
- avulsion: tendon or ligament tears/pulls off bone fragment; often in children, high energy
- compression/impacted: impaction of bone; typical sites are vertebrae or proximal tibia
- torus: a buckle fracture of one cortex, often in children 
![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img008.jpg)
- greenstick: an incomplete fracture of one cortex, often in children
- pathologic: fracture through bone weakened by disease/tumor

#### Displacement

- non-displaced: fracture fragments are in anatomic alignment
- displaced: fracture fragments are not in anatomic alignment
- distracted: fracture fragments are separated by a gap (opposite of impacted)
- impacted: fracture fragments are compressed, resulting in shortened bone
- angulated: direction of fracture apex, e.g. Varus/valgus
- translated/shifted: percentage of overlapping bone at fracture site
- rotated: fracture fragment rotated about long axis of bone

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — FRACTURE DESCRIPTION — Metabolic Bone Disease

- Often fractures can only be seen in one view, so its important to have both an AP and lateral view
- If a fracture is found – obtain images of joint above and below to rule out any associated fractures or dislocations

- Identification: name, MRN, age, type of study
- Bone: look for any abnormal black lines
- Articulation: look for any disruption of the cortex – especially intra-articular
- Joint: look for narrowing/widening of joint space
- Soft tissue: look for swelling (soft tissue enlargement) or evidence of displaced fat pads

Description:

- Area – which bones/anatomical part
- View – AP/lateral
- Location – bone fracture. Part of bone. Intra-articular/extra-articular
- Pattern – Simple: broken into two pieces
- Comminuted: broken into more then two pieces
- Compound: fracture extends through skin or adjacent organs

- Displacement- Translation:  decreased contact between the fracture surfaces (lateral, medial, anterior or posterior translation).
- Angulation: the bony fragments form an angle (apex lateral, apex medial, apex anterior and apex posterior).
- Rotated: the distal bony fragment is rotated in relation to the proximal one (external, internal rotation)
- Impaction: a bony fragment has been driven into an other
- Dislocation: misalignment of articulating surfaces of a joint

- Type: pathological or stress
- Evaluation of healing Clinical: no longer tender to palpation or stressing on physical exam
- X-ray: trabeculae cross fracture site, visible callus bridging site on at least 3 of 4 cortices

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — fracture management — Metabolic Bone Disease

- ABC/Primary survey /Secondary survey (rule out other fractures/injuries)
- AMPLE History (Allergies, medications, past medical history, last meal, events surrounding injury)
- Analgesia: adequate pain relied and muscle relaxant if required
- Antibiotics must in open fractures Broad spectrum

- X-Ray: gold standard for fractures
- Fracture and joint above and below
- CT/MRI if there is evidence of ligament damage
- Ultrasound if there is suspicion for tendon damage, foreign body

#### Reduction

- Principle: restore length, rotation and angulation
- ClosedApply traction in the long axis of limb, reverse the mechanism which caused the fracture
- Reduce with iv sedation and muscle relaxation

- Indications for open reduction: “no cast” Non-union
- Open fracture,
- neurovascular Compression
- intra-Articular fracture,
- Salter-harris - 3,4,5
- polyTrauma
- Others: failure of closed reduction, position (e.g. Hip), pathological fracture

- Always re-check neurovascular integrity after reduction, and obtain post-reduction x-ray
- Maintain Reduction
- External stabilization
- Splints/casts: pad boney prominenceIf swollen or in a circumferential fracture use front and back slabs instead of a cast to avoid cutting off circulation/compartment’s syndrome

- Traction: (traction by gravity 🡪 sling)
- Functional bracing: prevents post-traumatic stiffness by changing from cast to functional brace
- External fixation:Used when there are severe soft tissue injuries or open fractures – to reduce likelihood of infection
- Temporizing e.g. in military situations
- leg lengthening

- Internal Fixation: percutaneous pinning, extra-medullary fixation (screws, plates, wires), IM fixation (rods)

#### Splinting

**Aim**

- Reduce pain
- Prevent further damage to skin, soft tissue, blood vessels and nerve
- Facilitate healing and bone union

**Method**

- Splint in a method that will reduce the fracture, and respect soft tissue
- Obtain an x-ray post-reduction

**Contra-indications**

- Infection
- Impending compartment syndrome
- Diabetic or other neuropathy
- Open fracture

**Complications**

- Plaster burn – plaster formation is an exothermic procedure
- Thermal injury – avoid >10 ply cast & >24 degree water
- Pressure sores – formed if there is insufficient padding
- Pressure necrosis – as early as 2 hours post cast
- Nerve palsy – direct compression; avoided by sufficient padding
- Vascular compression
- Splint dermatitis
- Permanent joint stiffness

#### Surgical

- Arthroplasty
- Fusion: stops movement and destroys joint, but no pain
- Osteotomy: change weight bearing portion of joint Wedge removal: changes alignment

- Excision or amputation
- Follow up to evaluate bone healing
- Rehabilitate to regain function and avoid joint stiffness

- Compartment syndrome – see below

- Hip dislocation 🡪 sciatic

- Post-traumatic OA
- Joint instability
- Septic arthritis
- Heterotrophic ossification – the formation of bone in abnormal locations e.g. Muscle, secondary to pathology
- Fracture blister – formation of vesicles or bullae that occur on edematous skin overlying fractured bone
- Muscle contracture following ischemia to muscles
- Tendon rupture
- Delayed union, malunion or non-union Nutrition, high energy injury impacts soft tissues
- Immobility
- Scaphoid fractures common to non-union

#### ankle fracture: WEBBER CLASSIFICATION

Type A (infra-syndesmotic)
![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img010.jpg)

- pure inversion injury
- avulsion of lateral malleolus below plafond or torn calcaneofibular ligament
- ± shear fracture of medial malleolus

Type b (trans-syndesmotic)

- external rotation and eversion (most common)
- ± avulsion of medial malleolus or rupture of deltoid ligament
- spiral fracture of lateral malleolus starting at plafond

Type c (supra-syndesmotic)

- pure external rotation
- avulsion of medial malleolus or torn deltoid ligament
- ± posterior malleolus avulsion with posterior tibio-fibular ligament
- fibular fracture is above plafond (called maisonneuve fracture if at proximal fibula)
- frequently tears syndesmosis
