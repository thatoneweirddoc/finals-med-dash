### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — Regulation — Additional Notes

Fracture is the disruption of the continuity of the bone, (long bone is more common but flat bone is more severe). Three phases in fracture healing:

- INFLAMMATORY PHASE
- Swelling and hyperaemia
- Organization of fracture haematoma
- Protein growth factors that regulate cell migration and differentiation
- REPARATIVE PHASE
- Primary callus response
- Independent of mechanical factors – dependent on tissue vascularity
- If it unites the two sides it progresses to bridging or hard callus
- REMODELLING PHASE
- Gradual modification of the feature region under influence of mechanical loads with conversion to lamellar bone
- FACTORS THAT PROMOTES HEALING: growth hormone, thyroid hormone, calcitonin, insulin anabolic hormones, vitamin D, vitamin C, exercise weight bearing, youth
- FACTORS THAT INHIBIT HEALING: NSAIDS, steroid, DM, anaemia and decreased oxygenation, vit d deficiency, excessive bone gap or motion, infection, osteoporosis, Paget’s Disease of the Bone, advanced age

### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — Displacement — Additional Notes

- NON-DISPLACED: fracture fragments are in anatomic alignment
- DISPLACED: fracture fragments are not in anatomic alignment
- DISTRACTED: fracture fragments are separated by a gap (opposite of impacted)
- IMPACTED: fracture fragments are compressed, resulting in shortened bone
- ANGULATED: direction of fracture apex, e.g. Varus/valgus
- TRANSLATED/SHIFTED: percentage of overlapping bone at fracture site
- ROTATED: fracture fragment rotated about long axis of bone

### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — initial — Additional Notes

- ABCS
- PRIMARY AND SECONDARY SURVEY (rule out other fractures/injuries)
- HISTORY

- AMPLE (allergies, medications, past medical history, last meal, events surrounding injury)
- Neurological signs
- Past medical history: predisposing conditions that may suggest pathological fracture

- EXAMINATION

- Inspection and palpation for swelling, discolouration, and deformity
- Assessment of active and passive range of motion of suspected joints
- Neurovascular assessment:
- Hands: ulnar, radial and median nerves; radial and ulnar pulses
- Feet: saphenous, dorsolateral, deep fibular, superficial fibular nerves; dorsalis pedis and anterior tibialis pulses

- INVESTIGATIONS

- X-Ray: gold standard for fractures
- Fracture and joint above and below
- CT/MRI if there is evidence of ligament damage
- Ultrasound if there is suspicion for tendon damage, foreign bod

- SUPPORTIVE

- Analgesia: adequate pain relief and muscle relaxant if required
- Antibiotics must in open fractures (broad-spectrum)

### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — Reduction — Additional Notes

- PRINCIPLE: restore length, rotation and angulation
- CLOSED:

- Apply traction in the long axis of limb, reverse the mechanism which caused the fracture
- Reduce with iv sedation and muscle relaxation

- INDICATIONS FOR OPEN REDUCTION: “NO CAST”

- Non-union
- Open fracture,
- Neurovascular Compression
- Intra-Articular fracture,
- Salter-harris - 3,4,5
- polyTrauma
- Others: failure of closed reduction, position (e.g. hip), pathological fracture

- Always re-check neurovascular integrity after reduction, and obtain post-reduction x-ray
- MAINTAIN REDUCTION

- External stabilization
- Splints/casts: pad the boney prominance
- If swollen or in a circumferential fracture use front and back slabs instead of a cast to avoid cutting off circulation/compartment’s syndrome
- Traction: sling (by gravity)
- Functional bracing: prevents post-traumatic stiffness by changing from cast to functional brace
- External fixation:Used when there are severe soft tissue injuries or open fractures to reduce likelihood of infection
- Temporizing e.g. In military situations
- Leg lengthening

- Internal Fixation: percutaneous pinning, extra-medullary fixation (screws, plates, wires), IM fixation (rods)

### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — Splinting — Additional Notes

AIM

- Reduce pain
- Prevent further damage to skin, soft tissue, blood vessels and nerve
- Facilitate healing and bone union

METHOD

- Splint in a method that will reduce the fracture, and respect soft tissue
- Obtain an x-ray post-reduction

COMPLICATIONS

- Plaster burn:  plaster formation is an exothermic procedure
- Thermal injury: avoid >10 ply cast & >24 degree water
- Pressure sores: formed if there is insufficient padding
- Pressure necrosis: as early as 2 hours post cast
- Nerve palsy: direct compression; avoided by sufficient padding
- Vascular compression
- Splint dermatitis
- Permanent joint stiffness

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — physical rehabilitation — Additional Notes

Generally, fractures will be immobilised for 4-6weeks which may lead to stiffness and muscle weakness in the adjacent joints and muscles. Immobilised in splint, cast, brace, surgically fixated or place in traction.

- Muscle assessment: following immobilisation muscles surrounding the fracture site lose bulk, length and strength. prescribed safe exercise program under supervision of physiotherapist to restore muscle length and balance

- Joint mobilization: joint stiffness often occurs when a limb is not allowed to move for several weeks🡪 improve and restore range of movement of the affected joints once the fracture has healed.Passive: therapist moves joint to maintain ligament and joint capsule
- Active and against resistance: improve muscle power.

- Massage: the release of tight bands and trigger points that occur within muscles following splinting or casting has been shown to reduce pain and restore muscle length.
- Heat and electrotherapy: it is very common for stiffness within soft tissues to occur following prolonged immobilisation. Useful adjuncts to manual treatment and exercise therapy in relieving pain and restoring muscle length. Ice packs help provide analgesia immediately and reduce pain and swelling whereas hear relives pain and stiffness.

- Gait education: if your fracture requires the use of gait aids such as crutches then the physiotherapist can advise you with the most appropriate equipment and way of walking that promotes optimal healing and safety.
- Ergonomic training: training how to use specific body parts for specific activities – i.e. Back pain need to be taught how to lift and carry again
- Functional aids: Cane, crutches, walking aid for lower limb
- Collars or partial splintage for a painful neck
- Footwear such as can-boot

- Presents with sudden high stress impact or in chronic age related decrease in blood supply or repeated micro tears e.g. Rowing, weightlifting overhead throwing

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — Primary haemopoietic tumours — Additional Notes

These so called “blood cancers” arise from the blood cells within the bone marrow. Hence, these cancers, which include leukemia, lymphomas and myeloma, are considered to be in the Haemopoietic and lymphoid system (i.e. Distinct from the musculoskeletal system).

#### Multiple myeloma

This is a neoplastic proliferation of plasma cells (activated b-lymphocytes producing immunoglobulins). Overall, it is the commonest tumor that originates in the bone. They present as osteolytic lesions, with infiltration of various organs and associated amyloidosis.

Investigations:

- Protein electrophoresis of blood and urine shows presence of paraproteins (abnormal immunoglobulins produced by tumors 🡪 igg most common.
- Bloods may show raised creatinine
- Radiographs of skull, axial skeleton and proximal long bones
- MRI detects lytic lesions

Treatment:

- High dose chemotherapy with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — feet pathology — Additional Notes

#### Treatment of hallux valgus

Conservative – symptom relief

- Change shoes
- Orthotics [1]splints or spaces bunion toe separators / bunion cushions / bunion splints.

- Padding and strapping
- Rice
- Nsaids / corticosteroid injections

Surgical (if pain is unbearable)

May involve some of the following:

- Removing abnormal bony enlargement of 1st metatarsal
- Realigning the first metatarsal bone
- Straightening the great toe relative to the first metatarsal
- Realign the cartilaginous surfaces of the hallux

Risk factors

- Long term tight fitting shoes with pointed toes

#### Management of ingrown toenails

Conservative – mild to moderate

Warm water and salt soaks, antibacterial ointment and using dental floss or a gutter splint to provide a track along which the nail may grow is possible.

Surgery – severe or progressive mild ingrown toenails

- Partial avulsion of the lateral nail plate known as a ‘wedge resection; or a complete removal of the toenail
- If it recurs, destruction of the side of the nail bed with anesthesia is recommended.
- Topical abs used post procedure but orals are not recommended as they may delay healing

Nail bracing – less widely used

- Nail braces work by gently lifting the sides of the toenail and eventually retraining the nail to grow to a flatter shape over time. – one full growth of the nail, which is around 1-1.5 years.

Two types: adhesive and nail braces

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — Immunodeficiency — Additional Notes

- TYPES: congenital or acquired (infection, malnutrition, aging, malignancy, iatrogenic e.g. chemo)
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: individual deficiencies are rare

Arthocentesis

- DIAGNOSTIC: determine cause of pain or swelling in the joint
- THERAPEUTIC: removal of excess fluid may help lessen pain and improve antibiotic
- SYNOVIAL FLUID CHARACTERISTIC:
- Crystals: gout (urate), pseudogout (pyrophosphate)
- WCC: bacterial septic; may be slightly elevated in RA, psoaritic and reactive arthritis
- Blood: hemarthrosis from trauma or fracture
- Fat: fracture
- Non-inflammatory: OA

Hypersensivity

- HYPERSENSITIVITY: the immune system, which normally serves a protective role, has a harmful effect. The hypersensitivity reactions can be memorized with the mnemonic ACID: A – Allergic/Anaphylactic/Atopic (Type I); C – Cytotoxic (Type II); I – Immune complex deposition (Type III); D – Delayed (Type IV)
- ALLERGY: abnormal immunological response to an otherwise harmless environmental stimulus
- AUTOIMMUNITY: abnormal immunological response directed against an antigen that is actually part of the body itself

| Definition | Chronic inflammatory and pruritic skin disease that typically manifests in early child hoodOften associated with other atopic diseases e.g. asthma, allergic rhinitis |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | 10-15% of all children are affected with an age of onset between 3-6 months of ageDisease often improves with age |
| Aetiology | Incompletely understoodGENETIC: polygenic inheritance of predisposition for increased IgE formation and sensitisation in which abnormal Th2 response in the skin → cytokine overproduction → excessive IgE production → allergic dermatitisTRIGGERS: dust, heat, dry/humid climates, emotional stress, infections, skin irritation |
| Presentation | Intense pruritis and dry skinINFANTILE AD (<2 YEARS): eczema (cheeks, face, head, extensors), Dennie-Morgan fold (below eye)CHILDHOOD AD (2-12 YEARS): eczema (flexural creases, skin folds, lichenified lesions (thickened skin)ADULT/ADOLESCENT AD (> 12 YEARS): LICHENIFIED lesions and pruritis of flexor surfaces of extremities), nummular eczema (coin-shaped)ASSOCIATED: asthma, allergic rhinitis, xerosis |
| Diagnostic Criteria | Essential: pruritis, eczema (maculopapular, erythematous lesions that can also have scale, crusting, oozing, or blistering)Important features: early age of onset, atopy, xerosis |
| Differentials | Seborrheic dermatitis (usually more greasy lesions), psoriasis (onset in adolescent years) |

Autoimmunity

DEFINITION: Immune reaction against the body’s own cells, due to a loss of immunological tolerance

PRESUMED PATHOGENESIS

- Autoreactive B lymphocytes are eliminated in the bone marrow, spleen, or lymph nodes (can be detected)
- T lymphocytes that attack the body's own cells are amended in the thymus or destroyed in peripheral lymphoid tissues (e.g., lymph nodes, adenoids, Peyer's patches) due to a lack of stimulation (usually cannot detect specific antibodies)
- If the selection mechanisms fail, this results in the immune cells attacking the body's own cells

CAUSES

- Previous infection e.g. GBS, RF through molecular mimicry
- Genetic predisposition

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — Introduction — Additional Notes

- Heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases, all characterized by inflammation of blood vessels (vasculitis) and subsequent ischemia and damage to the organs supplied by these vessels.
- PRIMARY: idiopathic
- SECONDARY: response to infection e.g. hepatitis B, hepatitis C
