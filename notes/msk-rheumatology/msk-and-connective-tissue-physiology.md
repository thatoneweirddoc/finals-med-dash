## MSK & Connective-Tissue Physiology


### MSK & Rheumatology Notes — MSK & Connective-Tissue Physiology

##### 1.1 Bone biology and remodelling

Bone is a living composite: an **organic** matrix (mostly **type I collagen** = osteoid, giving tensile strength) mineralised by **hydroxyapatite** (calcium phosphate, giving compressive strength). Two macroscopic types:

| Type | Also called | Where | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cortical** | Compact | Shafts (diaphysis), outer shell | ~80% of skeletal mass, dense, slow turnover |
| **Trabecular** | Cancellous / spongy | Ends of long bones, vertebrae, pelvis | Metabolically active, high surface area → **fractures/osteoporosis show here first** (vertebrae, NOF) |

**The three cells (know their lineage — it explains the drugs):**

| Cell | Lineage | Job | Drug relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Osteoblast** | Mesenchymal | Lays down osteoid, then mineralises it | Stimulated by **Wnt** signalling; **teriparatide** (PTH analogue) and **romosozumab** boost this |
| **Osteoclast** | Haematopoietic (macrophage/monocyte line) | Resorbs bone | Activated by **RANKL**; blocked by **bisphosphonates** and **denosumab** |
| **Osteocyte** | Terminally differentiated osteoblast, buried in matrix | Mechanosensor; secretes **sclerostin** | Sclerostin inhibits bone formation → **romosozumab** is an anti-sclerostin antibody |

**The RANKL–RANK–OPG axis** — the single most exam-relevant piece of bone physiology:
- Osteoblasts express **RANKL**, which binds **RANK** on osteoclast precursors → osteoclast activation → resorption.
- Osteoblasts also secrete **OPG (osteoprotegerin)**, a decoy receptor that mops up RANKL → protects bone.
- **Memory hook:** *RANKL Ruins bone, OPG is On your side (Protects).* **Denosumab is a monoclonal anti-RANKL antibody** — it mimics OPG.

**Calcium/phosphate & the bone loop** (cross-links to endocrine): PTH raises calcium (bone resorption, renal Ca reabsorption, activates vitamin D); **active vitamin D (calcitriol)** raises calcium and phosphate by gut absorption; calcitonin lowers calcium (minor in humans). This loop underlies osteomalacia, renal bone disease and hyperparathyroid bone disease (Section 6 / endocrine notes).

**Fracture healing (four overlapping stages):**

1. **Haematoma / inflammation** (hours–days) — clot, cytokines, inflammatory cells.
2. **Soft callus** (days–weeks) — fibrocartilage bridges the gap.
3. **Hard callus** (weeks) — woven bone replaces cartilage (endochondral ossification).
4. **Remodelling** (months–years) — woven bone → lamellar bone along stress lines.

⚑ *Anything that starves any stage delays or prevents union: poor blood supply (scaphoid, NOF), movement/instability, infection, smoking, NSAIDs (theoretical), diabetes, malnutrition.*

##### 1.2 Cartilage, synovium and the joint

**Hyaline articular cartilage** covers joint surfaces: **type II collagen** + proteoglycan (aggrecan) holding water. **It is avascular, aneural and alymphatic** → nourished by synovial fluid diffusion → **poor intrinsic healing** (the reason OA is progressive and cartilage injuries don't heal well). **Memory hook:** *cartilage is "type II" and has no plumbing.*

**The synovial joint** — the RA battleground:
- **Synovium (synovial membrane)** lines the capsule (not the cartilage), secretes **synovial fluid** (hyaluronan-rich, lubricates + nourishes).
- In **RA**, the synovium becomes inflamed and hypertrophies into a **pannus** that erodes cartilage and bone → the synovium is the target.
- In **OA**, the primary problem is the **cartilage** (± secondary synovitis).

**Enthesis** = where tendon/ligament inserts into bone. **Enthesitis (inflammation here) is the hallmark of the seronegative spondyloarthropathies** — contrast with the **synovitis** of RA. **Memory hook:** *RA inflames the lining (synovitis); spondyloarthropathy inflames the anchor points (enthesitis).*

**Joint types:** synovial (freely mobile, e.g. knee), cartilaginous (e.g. intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis), fibrous (e.g. skull sutures).

##### 1.3 Muscle, tendon and connective tissue

**Skeletal muscle** contracts via the actin–myosin cross-bridge cycle triggered by Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (excitation–contraction coupling). Relevant clinically for: **raised CK** in muscle inflammation/breakdown (myositis, rhabdomyolysis), and the **proximal weakness** pattern of inflammatory myopathy.

**Tendons and ligaments** are **type I collagen** dense connective tissue. Relevant inherited connective-tissue diseases (worth recognising):

| Disease | Defect | Buzz features |
|---|---|---|
| **Marfan syndrome** | **Fibrillin-1 (FBN1)** | Tall, arachnodactyly, lens dislocation (**up**), aortic root dilatation/dissection |
| **Ehlers–Danlos** | **Collagen** (various types) | Hypermobile joints, stretchy skin, easy bruising; vascular type → arterial rupture |
| **Osteogenesis imperfecta** | **Type I collagen** | Brittle bones, **blue sclerae**, hearing loss, dentinogenesis |

##### 1.4 Immunology of rheumatic disease *(the engine of half the syllabus)*

**Seropositive vs seronegative** — the organising concept:
- **Seropositive** = rheumatoid factor / other autoantibodies present (classically RA, and the connective tissue diseases).
- **Seronegative spondyloarthropathies** = RF-negative, **HLA-B27**-associated, enthesitis-driven, axial/asymmetrical (AS, psoriatic, reactive, enteropathic).

**Key immunological mechanisms and where the drugs hit:**

| Mechanism | Disease examples | Therapeutic target |
|---|---|---|
| **Autoantibodies** (against self antigens) | RA (anti-CCP/RF), SLE (anti-dsDNA), GPA (ANCA) | B cells → **rituximab (anti-CD20)** |
| **Immune complex deposition** | SLE nephritis, IgA vasculitis, PAN (HBV) | Complement consumed → **low C3/C4 in active SLE** |
| **T-cell / cytokine driven synovitis** | RA, spondyloarthropathy | **TNF-α, IL-6, IL-17, IL-1, JAK–STAT** |
| **Complement consumption** | Active SLE | Low C3/C4 = a **flare/activity marker** |

**The cytokine → drug map** (learn this once; it unlocks the pharmacology):

| Cytokine / pathway | Blocking drug(s) | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| **TNF-α** | infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept | RA, spondyloarthropathy, IBD |
| **IL-6** | **tocilizumab** | RA, **GCA**, JIA |
| **IL-17** | secukinumab, ixekizumab | psoriatic arthritis, axSpA |
| **IL-23 / IL-12-23** | ustekinumab, guselkumab | psoriatic arthritis, psoriasis |
| **IL-1** | anakinra, canakinumab | gout flare (refractory), autoinflammatory |
| **JAK–STAT** (intracellular) | **tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib** | RA, others — ⚑ safety signal (see Section 7) |
| **B cells (CD20)** | rituximab | RA, ANCA vasculitis, SLE |
| **BLyS/BAFF** | belimumab | SLE |
| **T-cell co-stimulation** | abatacept | RA |

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators — Physiology

| Clue / question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Collagen type in bone/tendon vs cartilage | **Type I** (bone, tendon, skin) vs **type II** (hyaline cartilage) |
| Cell that resorbs bone / its master activator | **Osteoclast** / **RANKL** |
| Decoy receptor protecting bone; drug that mimics it | **OPG**; **denosumab** (anti-RANKL) |
| Osteocyte product inhibiting bone formation; its blocker | **Sclerostin**; **romosozumab** |
| Why scaphoid & femoral neck fractures risk AVN | **Retrograde blood supply** → proximal fragment devascularised |
| Why articular cartilage heals poorly | **Avascular** (nourished by synovial fluid diffusion only) |
| Synovitis vs enthesitis — which disease group | Synovitis = **RA**; enthesitis = **seronegative spondyloarthropathy** |
| Low complement (C3/C4) in a rheum patient means | Active **SLE** (immune-complex consumption) |
| Fibrillin-1 defect / type I collagen defect (brittle bones) | **Marfan** / **osteogenesis imperfecta** |

### Orthopaedics & MSK (ANU) — CELLS — MSK & Connective-Tissue Physiology

- OSTEOBLASTS: produce bone matrix, type 1 collagen and other proteins
- Receptors: PTH, Vit D, glucocorticoids, oestrogen, PGDs, ILs

- Receptors: PTH, calcitonin

- Receptors: calcitonin, estrogen, IL-1, RANK-L

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — Principles of bone growth and healing — MSK & Connective-Tissue Physiology

Bone is made of: osteoid, a matrix of type 1 collagen and proteins; minerals and cells.

Cells of bone are:

- Osteoblasts which produce collagen and bone matrix 🡪 become entrapped in the osteoid 🡪 degenerate to osteocytes
- Osteoclasts: multinucleated giant cells which phagocytose and reabsorb bone matrix

#### Embryology &amp; Bone Growth

- Endochondral ossification occurs at embryological at week 8 of 40 and creates woven bone.

Woven bone is highly cellular, rapidly and randomly growing 🡪 gradually replaced by lamellar bone 🡪 mature skeleton.

- Embryonic somatic mesoderm 🡪 mesenchyme, in week 5 there is chondrification of mesenchymal cells to chondrocytes.
- Chondroblasts secrete cartilage and the perichondrium develops around the bone 🡪 cartilage proliferates 🡪 cellular hypertrophy and maturation 🡪 calcification and cartilage degeneration by osteoblasts 🡪 ossification of the primary and secondary growth centres.

| --- |
| Bone formation ( ossification ) occurs in 3 different ways: endochondral, intramembranous, appositional |
| Endochondral | • bone replaces a cartilage anlage (template). Osteoclasts remove the cartilage, and osteoblasts make the new bone matrix, which is then mineralized.• typical in long bones (except clavicle).• primary ossification centers (in shaft) typically develop in prenatal period.• secondary ossification centers occur at various times after birth, usually in the epiphysis.• longitudinal growth at the metaphysis also occurs by endochondral ossification.• also found in fracture callus |

![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img027.jpg)

![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img028.jpg)

#### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

- Primary bone tumours are quite rare compared to bone metastases.
- Metastases:These cancers arise from other organs, which has subsequently spread to the bones (in contrast to primary tumours). Usually equates to the terminal or near-terminal phase of the cancer illness. &gt;90% come from the thyroid, breast, lungs, kidneys or prostate in adults, commonly involving the axial skeleton. Metastases are typically lytic (kidney, thyroid) or sclerotic (prostate), giving a clue to their origin on a simple x-ray.

#### Investigations

- X-ray
- Radioscintigraphy using 99mtechnetium = the most sensitive method of detecting ‘silent’ metastatic deposits in bone

#### MANAGEMENT

- Palliation and pain relief using analgesia and narcotics.
- Radiotherapy for pain relief
- Treat hypercalcemia

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — Primary bone tumours — MSK & Connective-Tissue Physiology

These are tumours that arise from the bone (excluding marrow), and are quite rare. Most of them are benign, as opposed to malignant.

#### Pathophysiology

Sarcomas of the bone have a bimodal age distribution (10’s and &gt;60), with majority arising de novo. However, recognized precursors include:

- Paget disease
- Radiation exposure
- Bone infarction
- Chronic osteomyelitis
- Genetic syndromes

#### Clinical features

The clinical features are non-specific in general, although some hints may allow for distinction between a benign vs malignant tumor. They can be broadly divided into the following: pain, swelling, limitation of movement, pathological fracture and systemic symptoms.

#### classification

- Chondroid (cartilage producing tumours)

- Benign (osteochondroma, chondroma, chondroblastoma)
- Malignant (chondrosarcoma)Investigations: radiograph – radiolucent area with central flecks of calcification 🡪 increase in size post full bone development = malignant and ct / MRI should be performed before biopsy. Large lucent area encroaching upon or expanding the cortex. Patchy calcification in the center of the lesion.
- Treatment: chemo and radiotherapy are not very effective in chondrosarcoma. Complete surgical ablation is often required with reconstruction of bone if needed.

- Osteogenic (bone-producing tumours)

- Benign (osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma)
- Malignant (osteosarcoma)Investigations: radiographic appearance – can often be misdiagnosed for benign and non-neoplastic (infectious) conditions = codman’s triangle (subperisoteal lesion formed when the periosteum is raised due to the tumor). Pet scan, bone biopsy only definitive way to determine if malignant or benign.
- Treatment: neo-adjuvant chemotherapy (dose methotrexate with leucovorin, cisplatin, adriamycin) and limb salvage surgery (if possible)

- Fibrogenic (fibrous tissue/collagen producing tumours)

- Benign (fibrous dysplasia)
- Malignant (fibrosarcoma)

- Others

- Ewing sarcoma
- Giant cell tumor of the bone
- Aneurysmal bone cysts

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — IMMUNE SYSTEM ORGANS — MSK & Connective-Tissue Physiology

- 1O organs: bone marrow (B cell maturation) and thymus (T cell maturation)
- 2o organs: spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer’s patches
- The lymph node: Encapsulated with trabeculae and functions are nonspecific filtration by macrophages, storage of B and T cells, and immune response activation. The follicle is the site of B-cell localisation/proliferation, the medulla communicates with efferent lymphatics and contains reticular cells/macrophages and the paracortex houses T cells.
