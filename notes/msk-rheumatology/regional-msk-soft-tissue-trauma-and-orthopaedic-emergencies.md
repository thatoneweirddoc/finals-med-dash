#### 4.2 Mechanical back and neck pain (and the red flags)

**Most acute low back pain is mechanical and self-limiting** — the job in an exam is to **screen for the sinister causes**. NICE (NG59) advocates staying active, avoiding routine imaging, and using the STarT Back-type risk stratification.

**Radiculopathy / sciatica:** nerve-root pain radiating down the leg in a dermatomal pattern (commonly L5/S1 from a disc prolapse); positive **straight-leg raise**. **Spinal (canal) stenosis:** older patient, **neurogenic claudication** — leg pain on walking, **relieved by leaning forward/sitting** (vs vascular claudication relieved by simply stopping).

**⚑ RED FLAGS in back pain — the "don't miss" list:**

| Red flag | Points to |
|---|---|
| **Saddle anaesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral sciatica** | **Cauda equina syndrome** (emergency MRI) |
| **Thoracic pain, new severe pain in >50s, known cancer, weight loss** | **Malignancy / metastatic cord compression** |
| **Fever, IV drug use, immunosuppression, recent bacteraemia** | **Discitis / epidural abscess / vertebral osteomyelitis** |
| **Age <20 or >55 new onset; night pain; systemic upset** | Sinister cause / spondyloarthropathy (young + inflammatory) |
| **Progressive neurological deficit, UMN signs** | **Cord compression** |
| **Significant trauma / osteoporosis / steroid use** | **Vertebral fracture** |

#### 4.3 Fracture principles and common fractures

##### Describing a fracture (the systematic OSCE answer)
Site (which bone, which part), **pattern** (transverse/oblique/spiral/comminuted/greenstick), **displacement** (translation, angulation, rotation, shortening), **intra- vs extra-articular**, **open vs closed**, and any **complications** (neurovascular, skin).

##### Management principle: **Reduce – Hold – Rehabilitate** (the "3 Rs")
Reduce (closed or open), hold (cast, splint, internal/external fixation), rehabilitate (mobilise, physio). Overarching aims: restore anatomy, achieve union, preserve function.

##### Fracture complications
- **Immediate:** neurovascular injury, haemorrhage, skin/soft-tissue.
- **Early:** compartment syndrome, infection, fat embolism, VTE.
- **Late:** **malunion, non-union, avascular necrosis** (scaphoid, femoral head, talus), stiffness, **complex regional pain syndrome**, post-traumatic OA, myositis ossificans.

##### The must-know specific fractures

| Fracture | Mechanism / clue | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Neck of femur (NOF)** | Elderly, fall, **shortened + externally rotated** leg | **Garden I–IV; intracapsular vs extracapsular determines implant** (below) |
| **Distal radius — Colles'** | FOOSH, **"dinner-fork" deformity**, dorsal displacement | Smith's = reverse (volar) |
| **Scaphoid** | FOOSH, **anatomical snuffbox tenderness** | ⚑ **AVN + non-union** (retrograde supply); if X-ray normal but tender → **immobilise & re-image / MRI** |
| **Ankle** | Inversion/eversion; Weber A/B/C (relative to syndesmosis) | Ottawa ankle rules to guide X-ray |
| **Supracondylar humerus** | **Child**, FOOSH | **Brachial artery / median (AIN) nerve** injury; check pulse |
| **Distal radius (child) / greenstick** | Incomplete cortical break | Paediatric plasticity |
| **Salter–Harris** | Physeal (growth-plate) fractures, grades I–V | **SALTR**: Slip, Above, Lower, Through, cRush |

**NOF implant logic (a classic viva):**

| Fracture | Blood-supply risk | Typical management |
|---|---|---|
| **Intracapsular, displaced** | **High AVN risk** (retrograde head supply torn) | **Replace**: hemiarthroplasty, or **total hip replacement** if fit/active/independent |
| **Intracapsular, undisplaced** | Lower | Internal fixation (cannulated screws) in selected patients |
| **Extracapsular — intertrochanteric** | Head supply intact | **Fix**: **dynamic hip screw (DHS)** |
| **Extracapsular — subtrochanteric** | — | **Intramedullary nail** |

#### 4.4 Orthopaedic & spinal emergencies *(can't-miss — highest yield in the section)*

##### Compartment syndrome ⚑
Raised pressure within a closed fascial compartment → ischaemia. Classic after **tibial fracture, forearm fracture, crush, reperfusion, tight casts**.
- **Cardinal sign: pain out of proportion to the injury, and pain on passive stretch of the muscles in the compartment.**
- ⚑ **It is a clinical diagnosis — do NOT wait for the late "6 Ps" (pulselessness/paralysis are late and signify irreversible damage).** Pulses are usually **present** early.
- **Management: remove all constricting casts/dressings, keep limb at heart level, urgent surgical fasciotomy.** Compartment pressures (or ΔP = diastolic − compartment pressure <30 mmHg) support the diagnosis but should not delay surgery.

##### Cauda equina syndrome ⚑
Compression of the lumbosacral roots below L1/L2 (usually a large central disc prolapse; also tumour, abscess, haematoma).
- **Red flags: bilateral sciatica, saddle (perineal) anaesthesia, urinary retention/overflow incontinence, faecal incontinence/loss of anal tone, reduced sexual sensation.**
- **Immediate MRI whole spine; urgent surgical decompression.** Delay → permanent bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction. **Do a PR and check post-void residual — don't be reassured by "just back pain".**

##### Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC) ⚑
Compression of the **cord** (above L1/L2) — cancer patient (breast, prostate, lung, myeloma, renal) with back pain + UMN signs, a sensory level, sphincter disturbance.
- **Urgent MRI whole spine; high-dose dexamethasone; oncology/spinal referral for radiotherapy or surgical decompression within ~24 h.** (NICE MSCC pathway.)

##### Open (compound) fracture ⚑
Skin breached over a fracture → contamination.
- **Gustilo–Anderson classification** (I–IIIC by wound size/soft-tissue/vascular injury).
- **Management (BOAST): early IV antibiotics, tetanus prophylaxis, remove gross contamination, photograph and cover with saline-soaked dressing, splint, neurovascular assessment, urgent debridement and combined ortho-plastics fixation.**

##### Septic arthritis (cross-reference — full detail in Section 5.5) ⚑
Any acute **hot, swollen, immobile joint** = septic arthritis until excluded → **aspirate before antibiotics**. Covered in the arthritides section.

##### Other acute presentations
- **Cervical spine injury:** immobilise; image (CT) per **Canadian C-spine / NEXUS** rules; beware neurogenic shock in high lesions.
- **Fat embolism syndrome:** 24–72 h after long-bone/pelvic fracture → **respiratory distress, confusion, petechial rash** (the triad). Supportive care; early fracture fixation reduces risk.
- **Dislocations** (shoulder, hip): reduce promptly; check neurovascular status before and after; hip dislocation of a prosthesis is common, native hip dislocation implies high energy + AVN risk.

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — MSK TRAUMA HISTORY AND EXAMINATION — Regional MSK, Soft Tissue, Trauma & Orthopaedic Emergencies

#### History

- Mechanism of injury
- Predisposing factors, recent trauma, medications (may cause a pathological fracture)
- Pain: where is it? Localized or generalized?, periodicity and severity
- Any associated neurological features
- General health, PHx, FHx
- Extent of disability and effect on QOL

#### physical exam

- LOOK: deformity, swelling, rash, muscle wasting, scars, signs of inflammation and symmetry
- FEEL: swelling (fluid, synovial or boney), warmth and tenderness
- MOVE: active, passive, symmetry

In practice, the major part of most orthopedic examinations is centered on the joint that troubles the patient. However, the examination must often extend to include:

- The nerves responsible for movement of that joint and anatomically close to the injured joint
- The muscles responsible for movement of that joint and anatomically close to the injured joint.
- Joints proximal and distal to the injured joint must be examined

#### Rapid examination of the upper limbs

- Raise arms sideways to the ears (abduction). Reach behind neck and back. Difficulties with these movements indicate a shoulder or rotator cuff problem.

- Hold the arms forward, with elbows straight and fingers apart, palm up and palm down. Fixed flexion at the elbow indicates an elbow problem. Examine the hands for swelling, wasting and deformity.
- Place the hands in the ‘prayer’ position with the elbows apart. Flexion deformities of the fingers may be due to arthritis, flexor tenosynovitis or skin disease. Painful restriction of the wrist limits the person’s ability to move the elbows out with the hands held together.
- Make a tight fist. Difficulty with this indicates a loss of flexion or grip. Grip strength can be measured.

Quick nerve Exam – hand:

- Thumbs up – radial nerve
- Ok sign – median nerve
- Spread fingers - ulnar nerve

#### Rapid examination of the lower limbs

- Ask the patient to walk a short distance away from and towards you, and to stand still. Look for abnormal posture or stance.
- Ask the patient to stand on each leg.
- Severe hip disease causes the pelvis on the non-weight-bearing side to sag (positive trendelenburg test).
- Watch the patient stand and sit, looking for hip and/or knee problems.
- Ask the patient to straighten and flex each knee.
- Ask the patient to place each foot in turn on the opposite knee with the hip externally rotated. This tests for painful restriction of hip or knee. Abnormal hips or knees must be examined lying.
- Move each ankle up and down. Examine the ankle joint and tendons, medial arch and toes whilst standing.

#### Rapid examination of the spine

- Stand behind the patient.
- Ask the patient to (a) bend forwards to touch the toes with straight knees, (b) extend backwards, (c) flex sideways, and (d) look over each shoulder, flexing and extending and side-flexing the neck. Observe abnormal spinal curves – scoliosis (lateral curve), kyphosis (forward bending) or lordosis (backward bending). A cervical and lumbar lordosis and a thoracic kyphosis are normal. Muscle spasm is worse whilst standing and bending. Leg length inequality leads to a scoliosis which decreases on sitting or lying (the lengths are measured lying).
- Ask the patient to lie supine. Examine any restriction of straight-leg raising (see disc prolapse, below).
- Ask the patient to lie prone. Examine for anterior thigh pain during a femoral stretch test (flexing knee whilst prone), which indicates a high lumbar disc problem.
- Palpate the spine and buttocks for tender areas.

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — SHOULDER — Regional MSK, Soft Tissue, Trauma & Orthopaedic Emergencies

- Large range of motion
- Risk: shoulder injured by trauma (indirect or direct) or over-use, decreased strength of joint capsule and its ligaments
- DDX: Tendinosis and tendinitis of the rotator cuff and proximal biceps tendon
- Hx: pain, stiffness, instability and weakness
- Exam: inspected from anterior, posterior and lateral – deformity, ecchymosis, laceration, swelling or hematoma. Palpation of the shoulder should be performed systematically.

Active and passive range of motion should be tested, along with neurovascular function.

### Orthopaedics (SNEHA) — HIP — Regional MSK, Soft Tissue, Trauma & Orthopaedic Emergencies

Hip replacement

- Total hip replacement: replaces both acetabulum and femoral head 
![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img017.jpg)
- Hemiarthroplasty: only replaces femoral head

Indication FOR HIP REPLACEMENT

- Osteoarthritis / Rheumatoid arthritis
- Severe NOF OR AVN from NOF
- Traumatic arthritis
- Benign and malignant bone tumours
- Paget’s disease
- Juvenile rheumatoid arthritis

![](notes/images/orthopaedics-sneha/img018.jpg)

#### Management:

- Rice (rest, ice, compression and elevation)
- Seek referral and further management if there was popping noise, severe pain, loss of rom, limping or swelling at site of injury
- Non-surgical treatment: Immobilization: brace or cast
- Rest: crutches
- Nsaids
- Physical therapy to restore function

- Surgical treatment: Arthroscopic repair
- Open surgery

#### Risks of joint replacement surgery

- Vein thrombosisDvt and pe are common after replacement surgery. Treat with clexane (enoxaparin) for 21 days.

- Dislocation Most common complication. It takes 8-12 weeks for the soft tissues damaged during surgery to heal and during this period dislocation can occur.

- Intraoperative damage to neurovasculature
- Infection
- Nerve damage – anterior approach and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and sciatic nerve in posterior approach.
- Anesthesia issues
- Fracture
- OsteolysisLoss of bone due to reaction to polyethylene wear debris. An inflammatory process causes bone resorption that leads to subsequent loosening of the hip implants.

- Metal sensitivitySoft tissue masses containing necrotic tissue around the hip joint from release of cations from the metal for example cobalt. – toxin reaction or hypersensitivity.

- Metal toxicityMost replacements consist of cobalt and chromium alloys or titanium. All implants release ions into the blood. These are usually excreted but in certain individuals, they can accumulate.

- Leg length inequalityOverlengthening is the most common problem. After oa it can feel longer as the oa creates shortening.
- True inequality may be caused by improper implant selection – the femoral component may be too large and stick out of the femur further than needed.

- Wearing out – 10-15 years
- Loosening – wear of the prosthetic component.

#### Post-operative care of joint replacements:

- Pain medication
- Antibiotics – usually ceftriaxone (3rd generation cephalosporin)
- Enoxoparin 40mg daily s/c
- Ted stockings or pneumatic compression thingy.
- Rehabilitation: regain function and strengthen muscles around joint.
