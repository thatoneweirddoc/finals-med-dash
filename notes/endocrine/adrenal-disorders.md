## Adrenal Disorders


### Endocrine Master Notes — Adrenal Disorders

#### 7.1 Cushing's Syndrome

**Cushing's syndrome = chronic glucocorticoid excess** (of any cause). **Cushing's *disease* = the specific pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma.**

**Features:** central obesity, moon face, dorsocervical fat pad ("buffalo hump"), **purple striae >1 cm, easy bruising, thin skin, proximal myopathy**, hypertension, glucose intolerance/diabetes, osteoporosis, hirsutism, mood change; hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis (esp. ectopic ACTH).

**Commonest cause overall is iatrogenic (exogenous steroids)** — always the first thing to exclude.

**Step 1 — confirm cortisol excess (screening tests, need ≥2 positive):**
- **Overnight/low-dose dexamethasone suppression test** (fails to suppress)
- **Late-night salivary cortisol** (loss of diurnal trough)
- **24 h urinary free cortisol** (elevated)

**Step 2 — find the source with ACTH:**

| | ACTH | Source | Next test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ACTH-dependent** | **High/normal** | Pituitary (**Cushing's disease**, ~⅔) or **ectopic** ACTH (small-cell lung, carcinoid) | **High-dose DST** (pituitary suppresses, ectopic doesn't); **CRH test**; pituitary MRI; **inferior petrosal sinus sampling** if unclear |
| **ACTH-independent** | **Low/suppressed** | **Adrenal** adenoma/carcinoma (or bilateral hyperplasia) | Adrenal CT |

**Discriminator:** **very high ACTH + hypokalaemia + rapid onset + pigmentation** suggests **ectopic ACTH** (paraneoplastic). **Management:** treat the cause — transsphenoidal surgery (disease), adrenalectomy (adrenal), resect the ectopic source; medical control with **metyrapone/ketoconazole** meanwhile.

#### 7.2 Adrenal Insufficiency

**Primary (Addison's disease)** — the adrenal cortex itself fails, so **both cortisol AND aldosterone are lost**, and ACTH rises:
- **Causes:** **autoimmune adrenalitis** (commonest in developed world; may be part of autoimmune polyendocrine syndromes), **TB** (commonest worldwide), metastases, haemorrhage (Waterhouse–Friderichsen in meningococcaemia), adrenoleukodystrophy.
- **Features:** fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, **postural hypotension**, salt craving, GI upset, and — the discriminator — **hyperpigmentation** (palmar creases, buccal mucosa, scars) from **high ACTH/POMC (MSH activity)**.
- **Biochemistry:** **hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia** (aldosterone loss), hypoglycaemia, ± mild hypercalcaemia; **high ACTH**; **low cortisol failing to rise on short Synacthen**.

**Secondary** — pituitary ACTH deficiency (tumour, or **suppression by exogenous steroids** — the commonest cause):
- **Aldosterone is preserved** (RAAS-driven), so **no hyperkalaemia and no pigmentation** (ACTH is low). Cortisol low, ACTH low.

**Discriminator:** **pigmentation + hyperkalaemia = primary (Addison's); pale + normal potassium = secondary.**

**Addisonian (adrenal) crisis** *(emergency)* — hypotension/shock, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, hypoglycaemia, often precipitated by infection/surgery or steroid withdrawal: **IV hydrocortisone 100 mg immediately** + **aggressive IV 0.9% saline** (± glucose); don't wait for confirmatory tests (take a random cortisol/ACTH first if quick). **Steroid sick-day rules:** double oral glucocorticoid during intercurrent illness; switch to parenteral if vomiting; carry a steroid emergency card. Replacement: **hydrocortisone** (glucocorticoid) + **fludrocortisone** (mineralocorticoid) in primary disease.

#### 7.3 Mineralocorticoid and Catecholamine Excess

##### Primary aldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) ⚑
Autonomous aldosterone excess — **the commonest cause of secondary/endocrine hypertension**, and increasingly recognised (current guidance broadens screening toward **most people with hypertension**, not just the classic hypokalaemic case).
- **Presentation:** **hypertension** (often resistant), ± **hypokalaemia** (weakness, cramps, polyuria), metabolic alkalosis. Note many are **normokalaemic**.
- **Screening:** **aldosterone:renin ratio (ARR)** — **high aldosterone with suppressed renin**. (Stop interfering drugs where possible; spironolactone must be withheld.)
- **Confirm:** saline/fludrocortisone **suppression** (aldosterone fails to suppress).
- **Subtype/localise:** adrenal CT + **adrenal vein sampling** to separate a **unilateral adenoma** (→ **adrenalectomy**) from **bilateral hyperplasia** (→ **mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonist: spironolactone or eplerenone**).

##### Phaeochromocytoma / paraganglioma
Catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal **medulla** (or extra-adrenal paraganglion). "**Rule of 10s**" (classic teaching, now looser): ~10% bilateral, ~10% extra-adrenal, ~10% malignant, ~a quarter **hereditary** (MEN 2, VHL, NF1, SDH mutations).
- **Presentation:** paroxysmal **headache, sweating, palpitations** with **labile/severe hypertension** (the classic triad); pallor, anxiety, weight loss; crises provoked by anaesthesia, contrast, β-blockade-first.
- **Diagnosis:** **plasma or 24 h urinary metanephrines/normetanephrines** (most sensitive) → then imaging (CT/MRI, MIBG). **Never biopsy.**
- **Management:** **α-blockade FIRST (phenoxybenzamine/doxazosin), then β-blockade** once α-blocked, with volume expansion, before surgical resection. **Giving a β-blocker first causes unopposed α-vasoconstriction → hypertensive crisis** — a classic exam trap.

##### Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)
Enzyme defect in cortisol synthesis (**21-hydroxylase deficiency, ~90%**) → low cortisol → high ACTH → adrenal hyperplasia and shunting into **androgens**.
- **Classic salt-wasting** (severe): neonatal **hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypotension** + **ambiguous genitalia** in girls / adrenal crisis in the first weeks. **17-hydroxyprogesterone is high.** Treat with glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid replacement.
- Non-classic (mild): later hirsutism/oligomenorrhoea (a PCOS mimic).

##### Adrenal incidentaloma
An adrenal mass found on imaging done for another reason. Two questions: **is it functioning?** (screen for phaeochromocytoma, Cushing's/autonomous cortisol, and — if hypertensive — Conn's) and **is it malignant?** (size >4 cm, irregular, high Hounsfield units, rapid growth → resect).

### Endocrinology (ANU) — Adrenal Gland — Adrenal Disorders

**Adrenal cortex** (derived from mesoderm) and **medulla **(derived from neural crest)

![](notes/images/endocrinology-anu/img022.jpg)

![](notes/images/endocrinology-anu/img023.jpg)

Glucocorticoids: primarily cortisol

- Involved in the regulation of metabolism (counteracts insulin), supports blood pressure and vasomotor tone, and involved in regulation of behaviour and immunosuppression
- Stimulatory effects
- Hepatic gluconeogenesis
- Insulin resistance in peripheral tissues
- Protein catabolism and lipolysis
- Bone resorption
- Leukocytosis
- ↑ cardiac output, vascular tone, Na+ retention
- PTH release and urine calcium excretion
- Permissive effect on catecholamines
- Inhibitory effects: antiphlogistic (↓ inflammation)

Androgens: present as dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) in the adrenal cortex (intermediate male sex steroid )

- Minimal effect compared to the gonads
- Anabolic effects on muscles and bones
- Influences behaviour and CNS processes
- In men: sexual differentiation, spermatogenesis

Mineralocorticoids: primarily aldosterone → mainly regulated through renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)

- Act on nuclear mineralocorticoid receptors within the principal cells of the distal tubule and collecting duct
- Negative feedback to JGA by long loop (aldosterone → volume expansion) and short loop (angiotensin II → peripheral vasoconstriction)
- Aldosterone increases renal reabsorption of sodium (and water) and augments the excretion of potassium and protons → ↑ extracellular fluid, ↑ blood pressure, ↓ K+, ↑ pH

Adrenaline

- Catecholamines are synthesized from tyrosine in postganglionic sympathetic nerves (noaradrenaline) and chromaffin cells of adrenal medulla (adrenaline)
- Broken down into metanephrines and other metabolites (VMA, HVA) and excreted in urine
- Constricts blood vessels but dilates airways
- Increases RR, HR, BP and CO

Adrenal-sympathetic-thyroid link

- ACTH stimulates synthesis of DA and NA, and cortisol up-regulates the conversion of noradrenaline to adrenaline
- When stressed is sensed by the cortex, it activates the HPA axis and amplifies adrenaline production
- There is increased sympathetic drive to the adrenal medulla, leading to increased adrenaline release
- Simultaneously, high thyroid levels cause an increased expression of β-adrenoreceptors, and hence, an increased response to adrenaline

INVESTIGATIONS

- Hypocortisolism: ↓cortisol, confirmed with ACTH stimulation test (persisting ↓ cortisol)
- Hypoaldosteronism: ↑ plasma renin, ↓ urine aldosterone levels
- Adrenal hypoandrogenism: ↓ DHEA-S
- Additional laboratory findings
- Hyponatremia
- Hyperkalaemia and a normal anion gap metabolic acidosis
- Mild hypercalcemia (in up to ⅓ of cases)
- Increased serum creatinine and BUN as a result of hypovolemia
- Eosinophilia

Identifying the underlying cause of adrenal insufficiency

- Serum ACTH levels:  ↑ ACTH in primary and ↓ ACTH in secondary
- If primary adrenal insufficiency
- Adrenal antibodies and other autoimmune diseases (e.g., Hashimoto thyroiditis, pernicious anaemia, type 1 DM)
- Adrenal imaging (ultrasound, MRI, CT)
- If an infectious aetiology is suspected: chest x-ray, HIV test
- If secondary adrenal insufficiency
- Rule out iatrogenic Cushing syndrome
- CNS imaging to detect pituitary tumours

MANAGEMENT

- Glucocorticoid replacement therapy with hydrocortisone: in order to emulate the natural diurnal rhythm, the daily dose of hydrocortisone (10–25 mg) is administered in 2–3 doses (e.g., ½–¼–¼).
- Adjustment of glucocorticoid dosage under stress: If glucocorticoids is not increased during  stress (e.g., surgery, febrile infections, trauma), the patient may develop an adrenal crisis!
- Patients with Addison’s may require fludrocortisone in addition to GRT
- Loss of libido in female patients can be treated with dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)

ADRENAL CRISIS (ADDISON’S CRISIS)

- Definition: acute, severe glucocorticoid deficiency that is usually precipitated by stress (infection, trauma, surgery), sudden glucocorticoid discontinuation after prolonged use, Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome, and pituitary apoplexy
- Features: fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, severe abdominal pain (mimics peritonitis), impaired consciousness, hypotension ± shock, hypoglycaemia, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis
- Treatment:

- High doses of hydrocortisone (100 mg IV followed by 100-200 mg over 24 hours)
- Fluid replacement with normal saline
- Correct hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia and metabolic acidosis
- ICU monitoring

#### Phaeochromocytoma

**Definition: **Intra-adrenal or extra-adrenal (called paragangliomas)

**Aetiology: **Sporadic or familial🡪 associated with MEN 2a and 2b syndromes (50%), von Hippel- Lindau disease (20%) or von Recklinghausen’s disease (5%)

basophilc cytoplasm. Eosinophilic cytoplasmic globules.

**Prognosis: **can be malignant if very large, many mitoses, necrotic, haemorrhagic.

- Treatment  - 10% of pheochromocytomas are malignant🡪 open surgery
- Benign pheochromocytoma patients 🡪  laparoscopic surgery performed through several small incisions.

### Adrenal Summary — Clinical features of adrenal insufficiency — Adrenal Disorders

**Glucocorticoid deficiency** 🡪  hypoglycaemia - weight loss - weakness  - anorexia, nausea, vomiting - may be occult until time of stress (normally, stress causes body to put on an extra surge of cortisol without this everything will decompensate suddenly!)

**Mineralocorticoid deficiency** 🡪 hypotension (volume depleted) - salt craving (sodium depleted without aldosterone to help sodium reabsorption)

**ACTH excess (if primary)** 🡪 negative feedback …too little cortisol in blood so ACTH keeps being produced to try to raise it. However this causes excessive melanocyte stimulating hormone - this is because the pituitary gland simultaneously makes lots of  ACTH and MSH (they are packaged together in a precursor protein (POMC) preopiomelanocortin which is then divided) 🡪 pigmentation / bronzing suntanned appearance ALL OVER even the gums, non exposed areas

**Fun fact - vitilago** = depigmentation due to autoimmune disease against melanocytes. addisons = bronzing. you can have both at the same time because autoimmune diseases ↑ Rx of other autoimmune diseases.

### Adrenal Summary — CT and MRI — Adrenal Disorders

- Adrenal tumors &gt; 6cm are highly suspicious for adrenocortical carcinomas.
- CT &gt; MRI esp for detecting small unilateral adrenocortical adenomas or bilateral micronodular hyperplasia
- MRI with dynamic gadolinium-enhanced and chemical shift technique is as effective as CT in distinguishing malignant from benign lesions.
- MRI is much less useful for detecting adrenal nodularity owing to motion artifacts.
- differentiation btw malignant and benign adrenal tumors fat content
- Measurement of Hounsfield units (HU) in unenhanced CT attenuation value &gt; 10 HU OR enhancement washout &lt; 50% and a delayed attenuation &gt; 35 HU (on 10- to 15-min delayed enhanced CT) are suspicious for malignancy.

### Adrenal Summary — Treatment of Adrenal Disease eg adrenocortical cancer — Adrenal Disorders

- Laparoscopic adrenalectomy 1st choice for benign adrenal lesions w/ diameter of &lt; 6cm.
- The treatment of choice for bilateral adrenal hyperplasias associated with ACTH-independent Cushing's syndrome
- Complications: Patients require lifelong replacement therapy with glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids, and they should be adequately educated about the risk of acute adrenal insufficiency
- In stages I to III adrenocortical carcinoma, complete tumor removal offers best chance for cure.
- disadvantage: Surgery’s often extensive, with en bloc resection of invaded organs, and regularly includes lymphadenectomy.
- 5 YSR ranges between 16% and 38%. Median survival for metastatic disease (stage IV) is &lt; 12 months.

- Radiotherapyineffective for adrenocortical cancer
- can control localized disease not amenable to surgery.

Mitotane

- the only adrenal-specific agent available for treating metastatic adrenocortical carcinoma
- also an adjuvant for tumours with a high risk of recurrence
- Mechanism: cytotoxic effect on adrenocortical cells 🡪 focal degeneration of the fascicular and particularly the reticular zone.
- Complications: induces adrenal insufficiency and increases the metabolic clearance of glucocorticoids, glucocorticoid replacement is indicated, often at higher than normal doses owing to increased clearance.

Cytotoxic chemotherapy

- includes etoposide (VePesid),[1]doxorubicin (Adriamycin),[1] and cisplatin (Platinol),[1] or streptozocin (Zanosar)[1] plus mitotane.
- limited efficacy for advanced adrenocortical cancer and is associated mainly with partial responses.

### Thyroid & Parathyroid Summary — Oxidation of iodide ions — Adrenal Disorders

The oxidation of iodide from I- to I+ and its incorporation into thyroglobulin is catalysed by *thyroperoxidase* on the luminal surface of secretory vesicle🡪 requires the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as an oxidising agent

Thyroglobulin is released from Golgi apparatus with peroxidase
