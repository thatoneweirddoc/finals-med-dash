#### Composition of different intravenous fluid, Physiology of body compartments

![](notes/images/anaesthetics-anu/img011.jpg)

- The ECF is further divided into the intravascular (within the circulation) and the interstitial (extravascular fluid surrounding the cells) fluid spaces
- The intravascular space (blood volume=5-7% of body weight) has its own intracellular component in the form of red (haematocrit = 40-45%) and white cells and an extracellular element in the form of plasma (55-60% of total blood volume). It is separated from the extravascular component by the capillary membrane.
- While the hydrostatic pressure within the circulation tends to drive fluid out, the oncotic pressure of the plasma proteins, e.g. albumin, draws fluid in and maintains the relative constancy of the plasma volume as a proportion of the ECF (Starling effect)

![](notes/images/anaesthetics-anu/img012.jpg)

#### Principles of fluid replacement therapy

- Fluid Therapy is the administration of fluids to a patient as a treatment or preventative measure.
- It can be administered via an intravenous, intraperitoneal, intraosseous, subcutaneous and oral routes.
- 60% of total bodyweight is accounted for by the total body water. This can further be divided into intracellular or extracellular as shown below.

![](notes/images/anaesthetics-anu/img013.jpg)

- Fluid therapy is indicated either when there is a loss of fluid to any part of these compartments or there is a risk of loss of fluid. The severity of the fluid loss, and the compartment from which it has been lost, influence the choice of fluid and the speed at which it needs to be administered. If fluid therapy is performed as a treatment then it is necessary to diagnose and treat the underlying condition.
- IndicationsHypotension
- Hypovolemia
- Electrolyte, metabolic and acid base disorders
- Decreased oxygen delivery
- Geriatric patients at risk of organ failure

- Replacements are calculated based on the level of dehydration. To calculate the amount required for replacement within a 24 hour period, the percentage dehydration is used in the following calculation- Replacement = % Dehydration x Bodyweight (kg) x 10

The **“triad of anesthesia”** involves a **hypnotic agent (narcosis), analgesia **and **muscle relaxation. **

**induction**

HYPNOTIC AGENTS

1. iv induction agents

**Propofol **

- Hypnotic
- Mechanism of Action: GABA agonist within the CNS, most likely at the GABAA receptor complex, where it slows the channel-closing time and acts as a sodium channel blocker
- Major effects: Dose-dependent CNS, cardiovascular and respiratory depression
- Side-effects: irregular heart rate, low blood pressure (sudden drop), stopping of breathing, infection, seizures, allergy

**Thiopental**

- Ultra-short acting thiobarbiturate- hypnotic
- Uses: Induction phase of general anaestheia, but largely replaced by Propofol.
- Mechanism of Action: the GABAA receptor channel and enhance its inhibitory action
- Side effects- uncontrolled hypotension, cardiac failure, liver disease, status asthmaticus, myxedema

**Ketamine**

- Mechanism of action: Antagonist of the NMDA receptor and agonises opioid receptors weakly, causing: Increased HR, increased BP, increased SVR, increased coronary flow, increased myocardial O2 uptake
- CNS and respiratory depression, bronchial smooth muscle relaxation

- Side effects/Caution: allergy, TCA medication (causes HTN and dysrhythmias), nausea, vomiting, respiratory depression

**Benzodiazepines (midazolam, diazepam [Valium], lorazepam)**

- Mechanism of Action: Increases glycine inhibitory neurotransmitter 🡪 facilitates GABA
- Has five therapeutic effects:Anxiolytic
- Hypnotic
- Anticonvulsant
- Amnesia
- Muscle relaxation

- Side Effects: marked respiratory depression (particularly risky when given with opioids), cardiac depression (slow heart rate, arrhythmias and hypotension), CNS depression (drowsiness),  nausea/vomiting, anaphylaxis

**maintenance**

volatile inhalation agents- maintenance

- Properties: Liquid at room temperature, odourless and easy to inhale
- Uses: Induction and particularly maintenance of general anaesthesia particularly in difficult airways e.g. paediatric, inhaled foreign body where spontaneous ventilation is preferred
- Examples include sevoflurane (least irritative to airways), desflurane, halothane, and nitrous oxide
- Mechanism of Action:Act in different ways at the level of the CNS
- Disrupt normal synaptic transmission by either interfering with neurotransmitter release from the presynaptic terminal, altering NT uptake, influencing ionic conductance, or changing the post-synaptic receptors ability to bind the NT
- Respiratory: Depression, decreased response to CO2 reflexes, bronchodilation
- MSK: Muscle relaxation
- CVS: Varies with drug

- Side effects: Coadministration of drugs can exacerbate its effects, nausea, vomiting, cardiac dysrhythmias

Other

**Analgesia** (usually IV opioids) and **muscle relaxants** are also given as needed

**Muscle relaxants **

![](notes/images/anaesthetics-anu/img015.jpg)

Muscle Relaxants

**Net Effect: **

- Block nicotinic cholinergic receptors in NMJ
- Provides skeletal muscle paralysis, including the diaphragm, but spares involuntary muscles such as the heart and smooth muscle
- It:

- Facilitates intubation
- Assists with mechanical ventilation
- prevents muscle stretch reflex and decreases muscle tone
- Allows access to the surgical  field (intracavitary surgery)

**Important Information**

- Never use muscle relaxants without adequate preparation and equipment to maintain airway and ventilation
- Nerve stimulator (i.e. train of four) is used intraoperatively to assess the degree of nerve block; no twitch response seen with complete neuromuscular blockade. It is used during recovery from the application of general anesthesia to objectively determine how well a patient’s muscles are able to function.

- Depolarizing muscle relaxants

- e.g. Succinylcholine (SCh)/Suxamethonium Chloride
- Mechanism of Action: Mimics ACh and binds to Ach receptors, causing prolonged depolarization. Initial fasciculation may be seen, followed by temporary paralysis, secondary to blocked Ach receptors by SCh
- Side effects:
- Bradycardia/Dysrhythmias- Stimulates muscarinic cholinergic autonomic receptors and nicotinic receptors
- Hyperkalemia- Massive release of K+ outside of muscle cells

- Non-depolarizing muscle relaxants:

- E.g. Mivacurium (Short), Rocuronium, ,Vecuronium, Cistracurium (Intermediate), Pancuronium (Long)
- Mechanism of Action: Blockade of postsynaptic ACh receptors, preventing depolarization (competitive)
- Side effects:
- Mild hypotension
- Mild bradycardia

**emergence**

Reversing Agents for Non-Depolarisation Relaxants (only)

**Neostigmine**

- Mechanism of action: Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors inhibits enzymatic degradation of Ach 🡪  increases amount of ACh at nicotinic and muscarinic receptors,displacing non-depolarizing muscle relaxant
- Indications: Administer reversal agents when there has been some recovery of blockade (i.e. muscle twitch)
- Side effects: Muscarinic overdrive (SLUDGE) syndrome.S= Salivation by stimulation of salivary glands
- L=Lacrimation (tearing)
- U=Urination due to relaxation of the internal sphincter muscle of the urethra and contraction of detrusor muscles
- D= Diaphoresis due to stimulation of sweat glands
- G= Increased GI motility 🡪 diarrhoea
- E= Emesis

**Glycopyrrolate and Atropine**

- Anticholinergic and Antimuscarinic agents to treat the side effects of Neostigmine
- Muscarinic receptor antagonists prevent the effects of ACh by blocking its binding to muscarinic cholinergic receptors at neuroeffector sites on smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and gland cells; in peripheral ganglia; and in the central nervous system

**Sugamaddex**

- Newly developed agent for the reversal of Rocuronium
- Reverses profound blockade and can be given for immediate reversal without the need to wait for partial recovery
- It has no effect on acetylcholinesterase, eliminating the need for concomitant anticholinergic drugs (e.g. glycopyrrolate), which must be administered with acetylcholine esterase
- However, it’s currently very expensive ☹

**analgesia**

There are two components in the pathological pain state:

- The peripheral nociceptive afferent neuron, which is activated by noxious stimuli
- The central mechanisms by which the afferent input generates a pain sensation

The nerve fibres associated with pain include:

- C-Fibres (more than 70%)Non-myelinated with low conduction velocities (<1 m/s); this group is known as C polymodal nociceptors
- C-fibre activity causes a dull, diffuse, burning pain

- Aδ FibresFine myelinated fibres which conduct more rapidly
- Connected to high-threshold mechanoreceptors
- Aδ fibres cause a sensation of sharp, well-localised pain

Options include opioids (e.g. morphine, fentanyl, hydromorphone), NSAIDS, acetaminophen, local, and regional anesthetic

Non-Opioid Analgesics (Non-narcotics)

**Acetylsalicylic Acid**

- Inhibits endogenous prostaglandins 🡪 anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic, antirheumatic and anti-thrombotic activity
- Relieves mild to moderate pain
- Side EffectsHypersensitivity
- GI Effects- dyspepsia, heartburn, epigastric distress, nausea
- Bleeding- due to inhibition of platelet aggregation

**Paracetamol/Acetaminophen**

- Relieves mild to moderate pain
- Proposed mechanism: Inhibition of COX-1, COX-2 and COX-3 enzymes involved in prostaglandin synthesis. It does not inhibit cyclooxygenase in peripheral tissues, thus has no peripheral anti-inflammatory affects. Studies found that paracetamol is ineffective in the presence of peroxides, explaining its effectiveness in the CNS and endothelial cells, but not in platelets and immune cells. Also, it affects hypothalamic heat-regulating centres 🡪 peripheral vasodilation 🡪 sweating 🡪 antipyretic
- Side-effects (rare)- hypersensitivity, dermatological reactions, neutropenia and thrombocytopenic purpura

**Local anaesthetics**

Pharmacology of Local Anaesthetics

- LA are drugs that block the generation and propagation of impulses in excitable tissues: nerves, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, brain
- LA bind to receptors on the cytosolic side of the Na+ channel, inhibiting Na+ flux and thus blocking impulse conduction
- Different types of nerve fibers undergo blockade at different rates

SYSTEMIC TOXICITY

- CNS effectsNumbness of tongue, perioral tingling, metallic taste
- Disorientation
- Tinnitus
- Visual disturbances
- Muscle twitching
- Unconsciousness
- Convulsions, seisures
- CNS depression, respiratory arrest, coma

- CVS effectsVasodilation
- Hypotension
- Decreased myocardial contractility
- Delay in cardiac impulse transmission e.g. sinus bradycardia
- CVS collapse

- Treatment of Systemic ToxicityEarly recognition
- 100% O2, manage ABCs
- Diazepam/Sodium thiopental for seizures
- Manage arrhythmias
- Intralipid to bind LA in circulation
