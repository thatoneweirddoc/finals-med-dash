#### airway suctioning

An airway can be obstructed by secretions (**blood** and **vomitus**), potentially leading to **aspiration. **This is prevented by **airway suctioning**

**Pulmonary Aspiration**

- Pulmonary aspiration is the entry of material (e.g. pharyngeal secretions, stomach contents) from the oropharynx or gastrointestinal tract into the larynx and lower respiratory track (trachea to lungs).
- A person may inhale the material or it may be delivered into the trachea bronchial tree during positive pressure ventilation.
- Consequences:
- Chemical pneumonitis
- Pneumonia
- Asphyxiation (deficient oxygen supply due to abnormal breathing 🡪 generalised hypoxia 🡪 coma/death)
- Increased risk of aspiration with:
- Decreased LOC (loss of consciousness)
- Trauma
- Meals within 8 hours
- Suspected sphincter incompetence (GERD, hiatus hernia, nasogastric tube)
- Increased abdominal pressure (pregnancy, obesity, bowel obstruction, acute abdomen)
- Laryngeal mask vs. endotracheal tube (ETT)
- Management:
- Reduce gastric volume and acidity
- Delaying inhibiting airway reflexes with muscular relaxants
- Employ rapid sequence induction- This produces immediate unresponsiveness (induction agent) and muscular relaxation (neuromuscular blocking agent)

Equipment**

- Yankeur Catheter- hard plastic catheter that removes secretions from the oropharynx and external nares.
- Y Suction Catheter- soft flexible catheter available in many sizes and typically used to suction down endotracheal tubes. They can also be used to clear the nares, nasopharynx, oropharynx, stoma’s or airway adjuncts

**Indications and Contraindications**

- Indications- Remove blood/vomitus from airway or in preparation for endotracheal intubation
- Nil Contraindications

**Complications**

- Airway trauma
- Stimulate coughing or gagging
- Hypoxia from delays in ventilation with tracheal tube suctioning
- Vagal stimulation can result in bradycardia and hypotension

#### laryngeal mask airway (LMA)

![](notes/images/anaesthetics-anu/img005.jpg)

- Supraglottic airway device
- Maintains airway patency
- Procedure: Water-based lubricant is applied to the cuff. The mask tip is inserted over the hard and soft palate and continues into the laryngopharynx until there is resistance. The cuff is inflated with an appropriate volume of air
- Confirmation of placement: misting of tube, auscultation, EtCO2 detection and waveform

#### ENDOTRACHEAL INTUBATION

- Indications: Loss of airway patency/protection e.g. in cardiac arrest, unconscious patients with a loss of gag reflex
- Equipment: MDSOLES Monitors
- Drugs,
- Suction
- Oxygen source and self-inflating bag with oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways
- Laryngoscope
- Endotracheal tubes (appropriate size and one size smaller)
- Stylet, Syringe for tube cuff inflation]

- ProcedurePlace the patient’s head in the sniffing position.
- MILS in the neutral position if suspected C-spine injury
- Open mouth AND inspect oral cavity.
- Remove dentures or debris.
- Place laryngoscope with left hand into the right side of the patient’s mouth, sweeping the tongue to the left.
- Elevate the laryngoscope to lift the mandible without levering on the teeth. The end of the blade should rest in the epiglottic vallecula allowing direct visualization of the larynx.
- Suction the airway as required.
- Introduce bougie gently through the cords – tracheal clicks and hold-up should be appreciable.
- Advance the endotracheal tube over the bougie until the cuff is seen to pass through the cords. Approximate ETT length at lips for women is 20–21 and men 22–24 centimetres.
- Remove bougie.
- Connect BVM and commence ventilation.
- Inflate cuff.
- Confirm tube placementVisualisation of ETT passing through cords
- EtCO2 in exhaled gas by capnography (will be zero or near zero if inserted in oesophagus)
- Bilateral chest movement

- Complications: Dental damage, laceration, laryngeal trauma, oesophageal intubation, accidental extubation, aspiration, laryngospasm, bronchospasm

#### monitoring

- Anesthetic depthInadequate: blink reflex present when eyelashes lightly touched, HTN, tachycardia, tearing or sweating
- Excessive: hypotension, bradycardia

- Oxygenation: pulse oximetry, fraction of inspired O2 (FiO2)
- Ventilation: verify correct position of ETT, chest excursions, breath sounds, ETCO2 analysis, monitor inhaled and exhaled concentrations of inhaled anesthetics
- Circulation: pulse, heart sounds, BP, telemetry, oximetry, CVP, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure
- Temperature
- Other: Signs of anaphylaxis
- Minimum alveolar concentration (MAC): concentration of the vapour in the lungs that is needed to prevent movement (motor response) in 50% of subjects in response to surgical (pain) stimulus. MAC is used to compare the strengths, or potency, of anesthetic vapor
