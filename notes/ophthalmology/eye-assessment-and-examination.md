## Eye Assessment and Examination

### WSU GP Ophthalmology Workshop — Assessment

*Liverpool & Campbelltown Hospital Ophthalmology Department, Western Sydney University — Drs Jason Cheng, David Ng, Subha Kadappu and Stephen Ong.*

**History**: onset, laterality, trauma, contact history, **contact lens wear**. Symptoms — itch, pain, headache, vomiting. Discharge — watery, purulent, or mucopurulent. Medical history including hypertension and thyroid disease.

**Examination**: visual acuity; **pattern of redness — sectoral, diffuse, or circumciliary**; cornea (opacities, epithelium, fluorescein staining); conjunctiva (papillae, follicles); anterior chamber (**hypopyon, hyphaema**); pupils (size, shape, reflexes).

Practical skills taught: eye patching, **lid eversion**, fluorescein application, direct ophthalmoscopy, corneal foreign body removal.

### Finals Tutor — Structured Eye Assessment

#### Visual acuity — always first

Visual acuity is the **vital sign of the eye**. Record it before doing anything else, each eye separately, with the patient's usual correction.

**Pinhole testing** distinguishes refractive error from pathology: if acuity **improves with a pinhole**, the problem is refractive; if it does not, there is organic disease. This is the single most useful bedside discriminator and is easy marks.

If unable to read the chart, step down: counting fingers → hand movements → perception of light → no perception of light.

#### Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)

The **swinging torch test**. Swing the light between eyes: in a RAPD the affected pupil **dilates** when the light moves to it, because its afferent input is weaker.

A RAPD indicates significant **optic nerve or extensive retinal disease** — optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, central retinal artery or vein occlusion, large retinal detachment, advanced glaucoma. It does **not** occur with cataract or refractive error, which is exactly why it is useful: dense cataract reduces acuity but never causes a RAPD.

#### The rest of the examination

- **External** — lids, lashes, orbit, proptosis, ptosis
- **Pupils** — size, shape, direct and consensual reflexes, RAPD, accommodation
- **Eye movements** — six cardinal positions, diplopia, pain on movement
- **Visual fields** — confrontation; formal perimetry if indicated
- **Fluorescein** with blue light — corneal abrasions and **dendritic ulcers** of herpes simplex keratitis
- **Lid eversion** — essential when a subtarsal foreign body is suspected, and routinely forgotten
- **Fundoscopy** — disc (cup:disc ratio, swelling, pallor), vessels, macula, periphery
- **Intraocular pressure** where available

#### Red flags requiring same-day ophthalmology

Sudden vision loss; severe eye pain with nausea and vomiting; **contact lens wearer with a red painful eye** (assume microbial keratitis); penetrating trauma or chemical injury; flashes and floaters with a field defect; RAPD; hypopyon or hyphaema; post-operative red painful eye (endophthalmitis).

#### Chemical injury — the one exception to assessment first

**Irrigate immediately and copiously before anything else**, including before measuring acuity. Alkali burns penetrate deeper than acid (liquefactive necrosis) and are more damaging. Continue irrigation until pH normalises.
