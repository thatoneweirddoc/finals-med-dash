## Sudden Vision Loss

### Finals Tutor — Sudden Vision Loss

Structure the differential by whether the eye is **painful or painless**, and by anatomy from front to back.

#### Painless sudden vision loss

**Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)**
- Sudden, profound, painless monocular loss — "a curtain came down and stayed"
- **RAPD present**; pale retina with a **cherry-red spot** at the macula (the fovea is perfused by the underlying choroid); attenuated arteries with "boxcarring"
- This is a **stroke of the eye** — manage as a stroke: urgent referral, and investigate for the source (carotid disease, atrial fibrillation, cardiac emboli) plus vascular risk factors
- **Always exclude giant cell arteritis** in anyone over 50
- **Time window**: the literature genuinely conflicts. Experimental work suggests irreversible ganglion cell loss from about **12–15 minutes** with complete occlusion, while primate and clinical data suggest tolerance of roughly **90–105 minutes** and irreversible damage by about **240 minutes**. The practical answer for exams and clinical practice: treat it as an **emergency where time is vision**, with poor outcomes beyond a few hours. Ocular massage, anterior chamber paracentesis and pressure-lowering agents are attempted but evidence of benefit is weak

**Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO)**
- Variable, usually less profound loss
- Fundus: **widespread flame haemorrhages, dilated tortuous veins, cotton wool spots, disc swelling** — the "blood and thunder" or "stormy sunset" appearance
- Risks: hypertension, diabetes, hyperviscosity, glaucoma, smoking
- Complications: **macular oedema** (treated with anti-VEGF) and **neovascularisation** including rubeotic glaucoma

**Retinal detachment**
- **Flashes and floaters**, then a **curtain or shadow** across the visual field, painless
- Reduced acuity if the macula is involved ("mac-off" — worse prognosis, and the urgency of surgery depends on it)
- Risks: myopia, previous cataract surgery, trauma, previous detachment
- Differentials for flashes and floaters: **posterior vitreous detachment** and **retinal tear** — both less serious, but any new flashes/floaters need prompt assessment because a tear can be treated with laser before it detaches

**Vitreous haemorrhage** — sudden floaters or dark "cobwebs", loss of red reflex, poor fundal view. Commonest cause is **proliferative diabetic retinopathy**; also retinal tear, trauma.

**Wet age-related macular degeneration** — sudden central distortion (**metamorphopsia**) and central scotoma; **anti-VEGF** injections are effective and urgent.

#### Painful sudden vision loss

**Optic neuritis**
- Subacute monocular loss over hours to days, **pain on eye movement**, **RAPD**, reduced colour vision (especially red desaturation — often out of proportion to acuity)
- Disc may be swollen (papillitis) or normal (retrobulbar — "the patient sees nothing and the doctor sees nothing")
- Strongly associated with **multiple sclerosis**; also idiopathic, post-viral, infections, and MOG/NMO antibody disease
- Vision usually improves over 2–3 weeks; **IV methylprednisolone** speeds recovery but does not change final acuity. **Oral prednisolone alone is avoided** — historically associated with increased recurrence

**Giant cell arteritis / arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy**
- Age >50, **headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication**, malaise, polymyalgia symptoms, tender non-pulsatile temporal artery
- Sudden severe loss, often with **swollen pale disc**; the fellow eye is at high risk within days
- Raised **ESR and CRP**; temporal artery biopsy confirms (skip lesions mean a negative biopsy does not exclude)
- **Start high-dose corticosteroids immediately on clinical suspicion — do not wait for biopsy or bloods.** IV methylprednisolone if vision is already affected. This is the highest-stakes "treat first" answer in ophthalmology

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma** — see The Red Eye.

**Endophthalmitis** — pain, reduced vision, hypopyon after intraocular surgery or injection. Emergency: vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics.

#### Transient vision loss

**Amaurosis fugax** — painless transient monocular loss, "curtain descending", lasting minutes. It is a **TIA of the eye**: urgent assessment, carotid imaging, cardiac assessment, antiplatelet and vascular risk management.

| | Age-related macular degeneration | Dry (non-neovascular) 90% — slow progressive central decline and distortion; wet (neovascular) 10% — rapid, more substantial loss; treat wet with anti-VEGF |
