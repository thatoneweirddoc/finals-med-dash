## Gradual Vision Loss and Chronic Eye Disease

### Finals Tutor — Gradual Vision Loss and Chronic Eye Disease

The four causes of chronic progressive vision loss worth knowing cold: **cataract, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy**.

#### Cataract

Opacification of the crystalline lens. Gradual painless blurring, **glare and haloes especially at night**, faded colours, and a **reduced red reflex**. Acuity does **not** improve with pinhole to normal, and there is **no RAPD** — useful negatives.

Causes: age (commonest), diabetes, **corticosteroids** (posterior subcapsular), trauma, uveitis, congenital (rubella, galactosaemia — check the red reflex in every newborn).

Treatment: surgical phacoemulsification with intraocular lens, when vision affects function. Complications: **endophthalmitis** (rare, devastating — red painful eye with reduced vision post-op needs same-day review), posterior capsular opacification (treated with YAG laser).

#### Glaucoma

Progressive **optic neuropathy** with characteristic disc changes and visual field loss, usually but not always with raised intraocular pressure.

**Primary open-angle glaucoma**
- **Asymptomatic until advanced** — peripheral field lost first, central acuity preserved until late, which is why screening matters
- Signs: **increased cup:disc ratio (>0.5)**, disc notching, splinter haemorrhages, **arcuate scotoma** on fields
- Risks: age, family history, African ancestry, myopia, diabetes, corticosteroids
- Treatment ladder: **prostaglandin analogue drops (latanoprost) first-line**, then beta-blocker (timolol — caution in asthma and heart block), alpha-agonist, carbonic anhydrase inhibitor; laser trabeculoplasty; trabeculectomy
- Prostaglandin analogue side effects worth knowing: **iris darkening, eyelash growth, periorbital fat atrophy**

**Angle-closure** is the acute emergency covered in The Red Eye.

#### Age-related macular degeneration

Central vision loss with **preserved peripheral vision** — patients navigate but cannot read or recognise faces.

| | Dry (non-neovascular) | Wet (neovascular) |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion | ~90% | ~10% |
| Onset | Slow, progressive | **Rapid** |
| Features | **Drusen**, RPE changes, geographic atrophy | Choroidal neovascularisation, haemorrhage, exudate, fluid |
| Treatment | No specific treatment; **AREDS2 supplements** may slow progression; smoking cessation | **Intravitreal anti-VEGF** — effective, and urgent |

**Metamorphopsia** (straight lines appearing wavy) is the key symptom of conversion to wet AMD — patients monitor with an **Amsler grid** and are told to present immediately if it changes.

Risks: age, **smoking** (the biggest modifiable factor), family history, hypertension, fair skin.

#### Diabetic retinopathy

The leading cause of blindness in working-age adults. Driven by chronic hyperglycaemia causing microvascular damage — pericyte loss, capillary occlusion, ischaemia, and VEGF-driven neovascularisation.

**Classification**
- **Non-proliferative (NPDR)** — microaneurysms, dot and blot haemorrhages, **hard exudates**, **cotton wool spots** (ischaemic nerve fibre infarcts), venous beading, IRMA. Graded mild → moderate → severe
- **Proliferative (PDR)** — **new vessels** at the disc or elsewhere. Risks **vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, rubeosis iridis and neovascular glaucoma**
- **Diabetic maculopathy** — macular oedema or exudates threatening the fovea; the commonest cause of vision loss in diabetes, and can occur at any retinopathy stage

**Screening**: dilated fundus examination or retinal photography — from diagnosis in type 2 diabetes, and within 5 years of diagnosis in type 1, then at least 2-yearly (annually or more often with retinopathy, pregnancy, or poor control).

**Treatment**: optimise glycaemia, blood pressure and lipids; **anti-VEGF** for macular oedema; **panretinal photocoagulation** for proliferative disease; vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage or tractional detachment.

#### Hypertensive retinopathy

Graded: arteriolar narrowing → **AV nipping** → flame haemorrhages, cotton wool spots, hard exudates (**macular star**) → **papilloedema**, which indicates a hypertensive emergency requiring urgent BP management.

#### Papilloedema

**Bilateral optic disc swelling due to raised intracranial pressure** — the term is specific to that cause. Features: blurred disc margins, loss of venous pulsation, enlarged blind spot, **transient visual obscurations**, headache worse lying down or on Valsalva, and preserved acuity until late.

Causes: space-occupying lesion, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (typically young, female, obese, and associated with tetracyclines, vitamin A, and retinoids), venous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, malignant hypertension. Requires urgent neuroimaging **before** lumbar puncture.
