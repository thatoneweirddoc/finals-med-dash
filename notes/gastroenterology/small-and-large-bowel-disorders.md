## Small & Large Bowel Disorders


### Gastroenterology Master Notes — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

#### 5.1 Malabsorption and coeliac disease

**Malabsorption** presents with steatorrhoea, weight loss, and deficiency states (iron/folate → anaemia; B₁₂; fat-soluble vitamins; osteomalacia). Causes span mucosal (coeliac, Crohn's), luminal (pancreatic insufficiency, bile-salt deficiency), and structural (short bowel, bacterial overgrowth).

**⚑ Coeliac disease** — T-cell-mediated sensitivity to **gliadin (gluten)** → **villous atrophy + crypt hyperplasia + intraepithelial lymphocytes** in the **proximal small bowel** (hence **iron/folate** deficiency). Associations: **dermatitis herpetiformis** (itchy extensor vesicles — IgA, treat with dapsone), type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid, **IgA deficiency**, Down syndrome. Long-term risk: osteoporosis, hyposplenism, and **enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL)** / small-bowel adenocarcinoma.
- **Serology:** **anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA (anti-tTG) + total IgA** (to catch IgA deficiency → then use IgG-based tests). Endomysial antibody (EMA) is confirmatory. **Must be tested while eating gluten.**
- **⚑ Diagnosis:** historically duodenal **biopsy** (villous atrophy) was mandatory in adults. Current guidance now supports a **"no-biopsy" diagnosis in adults** with **anti-tTG ≥10× the upper limit of normal** *plus* a positive EMA on a second sample (mirroring the established paediatric ESPGHAN pathway) — otherwise biopsy still confirms. **HLA-DQ2/DQ8** has high negative predictive value (absence largely excludes coeliac).
- Treatment: **lifelong gluten-free diet**; pneumococcal vaccination (hyposplenism).

Other malabsorption: **bile-salt malabsorption** (terminal ileal disease → watery diarrhoea, SeHCAT test, treat with colestyramine); **small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)** (hydrogen breath test, rotating antibiotics); **tropical sprue**; **Whipple's disease** (*Tropheryma whipplei* — arthralgia, weight loss, diarrhoea, PAS-positive macrophages).

#### 5.2 Inflammatory bowel disease

**Crohn's disease vs ulcerative colitis — the core discriminators:**

| Feature | **Crohn's disease** | **Ulcerative colitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution | **Mouth → anus**, any part; **skip lesions**; terminal ileum classic | **Colon only**, from **rectum** proximally, **continuous** |
| Depth | **Transmural** | **Mucosa/submucosa** |
| Histology | **Non-caseating granulomas**, cobblestoning | Crypt abscesses, goblet-cell depletion |
| Smoking | **Worse** with smoking | **Protective** (paradox) |
| Complications | **Strictures, fistulae, abscesses, perianal disease**, B₁₂ malabsorption, gallstones | **Toxic megacolon**, higher **colorectal cancer** risk |
| Antibodies | ASCA+ | pANCA+ |
| Surgery | Not curative (recurs at anastomoses) | Colectomy is **curative** of the colitis |

**Shared extraintestinal manifestations** — some track disease activity, some don't:

| Manifestation | Note |
|---|---|
| Enteropathic **arthritis** (peripheral) | Tracks gut activity |
| **Erythema nodosum** | Tracks activity |
| **Pyoderma gangrenosum** | Independent of activity |
| Anterior **uveitis / episcleritis** | Eye |
| **Primary sclerosing cholangitis** | Strongly linked to **UC**; independent of activity |
| Sacroiliitis / ankylosing spondylitis | HLA-B27-linked |

**Assessing acute severe UC — Truelove & Witts** (severe = **>6 bloody stools/day** plus any systemic feature: temp >37.8°C, HR >90, Hb <105 g/L, ESR >30). This is a **medical emergency**: admit, IV fluids, **IV hydrocortisone**, VTE prophylaxis, stool chart, daily AXR (watch for **toxic megacolon**: transverse colon >6 cm — high perforation risk). Rescue therapy (**infliximab** or ciclosporin) if no response by **day 3**; **colectomy** if failing. Investigate: **faecal calprotectin** raised, endoscopy + biopsy, exclude infection/*C. difficile*.

**Medical management (⚑ the therapeutic landscape has expanded):**
- **UC:** topical/oral **5-ASA (mesalazine)** for mild–moderate (induction + maintenance); corticosteroids for flares; step up to **biologics** (anti-TNF infliximab/adalimumab/golimumab, anti-integrin **vedolizumab**, anti-IL-12/23 **ustekinumab**, anti-IL-23 **mirikizumab**), **JAK inhibitors** (**tofacitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib**) and **S1P modulators** (**ozanimod, etrasimod**).
- **Crohn's:** 5-ASAs are less effective; use **budesonide/steroids** to induce, **thiopurines/methotrexate** and **biologics** (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, risankizumab) to maintain; **smoking cessation** is essential.

#### 5.3 Irritable bowel syndrome and functional disorders

A **positive diagnosis** (not just exclusion): **Rome IV** — recurrent abdominal pain ≥1 day/week over 3 months, related to defecation and/or a change in stool frequency/form; subtypes IBS-C/-D/-M. **Exclude red flags** (age >50 new symptoms, weight loss, PR bleeding, nocturnal symptoms, family history of bowel cancer/IBD, anaemia) — check **FBC, CRP, coeliac serology, and faecal calprotectin** (normal in IBS). Management: education/reassurance, diet (**low-FODMAP** with dietitian), fibre adjustment, antispasmodics, and symptom-directed drugs (loperamide, laxatives, low-dose tricyclics, linaclotide).

#### 5.4 Diverticular disease and other colonic conditions

**Diverticulosis** (herniation of mucosa through the muscle wall at vessel points, commonest **sigmoid**; from low-fibre diet/raised intraluminal pressure) → **diverticular disease** (symptomatic) → **acute diverticulitis** (inflammation/infection: LIF pain, fever, raised inflammatory markers — "**left-sided appendicitis**"). Diagnose acutely with **CT**; classify complicated disease by **Hinchey** (I abscess→ IV faeculent peritonitis).
- **⚑ Management shift:** for **uncomplicated** acute diverticulitis in a systemically well patient, **antibiotics are no longer routinely required** (NICE NG147) — analgesia, fluids and review. Antibiotics/admission for complicated disease (abscess → drainage; perforation/peritonitis → surgery, e.g. Hartmann's).
- Complications: abscess, perforation, fistula (**colovesical → pneumaturia/faecaluria**), stricture, and **lower GI bleeding**.

**Other colonic conditions:** **angiodysplasia** (arteriovenous malformations, painless bleeding/iron deficiency, right colon, associated with aortic stenosis — Heyde's); **ischaemic colitis** (splenic-flexure watershed, "thumbprinting" on AXR, usually settles); **microscopic colitis** (chronic watery diarrhoea, normal endoscopy, abnormal biopsy — collagenous/lymphocytic; drug-associated, e.g. PPIs/NSAIDs); **radiation enteritis**.

#### 5.5 Colorectal cancer and polyps

Nearly all arise via the **adenoma–carcinoma sequence** (APC → KRAS → p53). **Hereditary syndromes** (worth spotting):

| Syndrome | Genetics | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **FAP** | APC (AD) | **Hundreds–thousands of polyps**, ~100% cancer → prophylactic colectomy; **Gardner** variant (osteomas, desmoids) |
| **Lynch (HNPCC)** | Mismatch-repair genes (AD) | Fewer polyps, **right-sided**, younger; also endometrial/others; **Amsterdam criteria** |
| Peutz–Jeghers | STK11 | Hamartomas + **mucocutaneous pigmentation** |
| MUTYH-associated polyposis | MUTYH (AR) | Polyposis |

**⚑ Screening (England):** **FIT (faecal immunochemical test)** home kit every **2 years**, now offered from **age 50 to 74** (the programme has been extended down from 60 to 50 — fully rolling out across the over-50s from 2025). A positive FIT → **colonoscopy**. In symptomatic patients, **FIT is also used to triage** urgent referral.

**Presentation by side:** **left-sided/rectal** → change in bowel habit, PR bleeding, obstruction, tenesmus; **right-sided** → **iron-deficiency anaemia**, weight loss, occult bleeding, later obstruction (wider lumen). **Any iron-deficiency anaemia in a man or post-menopausal woman is colorectal cancer until proven otherwise.**

**Referral/diagnosis:** 2-week-wait for red-flag features (FIT-supported); **colonoscopy + biopsy**; stage with **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis + MRI rectum** (rectal cancer) + CEA baseline. **Staging: TNM** (has largely replaced Dukes' — ⚑; Dukes A confined to bowel wall → D distant metastases). Management: **surgical resection** (the only cure) ± neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (rectal) + adjuvant chemotherapy (node-positive); palliative chemo/biologics for metastatic disease.

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — boerhaave’s syndrome — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- DEFINITION: spontaneous transmural oesophageal perforation
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: Very rare, 50-70 y/o, more common in males
- AETIOLOGY: Iatrogenic (endoscopy/paraoesophageal surgery), repeated/forceful retching/vomiting, forceful swallowing
- PATHOGENESIS:
- Excessive intraluminal pressure leads to tear
- Most frequent site in the left posterolateral aspect, 3-5cm above the gastro-oesophageal junction
- Overlying pleura can also tear 🡪 mediastinum/pleural cavity are contaminated with oesophageal contents
- Left chest is affected more than right chest
- DIAGNOSIS
- History: severe chest/neck/upper abd. pain, Hx of severe retching/vomiting, other causes of ↑ intrathoracic pressure, hematemesis not typically seen; dysphagia/odynophagia over time (+ patient deterioration)
- Examination: Obs (↓ grade fever, tachycardia, tachypnoea, dyspnoea, ↓BP), Hamman sign (air escapes into the mediastinum 🡪 heart beating against air-filled tissues🡪 mediastinal crunch)
- INVESTIGATIONS
- Oesophogram: If perforation in abdomen, use gastograffin (barium causes peritonitis); if perforation above diaphragm, use barium (gastrograffin causes pneumonitis)
- CT chest: With thoracic perforations, mediastinal widening and pleural effusion with or without pneumothorax
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: MI, pancreatitis, lung abscess, pericarditis, spontaneous pneumothorax
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT: NBM, broad-spectrum IV ABx, IV PPI, drainage of fluid collections/debridement of infected &amp; necrotic tissue, surgical consultation
- INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY: diffuse extravasation, extension of perforation, clinical deterioration (persistent fevers/sepsis), progression of pneumomediastinum/pneumothorax, empyema
- COMPLICATIONS: septicaemia, mediastinitis, massive pleural effusion, empyema, pneumothorax, sepsis, abscess
- PROGNOSIS: Dependent on early recognition and appropriate intervention (Within 24 hours= ~75% survival rate)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — coeliac disease — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- DEFINITION: autoimmune enteropathy creating abnormal small intestine mucosa due to permanent insensitivity to gliadin, a component of gluten
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: W&gt;M, most commonly diagnosed at ~40 y/o
- PATHOGENESIS: Gluten (a protein in cereal) is broken down to gliadin (toxic factor).  ↓ immune tolerance to gliadin peptide antigens 🡪 antigens resistant to human proteases 🡪 persist in SI 🡪 gain access to lamina propria via faulty tight junctions/dendritic cells 🡪 intestinal submucosa 🡪 innate &amp; adaptive immune responses 🡪 peptides deamidated by tissue tranglutaminase (tTG) 🡪 binding to coeliac-associated HLA peptides (DQ2 or DQ8) found on APCs and activation of T-Helper cells 🡪 1. Cytotoxic T cells- cell death/tissue remodelling with villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia 2. Plasma cell maturation (anti-gliadin, anti-tTG antibody production). Inflammatory process leads to disruption of small bowel 🡪 malabsorption of nutrients, minerals and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Gliadin fermented by bacteria 🡪 diarrhoea and abd distension. HLA-DQ2 (80-90% pts) and HLA-DQ8 associated with patients.
- RISK FACTORS: IgA deficiency. T1DM, AI thyroid disease, IBD
- HISTORY:Diarrhoea (loose, pale, malodorous, floats), weight loss, anaemia, vitamin/mineral deficiency, FTT, bloating, gas, abdominal pain/discomfort, fatigue. Sx ↑ with gluten-free diet
- Disease most severe in proximal bowel (mainly duodenum) 🡪 Fe, Ca, folic acid deficiency more common than vitamin B12 deficiency (absorbed in ileum)
- Gluten enteropathy may be associated with dermatitis herpetiformis skin eruption, epilepsy, myopathy, depression, paranoia, infertility, bone fractures/metabolic bone disease
- FHx autoimmune disease

- EXAMINATION: Abd exam signs of anaemia, abd distension, dermatitis herpetiformis (intensely pruritic papuloveiscular lesions that occur symmetrically over the extensor surfaces of the arms and legs)
- INVESTIGATIONSBloods- FBC (iron deficiency anaemia, macrocytic anaemia from folate deficiency), anti-tTG antibody (+predictive value), IgG-DGP (in IgA deficiency as false-neg anti-tTG), EMA (↑ specificity, ↓sensitivity)
- Evidence of malabsorption- ↓ferritin/iron, Ca2+, albumin, cholesterol, carotene, B12 absorption
- Small bowel histology (specific &amp; sensitive, duodenum, ↑ intraepithelial lymphocytes, crypt hyperplasia, villous atrophy)
- HLA typing- look for HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8, used if biopsy and serology results disagree
- Skin biopsy (d?ermatitis herpetiformis)
- CT enterography (visualise small bowel, rule out lymphoma)
- Fecal fat &gt; 7%

![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img004.jpg)

![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img005.jpg)

- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: Crohn’s disease Liver disease, eosinophilic enteritis, autoimmune enteropathy
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENTGluten-free diet in Barley, Rye, Oats, Wheat + refer to nutritionist
- Calcium, Vit D, Iron, Folate supplementation
- Check for associated osteopenia/osteoporosis using bone density scan + other autoimmune disease

- COMPLICATIONS: FTT, anaemia, osteopenia (↓ common osteoporosis due to ↓ Vit D 🡪 ↑PTH), ↑ risk of AI disease (T1DM, IBD, AI Thyroid Disease), malignancy
- PROGNOSIS: gluten-free diet resolve ~90% of patients (&lt;1% develop refractory coeliac disease where SI fails to heal)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — colorectal cancer — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- EPIDEMIOLOGY: 3rd most common cancer, ~70 y/o median age
- AETIOLOGY: Genetic factors [1. Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP); autosomal dominant affecting APC gene with ↑polyps usually in rectum, 2. Hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer (Lynch) – type I predominant colorectal cancer; type II involves other cancers especially uterine, ovarian and breast]
- Predisposing conditions (e.g. adenomas, IBD, DM, acromegaly, FHx)
- Environmental factors (↑wt, ↑energy intake, ↑inactivity, smoking, ETOH)

- PATHOGENESIS: dysplastic adenomatous polyps + inactivation of tumour-suppressor and DNA repair genes + activation of oncogenes 🡪 cells gains advantage in growth without apoptosis 🡪 adenocarcinoma (95% of case)

![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img009.jpg)

- HISTORY: constitutional Sx; frequently slow in onset, less pain and may not cause vomiting; patients either have suspicious Sx, asymptomatic individuals on routine screening OR ED intestinal obstruction/ peritonitis/ acute GI bleed

|  | Right Colon (25%) | Left Colon (35%) | Rectum (30%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Information | Asymptomatic Weakness, weight loss, occult blood loss, mucusUlcerating lesions 🡪 chronic, insidious blood loss without a change stool appearance | Constipation ± overflow diarrhea, hematochezia, abd cramps, mucusStool more formed, thus tumours tend to impede its passage 🡪 cramps, obstruction, perforation | Constipation ± overflow diarrhoea, tenesmus, hematochezia, mucus, pseudo-diarrhea |
| Signs | Fe deficiency anemia, RLQ mass (uncommon) | Bright blood on PR, LBO | Palpable mass, bright blood on PR |

- EXAMINATION: GI exam (signs of anaemia, palpable abd mass, increased liver span in metastases, Virchow’s node, DRE mass/bloody stool on glove)
- INVESTIGATIONS: FBC, Carcinogenic embryonic antigen (CEA) to monitor response of Tx, FOBT (sensitive, not specific, cheap), CT chest/abd/pelvis, colonoscopy [allows for biopsy for histological analysis; alternative: air contrast barium enema (“apple core” lesion) + sigmoidoscopy], pelvic MRI/endorectal US for rectal cancer, Coags/stool OCP &amp; MCS in active bleeding, LFTs for metastases
- STAGING: Duke’s staging is no longer used 🡪 TNM staging used
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: IBD, IBS, Diverticular Disease
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT
- Local excision or surgical resection: min 12 regional lymph nodes for staging, aim for adequate margins of excision, entails division of blood vessels, ileocolic + middle colic vessels, branches of the superior mesenteric R ant hemicolectomy – caecum, ascending colon and proximal 1/3 of transverse colon, with anastomosis
- L ant hemicolectomy –distal 1/3 of transverse colon and entire descending colon, with anastomosis
- Ant resection – resection of rectosigmoid colon, with anastomosis
- Hartmann’s procedure – resection of rectosigmoid colon with closure of rectal stump and end colostomy
- Abdomino-peritoneal resection – carcinomas in the distal third of the rectum and if low, an abdominal perineal resection (APR) with permanent end colostomy is required (esp if it involves sphincter complex)

- Adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy – mneomonic FOLFOX- FOL – folinic acid (leucovorin), F – fluorouracil (5-FU), OX – oxaliplatin (Eloxatin)
- Colostomy: Large intestine is cut 🡪 p art still ATTACHED to the colon is brought to skin through previously formed opening 🡪stitched to the skin so that stool can pass through into a disposable bag
- Serum CEA to monitor response to treatment
- CT chest/abdo/pelvis every 6 months for first 3 years
- Colonoscopy 1 year postoperatively, another 3 years later and then every 5 years. Subsequent endoscopic or radiographic surveillance of the large bowel, probably at triennial intervals, is indicated, since patients who have been cured of one colorectal cancer have ↑ risk of additional bowel cancer and adenomatous polyps

![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img010.jpg)

- COMPLICATIONS: surrounding structure damage from resection, anastomotic leak, metastases (most commonly lung and liver)
- PROGNOSIS: In stage III disease, the five-year survival rates are 44 – 83% and ↑ by 10-20^ in adjuvant chemotherapy

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — Diverticular Disease and Diverticulitis — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img011.jpg)

- DEFINITIONDiverticulum – herniation of mucosa and submucosa through the muscular layer of the colonic wall (Pseudo-diverticulum  involves only a protrusion of the mucosa through the muscularis propria)
- Diverticular disease – symptomatic diverticula
- Diverticulosis- diverticula without inflammation
- Diverticulitis – inflammation of diverticulum or diverticula

- EPIDEMIOLOGY: ↑ incidence with age
- PATHOGENESIS: ↓ fibre 🡪 ↓ stool volume and ↑ transition time 🡪 ↑ intraluminal pressure, colonic segmentation, colonic wall tension 🡪 degenerative changes in collagen and elastin content of colonic wall 🡪 diverticular formation 🡪 obstruction of diverticulum (inspissated faeces, fexolith) 🡪 bacterial overgrowth, gas &amp; toxin production 🡪 mucosal injury 🡪 perforation of colonic diverticulum 🡪 diverticulitis
- Sigmoid colon most affected but ↑ incidence on right side in Asians
- Protrusion occurs at the point where vasa recti penetrates through muscularis propria, breaking wall integrity
- RISK FACTORS: ↓ fibre, ↑ red meat, ↓ physical activity, obesity
- HISTORY: constipation; flatulence; LLQ colicky abdominal pain in sigmoid location (relieved by defecation); abrupt, painless, profuse lower GI bleeding; nausea; bloating
- INVESTIGATIONS: FBC (↑WBC in infection, ↓Hb in bleeding), CT abdomen (thickened bowel wall  &gt;4mm, masses, abscess, , inflammation with pericolic fat and collection of contrast material or fluid), other (CXR in perforation,  barium enema/colonscopy after 6 weeks of acute attack to rule out sigmoid malignancy)
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: colorectal cancer, appendicitis, ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, PID
- STAGING OF DIVERTICULITIS- HINCHEY’S CLASSIFICATION
- Stage I- diverticulitis with a pericolic abscess
- Stage II- diverticulitis with a distant abscess (retroperitoneal/pelvic)
- Stage III- purulent peritonitis
- Stage IV- faecal peritonitis
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENTConservative: ↑ fibre intake, fluid intake, analgesia, laxatives if constipated
- Antibiotics (ceftriaxone/metronidazole for gram -ve colonic bacteria + anaerobic cover)
- In acute bleeding, maintain hemodynamic stability by infusions of crystalloids, cease NSAIDs/anticoags, endoscopic haemostasis/angiographic embolisation, or surgery (percutaneous drainage, Hartmann’s with a stoma) if significant bleeding continues
- Surgery with those for at least 2 hospitalisations in past, ↓ response to medical treatment, and/or complications. Used in Hinchey Stage 3-4.

- COMPLICATIONS: diverticulitis, perforation/abscess, stricture, obstruction, fistula (into bladder 🡪 dysuria, pneumaturia or into vagina 🡪 discharge), obstruction, bleeding

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — meckels diverticulitis — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- DEFINITION: True congenital diverticulum (involves all layers). Slight bulge in the small intestine present at birth due to vestigial remnant of the vitelline duct.
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: Symptoms peak in first two decades usually ~2 y/o, most common bowel congenital malformation
- PATHOPHYSIOLOGY: Normally vitelline duct obliterates at 8th week, however a lack of regeneration causes an outpouching. If diverticula contains ectopic gastric/pancreatic cells it may secrete gastric acids or pancreatic juices, causing ulceration adjacent to diverticulum
- RULE OF TWOS: 2% of population, 2 inches (5cm) long, 2 feet (60cm) from ileocecal valve , 2/3rds have ectopic mucosa , 2 types of ectopic tissue (pancreatic and gastric), 2% become symptomatic
- CLINICAL PRESENTATION: occult haematochezia, Severe or complete constipation, N/V RIF pain
- INVESTIGATIONS: FBC, AXR (? Bowel obstruction), Meckel Scan (technetium-99m is taken up preferentially by mucous secreting cells of the ectopic gastric tissue in the diverticulum)
- MANAGEMENT: excision, lysis of adhesions/treatment of complications

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — ulcerative colitis — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- DEFINITION: IBD affecting colonic mucosa always affecting the rectum, extending proximally to caecum
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: M&gt;F, 20-40 y/o + another peak at ~60 y/o, smoking is protective
- PATHOGENESIS: involves only the mucosa, with the formation of crypt abscesses (crypts of leiberkuhn; hallmark of disease) and a coexisting depletion of goblet cell mucin
- RISK FACTORS: strong (FHx, HLA-B27, infection), others (NSAIDs, non-smoker/former smoker)
- HISTORY: rectal bleeding (hallmark feature), diarrhoea (chronic &gt; 4 weeks, with/without blood), colicky cramping abd pain, tenesmus, urgency, incontinence, systemic (fever, anorexia, weight loss, fatigue)
- ABDOMINAL EXAM: non-specific tenderness, gross/occult blood on DRA
- INVESTIGATIONS: FBC (↓Hb, ↑WCC), CMP (LFTs), ESR, CRP, stool studies (?C.Diff toxins A and B; ?acute exacerbation with infection), AXR (dilated loops with air-fluid levels 2o to ileus, thumb-printing sign), flexible sigmoidoscopy/ colonoscopy (continuous uniform involvement, loss of vascular marking, engorgement of mucosa 🡪 erythematous appearance, extent of disease), biopsies (crypt abscesses, mucin depletion); ASCA/pANCA (70% of patients +ve)
- TRUELOVE AND WITTS SEVERITY INDEX: Bloody stools/day (mild &lt;4, moderate &gt;4, severe ≥6) plus pulse, temperature, Hb, High ESR/CRP
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: Crohn’s disease, infectious colitis, diverticulitis, IBS, radiation colitis
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT
- ![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img013.jpg)
Main: 5-ASA (mesalamine) and corticosteroids, with azathioprine in steroid-dependent/resistant cases
- Other agents that can be used include ciclosporin, infliximab or methotrexate
- For severe, intractable symptoms or intolerable medicine effects, surgery should be considered. In UC, surgery is curative!
- For flares: Steroids + Antibiotics (prednisone AND metronidazole or ciprofloxacin)
- COMPLICATIONS: ↑ liver problems (primary sclerosing cholangitis), CRC (↑ risk with duration, extent, active inflammation, sclerosing cholangitis), toxic megacolon (colonic diameter &gt;6cm on AXR and caecal &gt;9 cm + systemic toxicity; most common cause of death)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — Ulcerative colitis vs. Crohn’s disease — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

|  | Ulcerative Colitis | Crohn’s Disease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Location | Rectum 🡪 proximally to caecum | Any part of GIT |
| Rectal Bleeding | Very common | Uncommon |
| Diarrhoea | Frequent, mucous, bloody, small volumes | Less prevalent, non-bloody |
| Abdominal pain | Less common | Post-prandial, colicky |
| Fever | Uncommon | Common |
| Palpable mass | Rare | Frequent in RLQ |
| Recurrent | Non post-colectomy | Common |
| Endoscopic Features | Continuous diffuse inflammation, erythema, friability, loss of normal vascular pattern, pseudopolyps | Ulcers (aphthous, stellate, linear), patchy lesions, pseudopolyps, cobblestoning |
| Histologic Features | Mucosal distribution, continuous disease (no skip lesions), gland disruption, crypt abscess, granulomas absent | Transmural distribution with skip lesions, focal inflammation ± non-caseating granulomas, fissuring + ulcerations, strictures, glands intact |
| Radiological features | Lack of haustra, strictures rare; need to rule out complicating cancer | Cobblestone mucosa, frequent strictures and fistulae, AXR: bowel wall thickening “string sign” |

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — rectal prolapse — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- ![](notes/images/general-surgery-and-gastroenterology-anu/img016.jpg)
DEFINITION: protrusion of part or all layers of the rectum through the anal canal
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: uncommon, most patients are elderly women
- TypesFalse/partial/mucosal: protrusion of mucosa only, radial furrows at junction with anal skin; most common type of rectal prolapse in childhood
- True/complete (most common): full thickness extrusion of rectal wall, concentric folds inFirst degree: prolapse includes mucocutaneous junction
- Second degree: without involvement of mucocutaneous junction
- Third degree (internal intussusception): prolapse is internal, concealed, or occult

- RISK FACTORS: age, multiparity, pelvis surgery, straining/constipation, BPH, COPD
- HISTORY: extrusion of mass with ↑ intra-abdominal pressure (straining, coughing, laughing, Valsalva), constipation, incomplete bowel evacuation, faecal incontinence, possible uterine prolapse
- EXAMINATION: DRE (protruding rectal mass, thick concentric mucosal ring), colonoscopy (assess other colonic lesions)
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: haemorrhoids, intussusception, proctitis
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT
- Type I: conservative: gentle manual reduction of prolapsed area, mucosectomy + excision of redundant mucosa
- Type II: conservative (reduce if possible, pelvic floor exercises, biofeedback retraining), surgery (indicated in faecal incontinence and/or constipation)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — anorectal abscess — Small & Large Bowel Disorders

- DEFINITION: infection in one/more of the anal spaces; usually bacterial infection of blocked gland at the dentate line
- AETIOLOGY: E.coli, proteus, streptococci, staphylococci, bacteroides, anaerobes
- TYPES: perianal (downward spread), ischiorectal (horizontal spread), intersphincteric (origin), supralevator (upward spread)
- CLINICAL FEATURES: throbbing pain (↑ straining), tender perianal/rectal mass on exam
- TREATMENT: 1&amp;D, antibiotics
