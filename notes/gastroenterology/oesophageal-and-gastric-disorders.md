## Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders


### Gastroenterology Master Notes — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

#### 4.1 Dysphagia and oesophageal motility/structural disease

**First split mechanical vs motility** (see Section 3): progressive **solids → liquids** = mechanical; **solids and liquids together** = motility.

- **Achalasia** — failure of LOS relaxation + loss of peristalsis (degenerate myenteric plexus). Dysphagia to solids **and** liquids, regurgitation, weight loss. **Barium swallow: "bird's-beak"** tapering; **manometry** is diagnostic (↑LOS pressure, incomplete relaxation, aperistalsis). Small long-term **oesophageal cancer** risk. Treat: pneumatic dilatation, **Heller myotomy/POEM**, or botulinum toxin. *(Beware **pseudoachalasia** — a fundal/GOJ tumour mimicking it in older patients.)*
- **Diffuse oesophageal spasm** — intermittent chest pain + dysphagia; "corkscrew" oesophagus.
- **Systemic sclerosis** — smooth-muscle atrophy → absent lower peristalsis + **incompetent** LOS → severe reflux/stricture (the opposite pattern to achalasia).
- **Eosinophilic oesophagitis** — atopic young adults, food bolus impaction, rings/furrows; ≥15 eosinophils/hpf on biopsy; treat with PPI, topical steroids (swallowed fluticasone/budesonide), diet.
- **Structural:** **strictures** (peptic, from reflux), **Schatzki ring**, and **oesophageal webs** — the web of **Plummer–Vinson syndrome** (web + **iron-deficiency anaemia** + glossitis; premalignant).
- **Pharyngeal pouch (Zenker's diverticulum)** — elderly, regurgitation of undigested food, halitosis, gurgling neck lump; barium swallow diagnostic (avoid blind endoscopy/NG).

#### 4.2 GORD, Barrett's oesophagus and oesophageal cancer

**GORD** — LOS incompetence → retrograde acid. Heartburn, acid regurgitation, worse lying/bending; atypical: chronic cough, hoarseness, dental erosion. Diagnosis is usually **clinical**; endoscope only if **ALARM** features or refractory. Management ladder: lifestyle (weight, avoid triggers, smaller/earlier meals) → **PPI** → consider **fundoplication** for refractory/volume reflux. Complications: oesophagitis, peptic stricture, **Barrett's**.

**⚑ Barrett's oesophagus** — chronic reflux drives **metaplasia** of lower-oesophageal squamous → **columnar (intestinal) epithelium** — a premalignant change (adenocarcinoma). Confirmed on biopsy. **Surveillance** is length- and dysplasia-stratified: non-dysplastic short segment (<3 cm) roughly every **3–5 years**, longer segments every **2–3 years**; **any dysplasia → endoscopic therapy** (radiofrequency ablation ± endoscopic mucosal resection) rather than watchful waiting. *(Older texts undertreat dysplasia — RFA/EMR now standard for low- and high-grade dysplasia. ⚑)*

**Oesophageal cancer — the treatment-defining split:**

| | **Adenocarcinoma** | **Squamous cell carcinoma** |
|---|---|---|
| Site | **Lower third** / GOJ | **Upper two-thirds** |
| Risk factors | **GORD, Barrett's, obesity** | **Smoking, alcohol**, hot drinks, achalasia, Plummer–Vinson |
| Trend (West) | Now the **commonest** in the West ⚑ | Commonest worldwide |

Presents with **progressive dysphagia + weight loss**; diagnose by **OGD + biopsy**, stage with CT/PET + **EUS**. Most present late (poor prognosis).

#### 4.3 Peptic ulcer disease, *H. pylori* and gastritis

**Two big causes: *H. pylori* and NSAIDs** (± aspirin, steroids, physiological stress). *H. pylori* (Gram-negative, urease-positive) drives most **duodenal** ulcers; NSAIDs feature more in **gastric** ulcers.

| | Duodenal ulcer | Gastric ulcer |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Commoner (~4:1) | Less common |
| Pain vs food | **Relieved** by eating (worse at night/when hungry) | **Worsened** by eating (→ weight loss, food fear) |
| *H. pylori* | ~90% | ~70% |
| Malignant potential | Essentially none | **Can be malignant — always biopsy + repeat scope to confirm healing** |

**⚑ *H. pylori* testing:** non-invasive **urea breath test** or **stool antigen** (stop PPI ~2 weeks, antibiotics ~4 weeks before, to avoid false negatives); or biopsy-based (**CLO/rapid urease test**, histology) at endoscopy. Confirm eradication with a urea breath test.

**⚑ *H. pylori* eradication (guidance has shifted — ACG 2024):** because **clarithromycin resistance is now high**, empirical clarithromycin triple therapy is discouraged. **First-line is optimised bismuth quadruple therapy** — a **PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole for 14 days** — which does not depend on clarithromycin susceptibility. Clarithromycin-based triple therapy (PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin) is reserved for regions/patients with proven low resistance and **no prior macrolide exposure**. (In UK practice, a 7-day PPI-based triple regimen is still commonly first-line per local guidance, but bismuth quadruple therapy is the salvage/high-resistance choice — flag both.)

**Complications of PUD:** bleeding (posterior duodenal ulcer → **gastroduodenal artery**), **perforation** (anterior duodenal ulcer → peritonitis, free air), **gastric outlet obstruction** (succussion splash, vomiting).

**Zollinger–Ellison syndrome** — a **gastrinoma** (± MEN1) → massive acid → **multiple/refractory/distal** ulcers + diarrhoea; **very high fasting gastrin**.

**Gastritis:** *H. pylori* (antral), autoimmune (**pernicious anaemia** — anti-parietal/intrinsic-factor antibodies, achlorhydria, B₁₂ deficiency, ↑gastric-cancer risk), NSAID/chemical, stress.

#### 4.4 Gastric cancer and upper-GI malignancy clues

Mostly **adenocarcinoma**. Risk: *H. pylori*, pernicious anaemia, smoking, salt/smoked diet, previous partial gastrectomy. Presentation: dyspepsia + weight loss, anaemia, early satiety, epigastric mass; **linitis plastica** (diffuse "signet-ring" infiltration → rigid "leather-bottle" stomach). **Metastatic clues (eponyms):** **Virchow's node** (left supraclavicular, Troisier's sign), **Sister Mary Joseph nodule** (periumbilical), **Krukenberg tumour** (ovarian mets), acanthosis nigricans. Diagnose OGD + biopsy; stage CT + EUS ± laparoscopy.

Briefly: **GISTs** (c-KIT/CD117, treat with **imatinib**) and **gastric MALT lymphoma** (*H. pylori*-driven — **eradication can be curative**).

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — GORD — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- DEFINITION: reflux of gastric contents into the oesophagus, resulting in symptoms/complications
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: 10-20% of people in Western countries
- RISK FACTORS: transient relaxation or low basal tone of the LES, hiatus hernia, acid hyper secretion, Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome (gastrin secreting tumour), ↑ BMI, smoking/EtOH/caffeine
- AETIOLOGY: ↓resting LES pressure, pathologically ↑ intra-gastric pressure
- DIAGNOSISHistory: Heartburn (retrosternal area, postprandial, exacerbated by bending/recumbence)
- Associated: regurgitation, dysphagia, metallic taste in mouth, hiatus hernia, cough (atypical), chest pain (mimic angina pectoris; uncommon), globus sensation (lump in throat; uncommon), hematemesis (O)

- INVESTIGATIONSpH monitoring:  Catheter in oesophagus for 24 hours, electrodes every 5 – 10cm apart, [atient notes time of the event/symptoms which can be correlated with pH readings and if correlating pH <4 then GORD can be expected
- Endoscopy with biopsy – patients with troublesome dysphagia, odynophagia, GI bleeding, anaemia, weight loss or anorexia
- Oesophageal manometry (assessing movement/peristalsis and pressure) – dysphagia

- MANAGEMENT:Lifestyle: ↓EtOH/coffee/spices /citrus juices; elevate head off bed, weight loss
- Medical: antacids (Mg(OH)2), H2 receptor antagonists (e.g. ranitidine), PPI (e.g. omeprazole/esomeprazole; maintenance therapy)
- Surgical: Nissen Fundoplication (Upper portion of stomach is wrapped around the oesophagus, effectively creating a new sphincter; many modifications are utilised)
- Indications for surgery include: Failed optimal medical management (most frequent indication), high volume reflux, severe oesophagitis by endoscopy, benign stricture,  Barrett’s oesophagus, upper respiratory Sx (laryngitis, nocturnal asthma, cough, aspiration)

- COMPLICATIONS: BARRETT’S OESOPHAGUS (metaplasia of stratified squamous esophageal cells into columnar epithelium with ↑ malignant potential for oesophageal cancer; diagnosed via biopsy; consider oesophagectomy in high-grade dysplasia), Erosive Oesophagitis, stricture, PUD, chronic laryngitis (△ voice quality, persistent cough, globus sensation)
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: Peptic ulcer disease, eosinophilic oesophagitis, CAD, achalasia
- PROGNOSIS: usually a good response to PPI, low adenocarcinoma risk (0.1% at 7 years follow-up)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — oesophageal cancer — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- EPIDEMIOLOGY: M:F=3:1, 50-60 y/o onset, upper (20-33%)/middle (33%)/lower (33-50%)
- Regardless of type, is aggressive and spreads rapidly due to the extensive lymphatic network in the submucosa
- OESOPHAGEAL SCC (arise from the skin cells lining oesophagus, affecting upper 2/3 of oesophagus)
- ↑ risk in males; rarely seen < 30 y/o’; most common worldwide (incidence has fallen that adenocarcinoma has become most common)
- Causative factors: Smoking, EtOH, hot liquids, underlying oesophageal disease e.g. strictures/diverticula/achalasia
- Lesions are subtle and can be missed on endoscopy
- OESOPHAGEAL ADENOCARCINOMA (arise from the glandular cells present in lower 1/3 of oesophagus)
- ↑ risk in males; rarely seen < 40 y/o
- Causative factors: Gastric metaplasia from gastric reflux (GORD), Barrett’s oesophagus, smoking, EtOH
- HISTORY: progressively worsening dysphagia (first solids than liquids) with or without odynophagia, frequently asymptomatic (late presentation), constitutional symptoms, regurgitation and aspiration, anaemia, dyspepsia, hematemesis
- DIRECT HEMATOGENOUS, OR LYMPHATIC SPREAD- trachea (coughing), recurrent laryngeal nerves (hoarseness, vocal paralysis), aortic, liver, lung, bone, celiac and mediastinal nodes
- EXAMINATION: palpable supraclavicular lymph nodes
- INVESTIGATIONS
- Barium swallow (show narrowing); endoscopic biopsy and assess resectability (early lesions appear as plaques, nodules or ulcerations and advanced lesions appear as strictures); endoscopy ultrasound EUS (visualize local disease and nodal involvement); CT chest, abdomen and pelvis or PET Scan (metastatic workup to evaluate spread to adjacent

tissues/distanct organs); bronchoscopy ± thoracoscopy (rule out airway invasion)
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: achalasia, oesophageal strictures
- TUMOUR STAGING
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENTConservative: pain management, nutrition/electrolyte/fluid replacement
- Surgical: Oesophagectomy (open vs. minimally invasive) depending on depth of tumour invasion into oesophageal wall, size of lesion, lymphovascular invasion, extent of Barrett’s mucosa, comorbid conditions and patient’s age; Endoscopic reception
- Other therapies: chemoradiotherapy, speech pathology to help with dysphagia
- Adenocarcinoma requires oesophagectomy and SCC treated non-surgically with tumour staging
- If early stage, consider an oesophagectomy (transthoracic or trans-hiatal). Stomach/colon used for reconstruction
- If locally advanced, multimodal therapy with concurrent external beam radiation and chemotherapy (cisplatin and 5-FU). May need curative esopagectomy if disease responds well.
- If there is distant metastatic disease, treat with systemic therapy and palliate symptoms (oesophageal stent)
- Adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation are recommended for post-operative node positive disease

- COMPLICATIONS: metastases – commonly in the liver, lungs, pleura and bone
- PROGNOISIS: Note prognosis is usually poor because presentation is at advanced stag (5-year survival is only 17%)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — OESOPHAGITIS — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- PATHOPHYSIOLOGY: When acid reflux from the stomach is frequent and persistent, the result is damage to the lining of the oesophagus. This is known as reflux oesophagitis or peptic oesophagitis.
- CLINICAL PRESENTATION:  heart burn, dysphagia (intermittent with first few swallows), odynophagia, chronic cough, solid foods only but can progressively worsen, PMHx of atopy, GORD or toxin ingestion
- INVESTIGATIONS: Barium swallow and X-ray or endoscopy
- COMPLICATIONS: oesophageal stricture, Barrett’s oesophagus oesophageal Cancer

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — Achalasia — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- DEFINITION: failure of smooth muscle relaxation at LES, ↑ LES pressure and progressive ↓ of peristaltic function.
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: ↑ in young women and middle-aged men
- TYPESPrimary Achalasia: unknown aetiology 🡪 inflammatory degeneration of Auerbach’s/myenteric plexus  + selective loss of post-ganglionic inhibitory neurons🡪 increased LES pressure (stimulatory neurons sole effectors of smooth muscle) 🡪 incomplete relaxation of LES with swallowing
- Secondary Achalasia: can be due to malignancy, Chagas disease, Parkinsons, myasthenia gravis or scleroderma

- NORMAL SWALLOWING: Oral phase (Tongue 🡪 food bolus 🡪 oropharynx), Pharyngeal phage (sensory signals through glossopharyngeal, vagus and trigeminal nerve to the swallowing centre in the medulla and pons causing closure of the opening to the pharynx), Oesophageal phase (vagal stretch receptors detect distension via the bolus and induce a vagovagal reflex, during which motor nerves induce a wave of contractions and causes relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter)
- CLINICAL PRESENTATION: dysphagia (starting from solids 🡪 liquids), weight loss, vomiting/regurgitation, postprandial chest pain
- EXAMINATION: oesophageal enlargement
- RISK FACTORS: Allgrove syndrome (achalasia, alacrima and adrenal insufficiency), AI diseases, FHx, Chagas disease
- INVESTIGATIONS: CXR (shows no air in stomach and a dilated oesophagus), barium studies (birds beak narrowing of oesophagus at LES, dilated oesophagus and loss of peristalsis), endoscopy to rule out malignancy, clinical features of scleroderma, oesophageal manometry
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: oesophageal carcinoma (Pseudo-achalasia when cancer invades myenteric plexus), CTD, reflux/eosinophilic oesophagitis
- COMPLCIATIONS: aspiration pneumonia, GORD, oesophagus SCC (caused by mucosal injury)
- MANAGEMENT: Ca2+ channel blockers (nifedipine), botulinum toxin A injection, LES dilation (bougie/endoscopic balloon dilatation; mechanical dilatation of the lower oesophageal sphincter using a balloon with a sufficiently forceful mechanical stretch to rupture the muscle fibres)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — barrett’s oesophagus — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- DEFINITION: metaplastic columnar epithelium replaces the stratified squamous epithelium that normally lines the distal esophagus (intestinal metaplasia)
- AETIOLOGY: Long-standing GORD (gastric acid secretion + reflux)
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: > 50 y/o, M>F, Caucasians, smokers, overweight, hiatus hernia, reflux Sx
- PATHOPHYSIOLOGY: endoscopy shows erythematous epithelium in distal oesophagus, need biopsy for diagnosis
- Pre-malignant changes categorized as low/high-grade dysplasia 🡪 can progress to adenocarcinoma
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT: 1. Acid suppression (high-dose PPI/surgical fundoplication), 2. endoscopy every 3 year if no dysplasia, 3. Regular surveillance + biopsy, endoscopic ablation/resection or oesophagectomy

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — gastric cancer — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- EPIDEMIOLOGY: 50-59 y/o, M>F
- AETIOLOGY: compensatory epithelial cell proliferation via gastric atrophy (H.Pylori gastritis, pernicious anaemia, previous partial gastrectomy), host-related (blood type A, HNPCC, hereditary diffuse gastric carcinoma HDGC, gastric adenomatous polyps), environmental (smoking, EtOH, smoked/salted food)
- PATHOGENESIS: Gastric dysplasia 🡪 gastric adenocarcinoma 🡪 early cancer limited to the mucosa + submucosa, regardless of lymph node status 🡪 malignant epithelial tumour, originating from glandular epithelium of the gastric mucosa
- CLASSIFICATIONS
- Intestinal type: irregular tubular structures (frequently ulcerative) 🡪 intestinal metaplasia commonly in lesser curvature of antrum
- Diffuse type: poorly/un-differentiated + lacking gland formation 🡪 infiltrate gastric wall 🡪 secretes mucous into interstitium 🡪 large pools of mucous/colloid in tumour cell 🡪 pushes to nucleus to periphery 🡪 Signet Ring Cell
- HISTORY: Clinical suspicion (ulcer fails to heal, lesion on greater curvature of stomach/cardia)
- Insidious onset + late presentation (post-prandial abdominal fullness, vague epigastric pain, anorexia, constitutional symptoms, burping, N/V, dyspepsia, dysphagia, hepatomegaly/epigastric mass, hematemesis, fecal occult blood, melena, iron-deficiency anaemia)
- Metastasis (distal lymph nodes, peritoneum, Krukenburg ovarian, liver, lung, brain)

- EXAMINATION:
- Palpable abdominal mass, ascitesSigns of metastases- Virchow’s node (left supraclavicular), Sister Mary Joseph’s node (periumbilical), Irish node (left axillary), Blumer’s shelf (mass in pouch of Douglas), Krukenberg tumour (metastases to ovary)
- Less commonly, Acanthosis nigricans (thickening and darkening of skin, particularly in axilla) or Leser-Trelat sign (seborrhoeic keratoses all over skin)

- INVESTIGATIONS: Upper GI endoscopy + biopsy; EUS; immunohistochemistry (HER2);  CT chest, abdomen and pelvis or PET scan- looks for metastases
- CLINICAL STAGES OF GASTRIC CANCER- TNM system used to classify cancer into rudimentary stagesStage 0 – limited to inner lining of stomach
- Stage I – second or third layers of stomach (1A) or to second layer and nearby lymph nodes (1B)
- Stage II – second layer and more distant lymph nodes, to the third layer with nearby lymph nodes, or all four layers with no lymph nodes
- Stage III – third layer and more distant lymph nodes, or to fourth layer and nearby tissues and lymph nodes
- Stage IV – spread to nearby tissues and more distant lymph nodes, or metastasised to other organs

- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: PUD, benign polyp
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENTProximal lesions: Total gastrectomy and Roux en Y esophagojejunostomy
- Distal lesions: Distal gastrectomy or subtotal gastrectomy (5-6 cm margins)
- Palliation: gastric resection to decrease bleeding/relive obstruction + radiation therapy
- Adjuvant and palliative chemotherapy
- Low grade – endoscopically; High grade – surgical

- COMPLICATIONS: metastases, malnutrition, gastric obstruction, GI bleeding, gastric perforation
- PROGNOSIS: 5-yr survival 71% for stage 1A, 56% for stage IB, 37% for stage II, 15% for stage III, and 5% for stage IV period bimbo and then just

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — complications of gastric surgery — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- The anatomical changes that result after gastrectomy affect the emptying time of the stomach. Symptoms include ↓ tolerance for large meals, rapid emptying food into small intestine (“dumping”), abdominal cramping pain, diarrhoea, light-headedness after eating, ↑HR, and sharp ↓ in blood sugars.
- ALKALINE REFLUX GASTRITIS: duodenal bilious reflux into stomach 🡪 gastritis/oesophagitis; Tx: bile acid sequestrant (e.g. H2 Blocker) + surgical (Bilroth 1/II 🡪 Roux-en-Y conversion)
- AFFERENT LOOP SYNDROME: bile + pancreatic secretions 🡪 intermittent mechanical obstruction and distention of afferent limb 🡪 post-prandial distension, RUQ pain, N/biious vomiting anaemia; Tx: conversion to Roux-en-Y
- DUMPING SYNDROME: hyperosmotic chime released into small bower 🡪 post-prandial Sx, epigastric fullness/pain, emesis, nausea, diarrhoea, palpitations, dizziness, ↑HR, diaphoresis; Tx: small multiple meals, surgery
- BLIND-LOOP SYNDROME: bacterial overgrowth of colonic gram-neg bacteria in afferent limb🡪 diarrhoea, malnutrition, abd pain, hypoCa2+, anaemia/weakness; Tx: broad-spectrum ABx, conversion to Billroth I
- POSTVAGOTOMY DIARRHOEA: bile salts in colon inhibit water resorption; Tx: medical, surgery

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — peptic ulcer disease — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- DEFINITION: Break in the mucosal lining of the stomach/duodenum > 5mm in diameter, with a depth to the submucosa
- EPIDEMIOLOGY: ↑incidence with age, M=F
- AETIOLOGY: H. pylori infection (90% of duodenal, 60% of gastric), NSAID use, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, malignancy. Only 5% of population of H.Pylori are symptomatic.
- PATHOGENESIS: Imbalance between gastric acid that damages the gastroduodenal mucosal defenses (mucus HCO3-, tight junctions b/w epithelial cells, process of restitution) that normally limits the injury.
- NSAID 🡪 inhibits COX-1 🡪 inhibits synthesis of certain prostaglandins responsible for protective mucous laye.
- H Pylori 🡪 inflammation 🡪 ↑gastric secretion + disruption of tight junctions in stomach and impairs somatostatin in antrum of duodenum 🡪 ↑gastrin 🡪 gastric acid hypersecretion.
- Gastric ulcers usually due to NSAID use and duodenal ulcer due to H. pylori infection

- RISK FACTORS: smoking, ICU patients, H.Pylori infection, NSAID, ↑ age, FHx/PMHx
- PREVENTION: careful NSAID use, prophylactic PPI (especially in ICU), avoidance of smoking/EtOH
- HISTORY: Gastric- epigastric pain exacerbated by eating (1-2 hours postprandial), heartburn, chest discomfort, early satiety, hematemesis
- Duodenal- epigastric pain 2-5 hrs after eating, melena more common (than hematemesis), heartburn uncommon

- EXAMINATION: epigastric tenderness, pallor, ‘pointing sign’ (patient pointing at site of pain with one finger)
- INVESTIGATIONS
- Urea breath test: Urea-labelled isotope swallowed 🡪 wait for 15 minutes 🡪 H.Pylori converts urea to ammonia to CO2 🡪 patient breath tested for CO2 🡪 If positive, H.Pylori positive
- Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy: Allows visualization of ulcers and also for biopsy (+ rapid urease test ); H.Pylori (single ulcer), NSAID (multiple, shallow ulcers), malignancy (heaped-up margins, necrotic centre ulcers)
- Stool haem test: often performed, but usually negative
- FBC: anaemic, evidence of GI Bleeding
- Serology for H.Pylori- can remain positive after treatment
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: stomach cancer, GORD, acute pancreatitis, coeliac disease
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT
- Goals: 1. Treat underlying cause 2. Inhibit hypersecretion of acid (pH >6  ↓  pepsin which ↓ fibrin clot degradation)
- Cessation of NSAID, smoking, EtOH if possible
- Triple Therapy (PPI + 2ABx)
- Acid Suppression- PPI e.g. emomeprazole: inhibits parietal cell H+/K+ ATPase pump which secretes acid (heals most ulcers even if NSAIDs continued). H2 antagonists e.g. ranitidine can be used; except less effective.
- If there is H. pylori– 2 antibiotics are usually clarithromycin AND amoxicillin (or metronidazole if penicillin allergic)
- In actively bleeding ulcer 🡪 blood transfusion, PPI
- In perforated ulcer 🡪 endoscopy to stop bleeding or surgery (adrenaline injection, coagulation, endoscopic clips)
- Indications for Surgery: haemorrhage, rebleed in hospital, perforation, gastric outlet obstruction, refractory to medical/endoscopic management
- FORREST CLASSIFICATION OF BLEEDING PEPTIC ULCERS: re-bleeding risk without endoscopic therapy (%)Type 1: a (spurting) – 100%; b (oozing) – 20%
- Type 2: a (visible vessel) – 50%;  b (adherent clot) – 25%; c (flat pigmented spot) – 10%
- Type 3: Clean base – 3%

- PROGNOSIS: Duodenal ulcers heal in 4 weeks and gastric ulcers in 8 weeks with PPI therapy
- PERFORATED PEPTIC ULCER: Sudden onset epigastric pain that may radiate to back and shoulders (+ diffuse as conditions worsen), syncopeExamination: diaphoresis, deranged vitals (↑HR, ↑HR, ↓temp), weak pulse, cold extremities, abdomen tender with board-like rigidity, loss of liver dullness to percussion due to peritoneal air
- Investigations: Prompt diagnosis within first 6 hours important; CXR/AXR (free air under diaphragm)
- Management: intravenous  PPI, IV ABx (gram neg cover ceftriaxone and anaerobic cover metronidazole), IV fluid, NG tube, surgery (oversew ulcer plication and omental Graham patch)

### General Surgery & Gastroenterology (ANU) — hepatocellular carcinoma — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

- RISK FACTORS: Hep B/C, haemochromatosis, cirrhosis, EtOH, smoking, DM, [note: statins are protective]
- HISTORY: chronic liver disease Sx, upper abd pain, weight loss, early satiety
- EXAMINATION: palpable liver mass, jaundice
- INVESTIGATIONS
- Bloods- FBC, LFTs, EUC
- Screening with AFP + ultrasound
- CT chest/abdo with contrast
- Percutaneous biopsy + histology: only when uncertain, risk of spreading tumour along needle track
- DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES: cavernous haemangioma, focal nodular hyperplasia, adenoma (all benign neoplasms), metastatic liver disease (from CRC, but also lung, breast, gall bladder, pancreas, ovary, uterus, kidney, prostate)
- TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT
- Resection for patients with: solitary HCC confined to the liver, no radiographic evidence of invasion of hepatic vasculature, no evidence of portal hypertension, well-preserved hepatic function
- Other treatment options include liver transplant, radiofrequency ablation, transarterial chemoembolisation
- COMPLICATIONS: paraneoplastic (hypoglycaemia, hyperCa2+, erythrocytosis, watery diarrhoea), metastases to lung/intra-abd lymph nodes/bone/adrenal gland/brain (in order), intraperitoneal bleeding due to tumour rupture
- PROGNOSIS: freq diagnosed late due to absence of Sx2 thus following diagnosis median survival ~ 6-20 months

### Peptic Ulcer Summary — Oesophageal & Gastric Disorders

**Define peptic ulcers**

- defects / erosion in the GI mucosa of the gastric or duodenal wall that extend through the muscularis mucosae into the deeper layers of the wall
- persist due to acid / peptic activity in gastric juices. (pepsin degrades proteins)
- Chronic = focal mucosal lesions that PERSIST and RECUR over time in the SAME LOCATION
- Gastric vs duodenal

**Describe the anatomy of the stomach and duodenum including where ulcers tend to form.**

Most occur in the body (particularly lesser curve) or antrum of the stomach OR the duodenal bulb (1st part). In the 2nd part of the duodenum, ulcers are abnormal because the luminal pH is neutralised by the alkaline secretions from pancreas and biliary tree.

**What are the risk factors for developing peptic ulcers?**

- helicobacter pylori infection (50% of people have this, not all get PUD)
- NSAID use
- chemical - gastric acid hypersecretion; duodenal abnormalities of bicarb secretion
- smoking - a H pylori infected person who smokes is more likely to get PUD
- stress - patients in hospital can develop stress ulcers which response poorly to therapy

**What common “risk factors” for PUD actually have no convincing data?**

- Coffee = strong stimulant of acid secretion and produces dyspepsia in many individuals, which often results from enhanced esophageal reflux . Decaffeinated coffee does the same.
- Diet = Although certain foods, beverages, and spices cause dyspepsia, there are no convincing data these cause, perpetuate, or reactivate peptic ulcers.
- Psychological distress = stress increases gastric acid secretion but there is no further evidence
- Alcohol in high concentrations damages the gastric mucosal barrier to hydrogen ions and is associated with acute gastric mucosal lesions characterized by mucosal haemorrhages. Alcohol also stimulates acid secretion. Despite these acute effects, there is no evidence that alcohol intake causes or exacerbates chronic PUD

**What is the difference between an erosion and an ulcer?**

erosions (mucosal breaks, without visible depth to the lesion)

ulcers (mucosal breaks, with visible depth to the lesion).

**Describe the normal physiology of the gastric mucosa and how this is disrupted to cause PUD.**

The stomach is an acid-peptic environment. Noxious agents are often ingested. lots of damage potential

Normal protective mechanisms exist to control gastric mucosal function and repair. Peptic ulcers form when acid erodes the mucosa ie when there is too much acid or the protective mucus layer is degraded.

*1. THE CENTRAL ROLE OF CYCLO-OXYGENASE PRODUCTS (PROSTAGLANDINS)*

- COX, the rate-limiting enzyme in PG synthesis; COX converts the unsaturated fatty acid arachidonic acid (derived from phospholipids in cell membranes) into PGG2 and then to PGH2. The gastric and duodenal mucosa convert PGH2 to various prostanoids (prostaglandins and thromboxane A2).
- PGs such as PGE2 protect the mucosal lining from injury by luminal acid-pepsin.
- There are two functional forms of COX. COX-1 is a constitutive enzyme with a fairly steady rate of expression in most body cells. In contrast, COX-2 is expressed in many cells only when bacterial polysaccharides (endotoxin), pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNFa or IL-1b, or growth factors (mitogens) induce its expression.
- The healthy gastric and duodenal mucosa constitutively use COX-1 to produce its mucosal-protective PGs while Gastric restitution has been associated with induction of COX-2.

*2. MECHANISMS OF GASTRODUODENAL PROTECTION BY ENDOGENOUS PROSTAGLANDINS *

There are also non-antisecretory effect of PGs (ie not to do with inhibiting gastrin) called "cytoprotection". Some of the cytoprotective mechanisms of PGs include:

●Stimulation of glycoprotein (mucin) AND bicarb secretion by epithelial cells

🡪 these 2 products combine to form an alkaline, unstirred water layer on the surface of the gastric mucosa, which retards diffusion of acid-pepsin from the lumen into the mucosa.

●Stimulation of phospholipid secretion by epithelial cells

●Enhancement of mucosal blood flow and oxygen delivery to epithelial cells via local vasodilation

●Increased epithelial cell proliferation AND migration towards the luminal surface (restitution)

*3. ROLE OF NITRIC OXIDE *

Generation of NO by NO synthase (NOS) may be a key intermediate in cytoprotection.

Similar to the role of COX-1, constitutive NOS is important in the maintenance of an intact mucosal lining.

Two enzymes contribute to the basal, constitutive NOS activity: neuronal NOS and endothelial NOS

The cytoprotective mechanisms of NO parallel prostaglandin (PG) effects and include:

●Mediation of the release of gastric mucin

●Stimulation of alkaline fluid secretion

●Maintenance of epithelial barrier function

●Enhancement of mucosal blood flow

*THEREFORE - *Primary malfunction of gastroduodenal secretory, defence, or repair mechanisms is a very uncommon cause of ulcer. Most ulcers occur when the normal mechanisms are disrupted by superimposed processes such as *H. pylori *infection and the ingestion of NSAIDs. Repair mechanisms include RESTITUTION: rapid migration of deeper epithelial cells lining gastric pits to cover the damaged surface and PROLIFERATION: less rapid regeneration of new epithelial cells from progenitor cells. PG (and thus COX) and NO are important to these processes.

**Explain the relevance of H Pylori co infection to the pathophysiology of PUD**

Patients may have a H PYLORI DEPENDENT HYPERGASTRINAEMIA which causes GASTRIC ACID HYPERSECRETION. However vagal hyperactivity could also cause the same thing without h pylori.

**Explain the regional differences in H pylori**

The prevalence of *H. pylori *both in the general population and in PUD patients is ↓ rapidly in developed regions, presumably due to improved hygiene and decreased *H. pylori *transmission in early childhood.

In Southern Europe and Japan, where prevalence rates for *H. pylori* in both GU and DU were high 90%, there is evidence of ↓  *H. pylori* prevalence in the population and in peptic ulcer, especially in younger patients.

**Explain the relevance of NSAID use to the pathophysiology of PUD**

NSAID affects

- prostaglandin synthesis
- amount of gastric acid generated
- integrity of mucosal barrier
- amount of bicarb and glutathione generated
- rate of mucosal blood flow

Aspirin and many other NSAIDs (eg, ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin, and ketorolac) are carboxylic acids 🡪 so they are not ionized at the acidic pH found in the gastric lumen 🡪 able to be be absorbed across the gastric mucosa. Once the drug moves from the acidic environment of the gastric lumen into the pH–neutral mucosa, the drug ionizes and is trapped temporarily in epithelial cells where it may damage these cells.

- non selective NSAIDs inhibit COX 1 and 2 and thus impair gastric PG production at even low concentrations eg ibuprofen, indomethacin, and naproxen.
- more selective COX-2 inhibitors have less suppressive effects on gastric PG synthesis and somewhat preserve the natural protection mechanisms eg celecoxib and etodolac.

Injury by NSAIDs can range from subtle alterations in gastric mucosal barrier function through microscopic damage to surface cells to gross injury visible through an endoscope or at the time of surgery for an ulcer complication. The most subtle change is DISRUPTION OF MUCOSAL BARRIER FUNCTION, manifest as ↑ mucosal permeability to hydrogen ions (which then diffuse more rapidly from the lumen into the mucosa) and to Na+ ions (which then diffuse more rapidly from the mucosa into the lumen). This disruption and the resultant damage to surface cells by gastric acid may result in macroscopic injury over time if repair mechanisms are ineffective.

Macroscopic injury by NSAIDs ranges from oedema, erythema, subepithelial haemorrhage, and erosions / ulcers. Only erosive/ulcerative lesions are considered clinically important, although lesser lesions may be precursors of erosive lesions if reparative mechanisms fail. Development of the full spectrum of lesions in the PG-depleted stomach or duodenum requires acid and pepsin.

**What is the risk associated with NSAID use?**

non selective NSAIDs a/w 1-4% per year risk of a clinically significant NSAID-induced gastrointestinal event, including gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, or pyloric obstruction. The effects of aspirin are probably dose dependent, especially in the higher dose range; doses of 75 to 300 mg daily cause a 2-3x increased risk of GI bleeding due both to upper GI ulceration and to lower GI causes. PPI use ↓ risk of ulceration and plays an ↑ role in the ↓  the rates of ulcer disease in NSAID users at risk.

**In which patients is NSAID use more likely to cause ulcers?**

- prior history of clinical PUD or PUD complications
- duration of action, dose, duration of therapy with NSAIDs
- old age (esp >75)
- co therapy with drugs that enhance toxicity
- genetic predisposition (P4502C9 polymorphism may delay metabolism)

**Describe the natural history of PUD**

~ 60 % of peptic ulcers heal spontaneously but with eradication of *H. pylori *infection, ulcer healing rates are >90 %. Even with continued PPI use, ~ 5 to 30 % of peptic ulcers recur within the first year based on whether *H. pylori* has been eradicated. ~ 5 to 10 % of ulcers are refractory to antisecretory therapy with a PPI. The risk of complications in patients with chronic PUD is 2 to 3 % per year.

**What are the complications of PUD**

- bleeding
- perforation
- gastric outlet obstruction (with pyloric stenosis)
- malignancy

**Describe the physical exam findings of a patient with PUD**

moderate epigastric pain in chronic uncomplicated PUD / severe epigastric pain in complicated PUD

signs of anaemia with a chronic bleed

signs of perf = lying still (peritonitis), rigidity, fever, tachycardia

**how is PUD diagnosed?**

- gastroscopy + biopsy (common) showing chronic inflammatory cells in lamina propria / crypts.

(note that PPI should be stopped 2 weeks before)

- h pylori testing = essential in all patients with peptic ulcers.

- invasive: biopsy with rapid urease tests are considered the endoscopic diagnostic test of choice.

- noninvasive: fecal antigen test more accurate than serum antibody test AND less $ than urea breath tests.

- fasting serum gastrin level should be obtained to screen for Zollinger-Ellison syndrome if suspected.
- barium swallow eg if adult onset pyloric stenosis and GOO is a suspected complication

**Explain the CLO test aka  rapid urease test**

CLO test (Campylobacter-like organism test)

H. pylori secrete urease which catalyses the conversion of urea to ammonia and CO2. A biopsy of mucosa is taken from the antrum of the stomach during gastroscopy, and is placed into a medium containing urea and an indicator such as phenol red. The urease produced by H. pylori hydrolyses urea to ammonia 🡪 raises the pH of the medium 🡪 colour change specimen from yellow (NEGATIVE) to red (POSITIVE).

**Explain the C - urea breath test**

Patients swallow urea labelled with an uncommon isotope, either radioactive carbon-14 or non-radioactive carbon-13. In the subsequent 10–30 minutes, the detection of isotope-labelled carbon dioxide in exhaled breath indicates that the urea was split; meaning that urease is present in the stomach and hence that H. pylori bacteria are present. Must stop PPI 2 weeks before and Abx 4 weeks before.

**Name some drugs used to treat PUD**

PPI options - nexium (esomeprazole), lansoprazole, omeprazole

H2 receptor antagonists include Zantac (ranitidine) or famotidine

**Describe the PRINCIPLES management of a patient presenting with suspected chronic PUD**

some ulcers heal on their own but most people need treatment for their PUD

- Determine h pylori status 🡪 give h pylori eradication therapy
- avoid contributing / risk factors eg smoking, NSAIDs
- anti secretory therapy PPI (OD then BD)/H2RA (at night)
- maintenance therapy in some groups + surveillance for gastric malignancy in some groups

**Describe in DETAIL the management of a patient presenting with suspected chronic PUD**

- All patients should be tested for h pylori and if found, aim for eradiation with triple / quadruple therapy
- avoid contributing factors eg NSAIDs, med comorbidities, nutrition, smoking, alcohol
- anti secretory therapy to facilitate healing -  Esp important for treating ulcers not due to h pylori - suppress acid with a H2 receptor antagonist or a PPI. only stop the PPI once ulcer healing has been confirmed by gastroscopy 🡪 Confirm eradiation 4 or more weeks after completion of therapy. length of rx:4-8 weeks for non h pylori non NSAID ulcers
- minimum 8 weeks for NSAID ulcer
- 10 - 14 days plus antibiotics for h pylori positive uncomplicated DUs
- 4-8 weeks for h pylori positive complicated DU
- 8-12 weeks for h pylori positive complicated GU

- surveillance - not needed for treated DU. Might be needed in GU - surveillance endoscopy + biopsy after 12 weeks of anti secretory therapy is suggested if there is persistence of symptoms, poor previous biopsies, unclear aetiology, risk factors for gastric cancer present, ulcer suspicious for malignancy on index endoscopy
- maintenance therapy - only for high risk subgroups

**Describe the antibiotic treatment of h pylori**

Triple therapy — First line - PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin for 10 - 14 days

- PPI (lansoprazole 30 mg BD, omeprazole 20 mg BD, somac aka pantoprazole 40 mg BD, rabeprazole 20 mg BD, or esomeprazole 40 mg 1D)
- Amoxicillin (1 g BD) (** or Metronidazole (500 mg BD) if penicillin allergic)
- Clarithromycin (500 mg twice daily) for 7 to 14 days.

Quadruple therapy — a PPI + bismuth subsalicylate + two antibiotics for 10 - 14 days

- metronidazole 250 mg four times daily
- tetracycline 500 mg four times daily ** can use doxycycline 100mg BD as substitute
- PPI
- bismuth subsalicylate (524 mg qid)

This is appropriate as initial therapy in areas in which the prevalence of resistance to clarithromycin or metronidazole is ≥15%, or in patients with recent or repeated exposure to clarithromycin or metronidazole.

**How is eradication of h pylori confirmed?**

Eradication may be confirmed by a urea breath test, fecal antigen test, or upper endoscopy performed four weeks or more after completion of therapy. Confirmation of eradication should be strongly considered for all patients receiving treatment for H. pylori because of the availability of accurate, relatively inexpensive, noninvasive tests (stool and breath tests) and increasing antibiotic resistance. Eradication should be confirmed in the following

●Patients who have persistent symptoms after H. pylori treatment for dyspepsia

●Patients who had an H. pylori-associated ulcer

●Patients who had gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma

●Patients who had resection for early gastric cancer

**Which people should be on maintenance anti secretory therapy?**

refractory PUD

H pylori negative and NSAID negative ulcer

giant (>2cm) ulcer and age > 50

**What happens when treatment of H pylori fails?**

An initial attempt at eradicating H. pylori fails in ~ 20 %; eradication rates after retreatment were 70% for triple therapy and 76% for quadruple therapy. For patients failing an initial course of therapy, we recommend an alternate regimen (triple or quadruple therapy) using a different combination of antibiotics for 14 days

●Clarithromycin should not be used unless resistance testing confirms the H. pylori strain is susceptible to the drug

●Tetracycline may be more effective in this setting than metronidazole.

●A simpler treatment regimen could improve compliance and, as a result, efficacy. emphasise compliance.

●Other combinations using different medications have been proposed, and are reserved for people who have failed twice eg  levofloxacin (250 mg),amoxicillin (1 g), and a PPI each given twice daily for two weeks

**Describe some difficulties in evaluating patients with PUD**

Two points are of critical importance in both discussing causes and in evaluating patients with peptic ulcers: exclude falsely-negative *H. pylori* status and unrecognized or surreptitious NSAID use.

- H. pylori can be difficult to diagnose in some patients; false-negative testing is an important consideration. False neg serologic tests occur in at least 10 % of patients. esp after antibiotics, PPIs, and bismuth in tests that depend upon the bacterial load of H. pylori. Similarly, acute GIB decreases sensitivity of urease testing.
- Surreptitious NSAID use is surprisingly common in the face of refractory and complicated ulcers

**How would you manage a patient with a bleeding peptic ulcer?**

- if blood seen in stool or vomit, take a  PPI, give IV fluids and blood transfusions if needed.
- if actively bleeding or at risk of bleeding soon, do a urgent gastroscopy and cauterise the ulcer, applying clips to cut off the vessels or inject adrenaline to close vessels off., argon plasma or bipolar coagulation
- if heavy active bleeding surgery might be needed - to find the specific bleeding vessels and embolise them

**How would you manage a perforated peptic ulcer?**

hole in wall of stomach or duodenum causing sudden severe abdo pain, tachycardia and LOW TEMP.

pain may radiate to the shoulder, rigidity on physical exam

treat asap 🡪 NG tube to decompress, IVF, PPI

exploratory laparotomy asap

**How would you manage a gastric outlet obstruction?**

less common complication 🡪 vomiting is most common symptom

also have early satiety, bloating, abdo pain, anorexia, nausea

insert NG tube to relieve stomach distension. Give IVF. Treat ulcer causing obstruction

- first line - supportive + medical
- second line - upper endoscopy with balloon dilatation + biopsy to rule out cancer etc
- third line - surgery

**Describe the role of surgery in management of PUD**

surgery can be used for perforated PU or GOO.

Generally, 5% of bleeding ulcers require operative management at some point.

Because of the success of medical therapy in the mx of PUD, surgery has a very limited role, usually only in emergency situations like…

- Failure to achieve haemostasis endoscopically
- Perforation (any patient with a perforated peptic ulcer w/ peritoneal signs should have exploratory laparotomy.)
- Recurrent bleeding despite endoscopic attempts at achieving haemostasis (after 2 attempts in some studies)

Most authors recommend simple oversewing of the ulcer in addition to treating the underlying *H pylori* infection or cessation of NSAIDs for bleeding PUD. Additional surgical options for refractory or complicated PUD include vagotomy and pyloroplasty, vagotomy and antrectomy with gastroduodenal reconstruction (Billroth I) or gastrojejunal reconstruction (Billroth II), or a highly selective vagotomy.

**Vagotomy**

The esophagus is retracted to patient's left, and the left lobe of the liver is retracted to the patient's right side. A transverse incision is made in the peritoneum overlying the esophagus at the hiatus in the diaphragm. This opening is widened. A right-angle clamp can be used to pass a Penrose around the esophagus. The anterior vagal trunk (left side) is then sought and separated from the esophagus. The posterior vagus is usually felt as a cord lying posterior (or right) to esophagus. After transecting the vagal trunks, the distal 5-6 cm of the esophagus should be cleared by meticulous dissection and division of nerve fibers and blood vessels. The criminal nerve of Grassi, which is a branch of the posterior vagus nerve, must be sought and divided. Careful attention to operative technique is essential to ensure complete vagotomy. The drainage procedure depends on the condition of the duodenum. Pyloroplasty is preferred by most surgeons; if the duodenum is inflamed, a gastrojejunostomy is the safest alternative.

**Pyloroplasty**

A Kocher maneuver is performed to mobilize the second part of the duodenum. Two silk stay sutures are made at the superior and inferior aspects of the pylorus. A 6- to 10-cm longitudinal incision is made, starting from the antrum and extending across the pylorus and into the first part of the duodenum. This incision is closed transversely with an inner layer of interrupted 3-0 absorbable sutures encompassing all layers; follow this with a seromuscular layer of 3-0 silk Lembert sutures.

**Gastrojejunostomy**

A loop of jejunum ~ 12-15 cm from the ligament of Treitz is lifted and brought next to greater curvature of the stomach, through an opening in the transverse mesocolon, usually to the left of the middle colic vessels. A gastrotomy is made in the prepyloric region or at the most dependent portion of the stomach. Before opening the bowel, noncrushing clamps are placed on both sides of the proposed anastomosis sides. The area of the anastomosis is isolated with moist laparotomy pads to avoid spillage and contamination. The stomach and the adjacent jejunum are opened. Using 3-0 absorbable sutures, the full-thickness inner layer is started posteriorly and completed anteriorly using inverting Connell sutures. Alternatively, a stapled anastomosis can be performed by putting one limb of stapler in the gastrotomy and the other limb in the jejunotomy sites.

**Billroth I and II Gastrectomy**

The greater omentum is identified at the greater curvature of stomach. It is separated from the proximal half of the transverse colon. Next, branches from the gastroepiploic arcade to the greater curvature are divided. The posterior wall of the first part of the duodenum is separated from the pancreas and divided with a GIA-60 linear stapler. The right gastric artery above the pylorus is identified and divided. The gastrohepatic ligament is divided proximally with electrocautery along the lesser curvature. The left gastric vessels lying along the lesser curvature are ligated. The stomach is divided with a GIA-90 linear stapler.

**Billroth I**

A Billroth I gastroduodenal anastomosis can be constructed if an adequate length normal duodenum is available. A 2-layer anastomosis is performed, with an outer layer of interrupted Lembert sutures and an inner layer of full-thickness continuous absorbable sutures.

**Billroth II**

If a Billroth II gastrojejunostomy is a chosen, a loop of proximal jejunum is selected and brought in an antecolic or retrocolic fashion toward the transected stomach. For a hand-sewn anastomosis, a posterior layer of interrupted Lembert sutures is placed. A longitudinal enterotomy is made in the loop of jejunum, and the appropriate length of adjacent gastric staple line is excised. The inner layer of continuous 3-0 absorbable sutures are placed. Then, anterior interrupted Lembert 3-0 silk sutures are placed.

For a stapled Billroth II anastomosis, stay sutures are placed so that the loop of jejunum is held adjacent to the gastric remnant. A small stab incision is made in the jejunum and at the adjacent posterior wall along the greater curvature of the stomach. The limbs of the GIA stapler are inserted and fired.

**Highly selective vagotomy**

Patient positioning and incision are the same as for truncal vagotomy and pyloroplasty. The key step is identification of the anterior nerve of Latarjet, which leaves the gastroesophageal junction and runs in the lesser omentum parallel to the lesser curvatureIn order to enter the lesser sac, the gastrocolic ligament is divided. Using a fine clamp, neurovascular branches are carefully ligated. Next, the stomach is turned upward, and posterior denervation is conducted in a similar fashion.

Complications - Diarrhea (30% after vagotomy); Dumping syndromes (10% after vagotomy and drainage procedures)

**Describe the vagal innervation of the abdominal viscera**

The left (anterior) and the right (posterior) branches of the vagus nerve descend along either side of the distal esophagus. As they enter the lower thoracic cavity, they can communicate with each other through several cross-branches that comprise the esophageal plexus. However, below this plexus, the 2 vagal trunks again become separate and distinct before the anterior trunk branches to form the hepatic, pyloric, and anterior gastric (also termed the anterior nerve of Latarjet) branches. The posterior trunk branches to form the posterior gastric branch (also termed the posterior nerve of Latarjet) and the celiac branch.

The parietal cell mass of the stomach is segmentally innervated by the terminal branches from each of the anterior and posterior gastric branches. These terminal branches are divided during highly selective vagotomy. The gallbladder is innervated from efferent branches of the hepatic division of the anterior trunk. Consequently, transection of the anterior vagus trunk (performed during truncal vagotomy) can result in a dilated gallbladder with inhibited contractility and subsequent cholelithiasis. The celiac branch of the posterior vagus innervates the entire midgut (with the exception of the gallbladder). Thus, division of the posterior trunk during truncal vagotomy may contribute to postoperative ileus.
