## Psychiatric Assessment and Mental State Examination

### Finals Tutor — Psychiatric Assessment and Mental State Examination

*Written in plain language rather than reproducing diagnostic manual text.*

#### History structure

Presenting complaint and its history; past psychiatric history (diagnoses, admissions, treatments, response, self-harm); past medical history and medications; **substance use** (always ask specifically); family history; **personal/developmental history** (birth, childhood, education, relationships, occupation, trauma, forensic); premorbid personality; social circumstances and supports.

**Collateral history is not optional in psychiatry** — insight may be impaired, and family, GP records, and previous notes often change the picture entirely.

#### Mental State Examination — the headings

| Domain | What you are describing |
|---|---|
| **Appearance and behaviour** | Grooming, clothing, psychomotor activity, rapport, eye contact, abnormal movements |
| **Speech** | Rate, rhythm, volume, tone, quantity — describe the *form*, not content |
| **Mood and affect** | **Mood** is subjective ("how do you feel?"); **affect** is your objective observation — range, reactivity, congruence |
| **Thought form** | How thoughts connect — circumstantial, tangential, flight of ideas, loosening of associations, thought blocking |
| **Thought content** | Delusions, overvalued ideas, obsessions, preoccupations, suicidal or homicidal ideation |
| **Perception** | Hallucinations (which modality), illusions, depersonalisation, derealisation |
| **Cognition** | Orientation, attention, memory, executive function; formal testing (MMSE/MoCA) if indicated |
| **Insight** | Does the person recognise the experience as illness, and accept the need for treatment? Graded, not binary |
| **Judgement** | Capacity to make reasoned decisions about their situation |

The commonest student error is putting content into the speech section. Speech = form of output; thought content = what the ideas are about.

#### Defining the key phenomena

- **Delusion** — a fixed, false belief held with unshakeable conviction, out of keeping with the person's culture and background
- **Hallucination** — a perception without an external stimulus, experienced as real and in external space (contrast with *pseudohallucination*, recognised as internal)
- **Illusion** — a misinterpretation of a real stimulus
- **Obsession** — an intrusive, repetitive thought recognised as one's own and resisted (ego-dystonic)
- **Compulsion** — a repetitive behaviour or mental act performed to reduce obsessional anxiety
- **Thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcast** — passivity phenomena, suggestive of schizophrenia

#### Risk assessment

Assess risk **to self** (suicide, self-harm, self-neglect), **to others**, and **from others** (vulnerability, exploitation, abuse).

For suicide risk, explore: ideation, intent, plan, means, preparatory acts, previous attempts and their lethality, protective factors, hopelessness, substance use, and access to means. Static risk factors (male, previous attempt, chronic illness) contextualise; **dynamic factors are what you can modify**.

Do not rely on risk-stratification tools to make the decision — they perform poorly at individual level. The exam answer is a structured clinical assessment leading to a management plan.

#### Formulation — the 5 Ps

**P**resenting problem, and factors that are **P**redisposing, **P**recipitating, **P**erpetuating, and **P**rotective. Applied across biological, psychological, and social domains. This biopsychosocial grid is the expected structure whenever asked to "formulate".

#### Mental health legislation (NSW)

Involuntary assessment and treatment under the *Mental Health Act 2007 (NSW)* requires that the person is a **mentally ill or mentally disordered person** and that **no less restrictive care is appropriate**. Detention is for assessment and treatment, not for risk alone, and is subject to review by the Mental Health Review Tribunal.

Distinguish this from **capacity**, which is decision-specific and time-specific: can the person understand the information, retain it, weigh it, and communicate a choice? A person can lack capacity for one decision and retain it for another.
