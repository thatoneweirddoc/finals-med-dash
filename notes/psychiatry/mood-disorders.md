## Mood Disorders


### Psychiatry Master Notes — Mood Disorders

*Sources: RANZCP mood disorders CPG (2015, updated 2020), Therapeutic Guidelines: Psychotropic, AMH, RACGP, TGA safety advisories (valproate; antidepressants in youth), COPE perinatal guideline, beyondblue/Black Dog, AIHW. Scope: unipolar depression, bipolar disorder, perinatal mood disorders, grief/adjustment and suicide risk — diagnosis, stratified management, and the drug-monitoring detail finals test.*

#### 2.1 Major depressive disorder

**The short version**
- A **sustained** change from baseline, **≥2 weeks**, built on two core features — **persistent low mood** and **anhedonia** — plus cognitive, somatic and psychomotor features.
- **Functional impairment is part of the definition** — feeling flat *and* no longer working, parenting or self-caring.
- Severity = symptom count + functional impact + psychosis/suicidality, and **severity selects the treatment** (§2.3).
- **Ask about past mania/hypomania before treating** — changes diagnosis and drug (§2.5).
- Organic mimics (thyroid, anaemia, OSA, alcohol) are cheap to exclude and embarrassing to miss.

**The picture.** Low mood most of the day, nearly every day, characteristically **not reactive** to what normally lifts the person; with it anhedonia — previously rewarding activity now effortful and empty. Around these cluster fatigue out of proportion to activity; disturbed sleep (classically early-morning wakening, but initial insomnia and hypersomnia both occur); appetite/weight change either way; impaired concentration (often presenting as "memory problems"); psychomotor retardation or agitation **observable to others**; and the triad of **worthlessness, inappropriate guilt and hopelessness**, ending in thoughts of death or acts.

| Specifier | Picture | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Melancholic/somatic** | Profound anhedonia, **non-reactive mood**, **early-morning wakening**, **diurnal variation worse in the morning**, psychomotor change, weight loss, guilt | Most "biological" subtype; best evidence for antidepressants and **ECT**; SNRI/TCA may beat SSRI |
| **Atypical** | **Mood reactivity preserved**, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, longstanding rejection sensitivity | Often misread as personality; raises suspicion of **bipolar II** |
| **Psychotic** | Mood-congruent delusions — guilt, poverty, nihilism (Cotard), deserved punishment | Antidepressant **alone inadequate** → add antipsychotic, or **ECT**; high suicide risk; usually admit |
| **Seasonal** | Regular seasonal onset, remission out of season, often atypical | ⚑ Rare at Australian latitudes — don't over-diagnose |

**Severity drives management.** **Mild** — few symptoms beyond threshold, function largely maintained → **therapy first-line, antidepressant not routine**. **Moderate** — clear impairment → therapy first-line, antidepressant co-equal. **Severe** — marked functional loss, ± psychosis, ± suicidality, ± food refusal → **combined** drug + therapy, admission, antipsychotic if psychotic, **ECT if speed matters**. Exam line: severity decides whether medication is offered at all; psychosis, suicidality and food refusal decide whether treatment must be *fast*.

| Differential | Discriminator |
|---|---|
| **Bipolar depression** | Any past **mania/hypomania** — ask directly, take collateral. Earlier onset, more episodes, atypical/psychotic/postpartum features, prior antidepressant switch |
| **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)** | Chronic low-grade depression **≥2 years**, no symptom-free interval >2 months; "double depression" = MDE on top |
| **Adjustment disorder** | **Identifiable stressor**; onset **within 1 month (ICD-11) / within 3 months (DSM-5-TR)**, distress disproportionate but **sub-threshold** (§2.8) |
| **Grief** | Waves centred on the deceased, **self-esteem preserved**, pleasure between waves (§2.8) |
| **Hypothyroidism** | Cold intolerance, constipation, weight gain, bradycardia, delayed reflex relaxation → **TSH** |
| **Anaemia/iron deficiency** | Fatigue and poor concentration dominate over guilt/anhedonia → FBC, ferritin |
| **OSA** | Snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence — gets treated as "depression" and fails |
| **Substance-induced** | Alcohol (commonest mimic *and* perpetuator), benzodiazepines, cannabis, stimulant withdrawal, corticosteroids — temporal relationship to use |

#### 2.2 Assessment and screening

**The short version**
- **PHQ-9** = depression-specific severity, **0–27**. **K10** = general distress, **10–50** (the instrument embedded in Medicare items and ABS surveys).
- **Two-question case-finding**: over the past month, low mood? and loss of interest/pleasure? Negative to both makes depression unlikely; either positive → full assessment.
- ⚑ **Scales grade severity and track change — they do not make the diagnosis.** Diagnosis is clinical.
- Baseline bloods exclude mimics and make prescribing safe — a short defensible list, not a shotgun.

| PHQ-9 | Band → action | K10 | Distress |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–4 | None/minimal → no treatment | 10–15 | Low |
| 5–9 | Mild → self-help, review | 16–21 | Moderate |
| 10–14 | Moderate → therapy ± antidepressant | 22–29 | High |
| 15–19 | Moderately severe → active treatment | 30–50 | Very high |
| 20–27 | Severe → combined treatment, refer | | |

**≥10 on PHQ-9** is the conventional threshold for probable major depression, and the suicidality item is followed up regardless of total. K10 is **not depression-specific** — a high score warrants assessment, not a diagnosis.

**Baseline workup.** **FBC** (anaemia, alcohol macrocytosis); **UEC** (renal baseline — essential before lithium; SSRI hyponatraemia); **LFTs**; **TSH** (the classic mimic); **BSL/HbA1c and lipids**; **B12/folate**; **iron studies**; **calcium** (mandatory if lithium planned); **βhCG** in anyone of childbearing potential; **ECG** if cardiac history, elderly, overdose risk or a QT-prolonging agent; **urine drug screen** if substances suspected.

⚑ **Not routine**: CT/MRI brain, EEG, autoimmune or infectious serology, "depression biomarkers", pharmacogenomic testing. Neuroimaging only for atypical features — first episode in the elderly, focal neurology, seizures, rapid cognitive decline or personality change.

#### 2.3 Management of depression, stepped

**The short version**
- **Mild–moderate: psychological therapy first-line** (CBT, IPT, behavioural activation), plus exercise, sleep and alcohol reduction.
- **Severe: combined antidepressant + therapy**, together, not sequentially.
- **SSRI first-line** — between-class efficacy is comparable, so the differentiators are tolerability and **overdose safety**.
- **2–4 weeks at a therapeutic dose** before judging response; continue **≥6–12 months after remission** at the dose that achieved it.
- ⚑ **Taper on stopping** — discontinuation syndrome is neither relapse nor dependence.

**The ladder.** At every severity: psychoeducation, sleep, alcohol, exercise, structured activity, treat comorbidity, safety plan if any suicidality. **Mild** — review at 2 weeks, guided self-help or digital CBT (This Way Up, MoodGYM). **Moderate** — therapy first-line; antidepressant equally defensible with prior response or preference. **Severe** — antidepressant **plus** therapy, antipsychotic if psychotic, **ECT** if urgent. **Non-response at 4 weeks** — recheck adherence, dose and diagnosis (bipolarity, alcohol, thyroid), optimise dose; **switch** at 6–8 weeks, **augment** if partial.

**Why SSRI first.** SSRIs lack the anticholinergic, antihistaminergic and α-blocking load of TCAs and — decisively where suicide risk is elevated — are **far safer in overdose** than TCAs (cardiotoxic, proconvulsant) or MAOIs (tyramine, hypertensive crisis). Choice within the class is by side-effect profile.

| Agent | Start → usual range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sertraline** | 50 → 50–200 mg | Fewest interactions; safest in cardiac disease and pregnancy — the default |
| **Escitalopram** (citalopram 20 → 20–40) | 10 → 10–20 mg | ⚑ Dose-dependent **QT prolongation** — **maximum maintenance 10 mg/day if >65 y**; in hepatic impairment start 5 mg for 2 weeks, maximum 10 mg |
| **Fluoxetine** | 20 → 20–40 mg (**registered maximum 80 mg/day**) | Long half-life, self-tapering; **best evidence under 18**; potent CYP2D6 inhibitor |
| **Paroxetine** | 20 → 20–40 mg (**registered maximum 50 mg/day for depression; 40 mg usual, max 60 mg for OCD/panic**) | Most anticholinergic, **worst discontinuation**; avoid in pregnancy |
| **Venlafaxine** (SNRI); **mirtazapine** | 75 → 75–225 mg; 15 → 15–45 mg nocte | Second-line. Venlafaxine: **dose-dependent hypertension**. Mirtazapine: sedating, orexigenic, least sexual dysfunction |

**SSRI adverse effects** — nausea (early, settles), headache, **jitteriness/akathisia in the first 1–2 weeks**, sexual dysfunction (commonest cause of covert non-adherence), **hyponatraemia via SIADH** (elderly, women, diuretics — check Na⁺ at 2–4 weeks if unwell), **bleeding** (additive with NSAIDs/anticoagulants), **serotonin syndrome** with MAOIs, tramadol, triptans or St John's wort.

**Latency and duration.** Sleep, appetite and energy often improve within 1–2 weeks; mood and cognition lag, so judge response only after **2–4 weeks at a therapeutic dose**, remission typically ~6 weeks. Then continue at the **same dose ≥6–12 months** (RANZCP: ≥6 months, up to 12, longer if recurrent). Indefinite maintenance after ≥3 lifetime episodes, psychotic episodes, or residual symptoms.

**Stopping.** Taper over **≥4 weeks**, longer for paroxetine, venlafaxine or years of use (hyperbolic taper). **Discontinuation syndrome** starts within days of a cut: flu-like malaise, insomnia and vivid dreams, nausea, dizziness, sensory disturbance (classic **"electric shock" brain-zaps**), irritability. Versus relapse — it is **early (onset within a day or two of a cut), physical rather than mood-dominant, and resolves within hours of reinstatement**. ⚑ Do **not** use duration as the discriminator: withdrawal symptoms are mild and brief only after short-term (6–12 weeks) therapy; after longer treatment they "can sometimes be severe and protracted", lasting months. Calling a protracted withdrawal syndrome "relapse" is the classic error and leads to unnecessary reinstatement or dose escalation. Not dependence: no craving, no escalation.

⚑ **Two Australian specifics.**
- **Antidepressants and suicidality in the young.** TGA-mandated warnings state antidepressants may increase **suicidal thinking and behaviour under 25**, greatest in the **first few weeks** and after dose changes. The consequence is not "don't prescribe" — untreated depression is the larger risk — but: clear indication, **monitor closely for the emergence or worsening of suicidal thoughts during the first 2–4 weeks of treatment and after any dose change, and through the initial months** — the TGA sets no fixed review interval; arrange frequent review (in practice weekly to fortnightly early on) and warn patient and family what to watch for, safety plan, limit quantity dispensed. **Fluoxetine** is preferred under 18, with therapy and CAMHS involvement.
- **Better Access.** A GP-prepared **Mental Health Treatment Plan** gives **up to 10 Medicare-rebated individual psychological therapy sessions per calendar year** (plus up to 10 group), released in blocks with a GP review between; unused sessions do not carry over. ⚑ MBS items were revised from **1 November 2025**: the **Mental Health Treatment Plan keeps its name**, and preparation items (2715/2717) are unchanged, but the **MHTP review item (2712 and equivalents) and the GP mental health treatment consultation items (2713, 277, 279, 92114/92115, 92120/92121, 92126/92127, 92132/92133) were removed** — ⚑ **2712 was the *review of a MHTP* item and 2713 the ≥20-minute *ongoing mental health treatment consultation* item; don't swap them** — reviews and mental health consultations are now billed on **time-tiered general attendance items** (e.g. 23, 36). Plans, referrals and reviews must be linked to the patient's **MyMedicare-registered practice or usual medical practitioner**. The 10 individual + 10 group session cap was unchanged.

#### 2.4 Treatment-resistant depression

**The short version**
- **TRD = failure of ≥2 antidepressants at adequate dose for ≥4–6 weeks each in the current episode.**
- Exclude **pseudo-resistance** first: wrong diagnosis (especially **undetected bipolarity**), inadequate dose/duration, non-adherence, untreated comorbidity (alcohol, OSA, thyroid, pain).
- Augment with **lithium**, an **atypical antipsychotic**, or **T3**; or switch class.
- **ECT is the most effective acute treatment for severe depression — not a last resort.**

**Augmentation.** **Lithium** — best-established, target **0.4–0.8 mmol/L** (the range Australian Prescriber gives for lithium in depression), best in recurrent depression with positive family history. **Atypical antipsychotic** — aripiprazole 2–5 mg or quetiapine; metabolic/EPSE burden. **T3** even when euthyroid. **Psychological therapy** if not yet adequately delivered — often the missing step, not a fallback. **rTMS** — MBS items **14216/14217** require: age **≥18**, a current major depressive episode, inadequate response despite adequate trials of antidepressants from **at least two different classes** (unless contraindicated), **psychological therapy already undertaken** unless inappropriate, and **no previous rTMS in any setting** (a once-per-lifetime course, plus one re-treatment course). **Esketamine** is specialist-initiated and not PBS-listed. **Refer** for diagnostic uncertainty, suspected bipolarity, psychosis, escalating risk, two failed trials, perinatal presentation, adolescents, or any consideration of lithium, ECT or rTMS.

**ECT.** A brief generalised seizure under GA with muscle relaxation, **2–3× weekly for 6–12 treatments**; unilateral (fewer cognitive effects) or bilateral (faster, more effective).

*Where ECT is **first**-line*: **psychotic depression** (remission ~90%+, far above pharmacotherapy alone); **catatonia** of any cause after a lorazepam trial; **severe suicidality** needing rapid response; **food and fluid refusal**, particularly **in the elderly**, where death is weeks away and drug latency is not survivable; severe melancholia; previous good response. Also refractory mania and severe depression in pregnancy.

*Speed*: response typically within **1–2 weeks** (often 3–6 treatments) versus 4–6 weeks for drugs — the whole rationale for early use. Relapse without continuation is high, so ECT is **always followed by maintenance pharmacotherapy**.

*Consent*: informed consent from a patient with **capacity**, assessed and documented for ECT specifically. Without capacity, ECT proceeds only under the **mental health legislation of the relevant state or territory** — ⚑ requirements differ across Australia but generally need authorisation by a **mental health tribunal/board** with independent psychiatric opinion, and several jurisdictions bar ECT in minors without it.

*Adverse effects*: those of a brief GA (the main mortality risk), post-ictal headache, myalgia, nausea, transient confusion. Cognitively — **anterograde amnesia** (difficulty forming new memories during the course, resolving over days–weeks) and **retrograde amnesia** (loss of memories from around the course; largely recovers but **some autobiographical loss may be permanent**), which must be consented for explicitly and is reduced by unilateral brief-pulse placement. ⚑ **No absolute contraindication** — raised ICP, recent MI/stroke and unstable aneurysm are relative.

#### 2.5 Bipolar affective disorder

**The short version**
- **Bipolar I** = at least one manic episode (depression not required). **Bipolar II** = at least one hypomanic **and** one major depressive episode, never mania.
- Bipolar spends most of its course **depressed** — which is why it presents as "depression" and gets mistreated.
- **Rapid cycling** = ≥4 episodes in 12 months. **Mixed features** = both poles at once, highest suicide risk.
- ⚑ **The trap: antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar depression can precipitate a manic switch or rapid cycling.**

| Feature | Hypomania | Mania |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | ≥4 consecutive days | **≥1 week** (or any length if admitted) |
| **Psychosis** | **Never** — its presence makes it mania by definition | May be present (grandiose, persecutory) |
| **Function** | Change obvious to others, function preserved or enhanced | **Marked** impairment of work/social/family function |
| **Hospitalisation** | Not required | Often required |
| **Insight** | Often partial | Usually absent |

Shared content: elevated, expansive or **irritable** mood; increased goal-directed activity; **reduced need for sleep** (not insomnia — rested on 3 hours); pressured speech; flight of ideas; distractibility; grandiosity; disinhibition. **Reduced sleep need and increased goal-directed activity discriminate best**; euphoria alone does not.

**Mixed features** — mania with prominent depression, or depression with racing thoughts, agitation and distractibility. Dangerous because it pairs **hopelessness with energy and disinhibition** → highest suicide risk of any bipolar state, and antidepressants worsen it. **Rapid cycling** — associated with **antidepressant exposure**, hypothyroidism and substance use; predicts poorer lithium response.

⚑ **The examinable trap.** Known bipolar patient presents depressed; the tempting answer is "start an SSRI". **Antidepressant monotherapy risks switching into mania/hypomania and destabilising into rapid cycling.** Instead: (1) **optimise a mood stabiliser or atypical antipsychotic first** — quetiapine and lurasidone have the best evidence in bipolar depression, with lithium and lamotrigine (the latter for the depressive pole); (2) if an antidepressant is genuinely needed, use it **only under adequate mood-stabiliser cover**, prefer SSRI over SNRI/TCA (higher switch risk), for the shortest effective period, watching for emerging mania; (3) **never** with mixed features or rapid cycling; (4) screen every depressed patient for past hypomania **before** prescribing — failure to ask is the commonest way an iatrogenic switch is produced.

#### 2.6 Management of bipolar disorder

**The short version**
- **Acute mania**: stop antidepressants, **atypical antipsychotic ± mood stabiliser**, benzodiazepine for sedation and sleep, admission (often involuntary) if severe.
- **Bipolar depression**: quetiapine or lurasidone; lithium or lamotrigine; antidepressants only under cover.
- **Maintenance**: lithium first-line and the **only agent with robust anti-suicide evidence**.
- Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index — level **12 hours post-dose**, toxicity above ~1.5 mmol/L.
- ⚑ **Valproate is effectively contraindicated in anyone of childbearing potential.**

**Acute mania.** Sedate, restore sleep (deprivation propagates mania), reduce stimulation, stop antidepressants and any stimulant, steroid or substance driving it. **Atypical antipsychotic** (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole) gives fastest behavioural control; add **lithium or valproate**, with **benzodiazepines** for agitation and sleep. **ECT** if refractory, or if exhaustion/dehydration makes it urgent. Severe mania usually needs inpatient, often **involuntary**, care given absent insight. **Bipolar depression** — quetiapine or lurasidone first-line, lithium (also reduces suicide risk), lamotrigine for the depressive pole; **not** antidepressant monotherapy. **Maintenance** — continue what achieved remission (lithium first-line for both poles), plus psychoeducation, regular sleep–wake routine, relapse-signature identification and adherence support.

**Lithium.** *Indications*: acute mania, maintenance prophylaxis of both poles, augmentation in TRD, **suicide risk reduction**.

⚑ *Therapeutic range — sources differ, a common exam trap.* Australian Prescriber gives **0.6–0.8 mmol/L maintenance** (0.6–1.0 acute mania); many hospital protocols quote **0.8–1.2 acute mania** and **0.4–1.0 maintenance**. Safe answer: **maintenance ~0.6–0.8, acute mania up to ~1.0–1.2, lower (0.4–0.6) in the elderly.**

*Level timing*: **exactly 12 hours after the last dose** (morning sample for nocte dosing), for once- and twice-daily regimens alike. Steady state at 5–7 days (7–10 if elderly or renally impaired) → check **5–7 days after starting and after each dose change**.

| Test | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Baseline | UEC/eGFR, TFT, **calcium**, FBC, BSL, lipids, ECG, **βhCG** |
| Lithium level | 5–7 days after start/change, then **3–6 monthly** once stable (Australian Prescriber sequences baseline → 7 d → 14 d → 28 d → 3, 6, 12 months → annually) |
| **Renal** (UEC, eGFR) | **3–6 monthly** |
| **Thyroid** (TSH) | **6-monthly** |
| **Calcium** | **6–12 monthly** |
| ECG, weight/metabolic | Annually |

*Adverse effects*: fine tremor, polyuria/polydipsia (**nephrogenic DI**), weight gain, acne/psoriasis, **hypothyroidism**, **hyperparathyroidism with hypercalcaemia**, long-term **chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy**.

*Toxicity* — usually above **~1.5 mmol/L**. Mild (1.5–2.0): coarsening tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, blurred vision. Moderate (2.0–2.5): **coarse tremor**, ataxia, dysarthria, hyperreflexia, myoclonus, confusion. Severe (>2.5): seizures, arrhythmias, renal failure, coma, death. *Precipitants* — **dehydration**, **vomiting/diarrhoea**, **NSAIDs**, **ACE inhibitors/ARBs**, **thiazides**, sodium restriction, AKI; lithium is handled like sodium by the proximal tubule, so anything causing sodium avidity increases reabsorption. *Management* — stop lithium, **IV normal saline**, levels 4–6 hourly with UEC and ECG, **haemodialysis** if >4 mmol/L (or >2.5 with significant symptoms or renal failure); levels **rebound after dialysis**. ⚑ **Activated charcoal does not bind lithium.**

*Pregnancy*: first-trimester exposure is associated with cardiac malformations including **Ebstein anomaly**. The classic "400-fold increase" is a gross overestimate. Cohort data give cardiac malformations in **~2.4% of lithium-exposed** vs **~1.2% of unexposed** live births (adjusted RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.02–2.68); meta-analysis gives cardiac malformation OR 1.86 (1.16–2.96) and any congenital anomaly OR 1.81 (1.35–2.41). **Dose-dependent**: adjusted RR versus *unexposed* is 1.11 at ≤600 mg/day, 1.60 at 601–900 mg/day and **3.22 (1.47–7.02) at >900 mg/day**. See §2.7.

**Sodium valproate.** Effective in acute mania, maintenance and rapid cycling. Adverse effects: weight gain, tremor, hair loss, sedation, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, PCOS.

⚑ **Pregnancy-prevention requirements (TGA).** The highest-risk psychotropic teratogen in common use: **major congenital malformations ~3.3% at <1100 mg/day, rising to ~30% at ≥1100 mg/day**, including neural tube defects, plus dose-related neurodevelopmental harm (intellectual disability OR ~2.4–4.5, autism OR ~1.7–4.4, ADHD OR ~1.4–1.8). Therefore **do not use valproate for bipolar disorder in anyone pregnant or of childbearing potential** unless nothing else works; if used, ensure **effective contraception is co-prescribed**, **documented counselling about teratogenic and neurodevelopmental risk**, a **prescriber experienced in managing epilepsy or bipolar disorder**, and pregnancy excluded before starting. ⚑ Australia has **no formal Pregnancy Prevention Programme** — the *annual risk acknowledgement form* signed by patient and specialist is a **UK MHRA / Irish HPRA** requirement, not a TGA one. ⚑ The TGA also advises **males** to use effective contraception during treatment and for **3 months after stopping**, over an unconfirmed neurodevelopmental signal.

**Lamotrigine.** Best for the **depressive pole** and prophylaxis; ineffective in acute mania. ⚑ **The rash rule**: **SJS/TEN and DRESS** risk is driven by starting dose and titration speed, so **25 mg daily for 2 weeks, then 50 mg daily for 2 weeks**, then stepwise to a usual 200 mg daily. ⚑ **Halve dose and titration rate with valproate** (which doubles levels — start 25 mg *alternate* days); **double it with carbamazepine**. **Any rash → stop immediately**, especially with mucosal involvement, purpura or fever. If stopped >~5 days, restart the full titration.

**Carbamazepine.** Second/third-line. A **potent CYP3A4 inducer that also induces its own metabolism (autoinduction)** — levels fall over the first weeks. Reduces efficacy of **hormonal contraception**, **warfarin/DOACs**, other anticonvulsants (**lowers lamotrigine**) and many antipsychotics. Also hyponatraemia, agranulocytosis/aplastic anaemia, hepatitis, and ⚑ **SJS/TEN strongly linked to HLA-B*15:02.** The Australian position (Epilepsy Society of Australia) is that testing **should be *considered*** before first prescribing carbamazepine **in patients of Han-Chinese ethnicity**. The broader list (South Asian Indian, Filipino, Malaysian, Thai) is the **US FDA** recommendation, not the Australian one. Teratogenic (NTDs).

#### 2.7 Perinatal mental health

**The short version**
- **Baby blues** — ~50–80%, day **3–5**, gone by **day 10–14**, reassurance only.
- **Postnatal depression** — ~1 in 7 Australian mothers; a major depressive episode needing treatment.
- **Puerperal psychosis** — **1–2 per 1000**, first **2 weeks**, a **psychiatric emergency**.
- **EPDS ≥13** is the referral threshold; **any positive score on the self-harm item is actioned regardless of total**.
- **Valproate contraindicated**; **sertraline** the usual first choice in pregnancy and breastfeeding (practice convention — COPE recommends SSRIs as a class, naming no preferred agent).

| | Baby blues | Postnatal depression | Puerperal psychosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | ~50–80% | ~10–15% (≈1 in 7) | **1–2 per 1000** |
| **Onset** | Day **3–5** | Any time in the first 12 months, commonly 1–3 months; often starts antenatally | **First 2 weeks** |
| **Duration** | Self-limiting by **day 10–14** | Months if untreated | Days–weeks with treatment |
| **Features** | Tearfulness, lability, irritability, poor sleep — mood **reactive**, function preserved | Anhedonia, "bad mother" guilt, impaired bonding, anxiety about the infant, suicidal ideation | **Rapid onset, marked fluctuation**, confusion, delusions often about the infant, mania or severe depression |
| **Action** | Reassure, support, review | Assess and treat — therapy ± SSRI, review risk | **EMERGENCY** — same-day assessment, almost always admission |

**Puerperal psychosis.** Strongest risk factor is **pre-existing bipolar disorder** (postpartum psychotic episode risk **~30%**) or **previous puerperal psychosis** (**>50%**). Risk is to **both mother and infant** — infanticidal ideation may be concealed and delusionally framed, and maternal suicide is a leading cause of perinatal maternal death in Australia. Manage with **urgent admission, ideally to a mother–baby unit**, as acute affective psychosis — antipsychotic ± mood stabiliser, benzodiazepine, **ECT if severe, catatonic or refusing food/fluid**. Exclude organic causes (sepsis, eclampsia, thyroiditis) first. Prophylaxis at delivery in known bipolar reduces recurrence.

**EPDS.** A 10-item self-report screen (**0–30**), validated antenatally and postnatally, deliberately excluding somatic items pregnancy confounds. COPE guidance: screen **at least once, preferably twice, antenatally and again postnatally — ideally 6–12 weeks after birth**. **≤9** routine care; **10–12** monitor and **repeat in 2–4 weeks**; **≥13** flags possible depressive disorder — antenatally repeat in 2–4 weeks and refer if still ≥13, postnatally **arrange referral or ongoing care**. ⚑ **Any score >0 on the self-harm item** → assess the safety of the woman **and any children in her care** immediately, **irrespective of total**. ⚑ EPDS **screens, it does not diagnose**.

**Prescribing.** Governing principle: **untreated maternal illness is itself harmful** — poor antenatal care, substance use, preterm birth, impaired attachment, maternal suicide — so do not reflexively stop effective treatment. **Sertraline** is widely used first in pregnancy and breastfeeding (extensive data, low milk transfer) — but note this is **practice convention and AMH/product-level data, not a named guideline recommendation**: the COPE 2023 Australian guideline recommends **SSRIs as a class** as first-line and deliberately does **not** nominate a preferred agent; **avoid paroxetine**. SSRIs carry small risks of **persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn** and **neonatal adaptation syndrome** (jitteriness, feeding and respiratory difficulty, self-limiting) — do not stop abruptly before delivery. **Lithium**: continue in severe bipolar where relapse risk is high, since destabilisation usually outweighs the ~2.4% cardiac malformation risk (vs ~1.2% background) — lowest effective dose, **monitor levels more often** (clearance rises 24–36% in pregnancy then falls sharply post-delivery: hold or reduce around labour, recheck within 24 h), **fetal echocardiography at ~18–20 weeks**. **Valproate contraindicated**; **carbamazepine** avoided (NTDs); **lamotrigine** safer but levels **fall substantially in pregnancy** and rebound postpartum. Olanzapine and quetiapine have the most antipsychotic safety data. **ECT** is safe where drug exposure is the concern; therapy is first-line for mild–moderate perinatal depression. **Mother–baby unit admission** where severe illness would otherwise separate mother and infant; mandatory child-protection reporting if the infant is at risk.

#### 2.8 Grief, loss and adjustment

**The short version**
- **Normal grief** is not a disorder, has no fixed timetable, and comes in **waves triggered by reminders**, with **self-esteem preserved**.
- **Prolonged grief disorder** — disabling grief persisting **beyond 6 months (ICD-11)** or **beyond 12 months in adults / 6 months in children and adolescents (DSM-5-TR)**, dominated by yearning and preoccupation.
- **Depression** after a loss is diagnosed on its own features — pervasive mood, global anhedonia, worthlessness beyond the loss.
- **Adjustment disorder** — a disproportionate but sub-threshold reaction to an identifiable stressor.
- ⚑ **Don't medicate normal grief.**

| | Normal grief | Prolonged grief disorder | Major depression |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | The deceased and the loss | The deceased — intense yearning, preoccupation, disbelief | **The self** — worthlessness, guilt, hopelessness |
| **Mood** | **Waves/pangs**, triggered; pleasure between them | Persistent yearning, numbness, difficulty re-engaging | **Pervasive**, unremitting; **global anhedonia** |
| **Self-esteem** | **Preserved** | Largely preserved | **Impaired** — self-loathing |
| **Death wishes** | "I'd rather be with them" — passive | Wish to join the deceased | Belief of deserving death; active plans; may be delusional |
| **Course/treatment** | Improves gradually; support and normalisation | **≥6 months (ICD-11) / ≥12 months in adults (DSM-5-TR)** disabling; grief-focused CBT | Persistent; treat as §2.3 |

**Adjustment disorder.** A maladaptive emotional or behavioural reaction to an **identifiable psychosocial stressor** (illness diagnosis, relationship breakdown, job loss), beginning **within 1 month of the stressor (ICD-11) / within 3 months (DSM-5-TR)**, with disproportionate distress or functional impairment, **not meeting criteria for another disorder**. A diagnosis of exclusion — check it is not an MDE, anxiety disorder or PTSD. Typically resolves within ~6 months of the stressor ending. Subtypes follow the predominant affect: depressed, anxious, mixed, or conduct disturbance.

**When to medicate.** For normal grief and most adjustment disorders, **no antidepressant** — they do not accelerate grief resolution and they medicalise a normal process. Offer psychoeducation, practical support, problem-solving, sleep hygiene, cultural and religious supports, and brief therapy where distress is significant; a few nights of a hypnotic is occasionally defensible. **Do** treat when a full **major depressive episode** supervenes — pervasive anhedonia, worthlessness, psychomotor retardation, psychosis or active suicidality after a loss is depression, not grief.

#### 2.9 Suicide and self-harm in mood disorders

**The short version**
- **2024 (latest ABS release): 3,307 deaths by suicide in Australia (≈9/day), age-standardised rate 11.8 per 100,000, ~76% male** (2023: 3,214 deaths, 11.8 per 100,000, 75% male). Suicide remains **the leading cause of death in people aged 15–44**.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rates are over 2.5× non-Indigenous**, peaking at 25–44 years.
- **Previous attempt is the strongest single predictor**; mixed states carry the highest risk of any mood state.
- ⚑ **Risk-stratification scales do not predict individual suicide and must not allocate care** (RANZCP explicit) — use **collaborative safety planning**.
- **The post-discharge period is the highest-risk window** of the entire illness course.

**Risk factors.** *Historical* — **previous attempt (strongest)**, self-harm history, family history of suicide, childhood abuse. *Clinical* — mood disorder with **mixed states highest**, substance use (especially alcohol), command hallucinations, personality disorder, recent discharge or medication change. *Demographic* — male (higher completion; women higher attempt rates), men 40–59, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, LGBTIQ+, rural/remote, veterans, people in custody. *Psychological* — **hopelessness** (a better predictor than depression severity), impulsivity, perceived burdensomeness, agitation, severe insomnia. *Situational* — recent relationship, job, financial or legal loss; isolation; **access to means**. *Protective* — family connection, dependent children, engagement with care, reasons for living.

**Safety planning** — a brief **written plan made *with* the patient**, evidence-supported and superior to a "no-suicide contract" (no evidence, no place). Six components: personal **warning signs**; **internal coping strategies**; **distracting settings and people**; **people to contact**, named with numbers; **professionals and services** — GP, case manager, state mental health line, Lifeline 13 11 14, 13YARN, 000; and **making the environment safe**.

**Means restriction** is one of the few interventions with population-level evidence. Remove or secure firearms with family involvement; **limit quantities dispensed**, use staged supply or family-held medication; ⚑ prefer **SSRIs over TCAs** where overdose risk is real; secure ligature points. Structural measures — bridge barriers, pack-size limits — have reduced deaths.

**Follow-up after discharge.** ⚑ The **first week and first month after discharge from an inpatient unit or ED are the highest-risk period of the whole illness course**. So: arrange **contact within 7 days** (many services aim for 24–72 hours) as an actual appointment, not a letter; discharge summary to the GP **same day** naming follow-up, medications and warning signs; involve family/carers with consent; use **assertive follow-up** — non-attendance is a risk marker, not a discharge from care; add brief-contact interventions (caring letters, texts, calls), which cheaply reduce repetition. **Lithium** and **clozapine** are the two psychotropics with direct anti-suicide evidence.

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Early-morning wakening, diurnal variation worse in the morning, non-reactive mood | Melancholic depression — best antidepressant/ECT responder |
| Hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, mood still **reactive** | Atypical depression — suspect bipolar II |
| PHQ-9 range and threshold for probable major depression; K10 "very high" | 0–27, **≥10**; K10 10–50, very high **30–50** |
| EPDS score prompting referral, and the item actioned regardless of total | **≥13**; the self-harm item — any positive score |
| Time to judge antidepressant response; how long to continue after remission | 2–4 weeks at therapeutic dose; **6–12 months** |
| Brain zaps, dizziness, flu-like symptoms days after a dose cut | Discontinuation syndrome — not relapse, not dependence |
| TGA antidepressant suicidality warning age; preferred SSRI under 18 | **Under 25**; **fluoxetine** |
| Psychotic depression, catatonia, food refusal in the elderly, severe suicidality | **ECT — first-line, not a last resort**; response in 1–2 weeks |
| The two ECT memory effects, and which may persist | Anterograde (resolves); **retrograde** (autobiographical loss may be permanent) |
