## Anaphylaxis ⚑ (emergency — highest yield in this section)


### Immunology & Allergy Master Notes — Anaphylaxis ⚑ (emergency — highest yield in this section)

**Definition:** a severe, life-threatening, generalised **Type I** hypersensitivity reaction — sudden onset with rapidly progressing **Airway, Breathing and/or Circulation** problems, usually with skin/mucosal changes.

**Recognition:**

- Sudden onset and rapid progression after exposure (food, drug, insect sting, latex, contrast).
- **A** — swelling, hoarseness, stridor. **B** — wheeze, tachypnoea, fatigue, hypoxia. **C** — hypotension, tachycardia, pallor, collapse.
- ⚑ **Skin/mucosal changes are absent in 10–20% of cases** — their absence does not exclude anaphylaxis.
- ⚑ **Isolated GI symptoms without A/B/C problems do not usually indicate anaphylaxis** (though abdominal pain and vomiting can feature in sting/venom anaphylaxis).

**Immediate management (Resuscitation Council UK 2021):**

1. Remove the trigger if possible (stop the infusion; remove the stinger).
2. **Position** — ⚑ **lie the patient flat** (legs raised if hypotensive), or sit up if breathing is the dominant problem. **Do not stand or walk the patient** — postural change from supine to standing is associated with cardiovascular collapse and death.
3. **IM adrenaline immediately** into the **anterolateral thigh (middle third)** — the single most important intervention, given even if IV access is available.
4. High-flow oxygen, IV fluid bolus for hypotension.
5. **Repeat IM adrenaline after 5 minutes** if A/B/C problems persist.
6. If refractory after two doses → senior/critical care help, IV adrenaline infusion by an experienced clinician only.

**IM adrenaline doses (1:1000):**

| Age | Resuscitation Council UK dose |
|---|---|
| Adult and child >12 years | **500 micrograms (0.5 mL)** |
| Child 6–12 years | 300 micrograms |
| Child 6 months–6 years | 150 micrograms |
| Child <6 months | 100–150 micrograms |

*(⚑ ANZCOR/ASCIA use a weight-based structure: 0.5 mg if >50 kg and ≥12 years, 0.3 mg for >20 kg/≥5 years, 0.15 mg for 7.5–20 kg — the adult dose agrees; know the local framing. Auto-injectors commonly deliver 300 micrograms, with 500-microgram devices available.)*

**⚑ What has changed from older textbooks:**

- **Corticosteroids are no longer routinely recommended** in the initial management of anaphylaxis — no good evidence they prevent biphasic reactions, and early steroid use may be associated with worse outcomes.
- **Antihistamines are not a treatment for anaphylaxis** — may be used afterwards for cutaneous symptoms only, and must never delay adrenaline.
- **Fixed observation periods have been replaced by risk-stratified observation.**

**Risk-stratified observation after resolution (RCUK 2021 / NICE NG258):**

| Observe for | When |
|---|---|
| **2 hours** | Good response to a single dose of adrenaline, prompt treatment, complete resolution, access to help, adequate supervision |
| **6 hours** | Two doses of IM adrenaline needed, or previous biphasic reaction |
| **12 hours** | >2 doses needed, severe asthma or severe respiratory compromise, possible continuing allergen absorption (e.g. slow-release medicine), late-night presentation, or difficult access to emergency care |

**Biphasic reactions** occur in roughly **5%**, typically recurring hours after apparent resolution without further exposure — this is the reason for observation.

**Follow-up (the marks people drop):** **serum mast cell tryptase** — ideally as soon as feasible after treatment, a second sample 1–2 hours later, and a **baseline sample ≥24 hours** after resolution; adrenaline auto-injector prescription with training; written emergency plan; referral to a specialist allergy clinic; document the trigger clearly in the notes and allergy record.
