### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — Rheumatoid arthritis — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Symmetric erosive synovitis of small joints — MCP, PIP, MTP, **not DIP**; morning stiffness >1 hour that improves with use.
- HLA-DR4/DR1 ⚠ **SOURCE ERROR — this source claims ~93%, which is implausibly high — HLA-DRB1 shared-epitope alleles are present in roughly **60–70%** of RA patients and are common in the general population.**; RF 80% sensitive but non-specific, **anti-CCP specificity >90%**.
- X-ray = **LESS** (loss of joint space, erosions, soft tissue swelling, soft bones); MRI if cervical spine involved.
- "Window of opportunity" — treat within 3 months; methotrexate first line, folic acid 24–48 h after.

| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | F>M, peak incidence ~ 20-40 years |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — scleroderma — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Non-inflammatory autoimmune small-vessel vasculopathy + fibroblast dysfunction → skin and organ fibrosis; HLA-DR1.
- **CREST**: calcinosis cutis, Raynaud's, (o)esophageal hypomotility, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia.
- Antibodies discriminate the subtype: **limited → anticentromere**; **diffuse → anti-Scl-70 / anti-RNA polymerase III**; ANA positive in 90%.
- Treatment is organ-directed — PPI for GORD, **ACE-I for renal hypertensive crisis**, immunosuppression e.g. MTX.

| Definition | Abnormal growth of connective tissue → diffuse thickening, hardening of skin/inner organs |
| --- | --- |
| Pathophysiology | Non-inflammatory autoimmune disorder characterised by widespread small vessel vasculopathy, production of autoantibodies and fibroblast dysfunction causing fibrosis |
| Aetiology | Associated with HLA-DR1 |
| Presentation | **CREST Syndrome**<br>• C- calcinosis cutis<br>• R- Raynaud’s phenomenon<br>• E- Esophageal hypomotility<br>• S- Sclerodactyly<br>• T- Telangiectasia |
| Investigations | • ANA (90% of cases)<br>• Limited SSc: anticentromere antibodies (ACA)<br>• Diffuse SSc: Anti-Scl070, Anti-RNA polymerase III<br>• Serum protein electrophoresis: ↑ γ-globulins<br>• CXR: pulmonary involvement and other tests based on organ-specific symptoms |
| Treatment | • General: PT, prevent dry skin<br>• Immunosuppressants e.g. MTX<br>• Organ-specific e.g. PPIs in cases of GORD, ACE-I for renal HTN crisis |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — Introduction — Additional Notes

- Heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases, all characterized by inflammation of blood vessels (vasculitis) and subsequent ischemia and damage to the organs supplied by these vessels.
- PRIMARY: idiopathic
- SECONDARY: response to infection e.g. hepatitis B, hepatitis C

**The short version**
- Vasculitis = vessel-wall inflammation → ischaemia and damage to the organs supplied; primary (idiopathic) or secondary (e.g. hepatitis B/C).
- Sorted by calibre: large (GCA, Takayasu), medium (PAN, Kawasaki, Buerger), small (GPA, MPA, EGPA, HSP).
- ANCA pattern splits the small-vessel group: **c-ANCA/PR3 → GPA**; **p-ANCA/MPO → MPA and EGPA**; HSP is IgA immune complex.
- Corticosteroids ± cyclophosphamide recur as treatment; Kawasaki is the exception (IVIG + aspirin), Buerger's is smoking cessation.

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — ankylosing spondylitis — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Seronegative inflammatory arthritis of sacroiliac joints and spine with enthesitis; M>F, 15–40 years, **HLA-B27 in 90–95%**.
- Six "A"s: atlanto-axial subluxation, anterior uveitis, apical fibrosis, aortic regurgitation, amyloidosis (renal), Achilles/enthesitis.
- RF and ANA **negative**; X-ray shows sacroiliitis then bamboo spine, but **MRI is best for early detection**.
- NSAIDs (e.g. indomethacin) first line → TNF-α inhibitor, sulfasalazine; physio, deep breathing, smoking cessation throughout.

| Definition | Seronegative chronic inflammatory arthritis of sacroiliac joints and vertebrae with enthesitis |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | M>F, age 15- 40 years old |
| Aetiology | 90-95% of people are HLA-B27 positive |
| Pathophysiology | Inflammation → osteopenia → erosion → ossification → osteoproliferation (syndesmophytes) |
| Classification |  |
| Presentation (6 “A”’s) | • ![](notes/images/rheumatology-and-immunology-anu/img007.jpg) ATLANTO-AXIAL SUBLUXATION: back pain, morning stiffness > 1 hour, night pain, tenderness over sacroiliac joints, limited mobility of spine <br>• ANTERIOR UVEITIS<br>• APICAL FIBROSIS<br>• AORTIC REGURGITATION<br>• AMYLOIDOSIS: renal<br>• ACHILLES, ILIAC CREST AND TIBIAL TUBEROSITIES  INVOLVEMENT: enthesitis |
| Investigations | • Clinical: Mennell sign (percussion tenderness on sacroiliac joints), FABER pain (flexion, abduction, external rotation)<br>• Bloods: ↑CRP, ↑ESR, auto-antibodies (RF, ANA) are negative, HLA-B27 positive<br>• X-Ray: sacroiliac joints, spine, (syndesmophytes causing ‘bamboo spine’ in later stages)<br>• MRI: best method for early detection |
| Management | • General: physical therapy, deep breathing exercises, smoking cessation<br>• Medical therapy: NSAIDs (first line e.g. indomethacin), TNF-α inhibitor (e.g. etanercept), DMARDs (e.g. sulfasalazine), temporary intra-articular glucocorticoids in severe cases<br>• Surgery: in severe cases to improve QoL e.g. hip replacement, vertebral osteotomy |
| Complications | Complete spine fusion (↓↓mobility), osteoporosis, pathological fractures, restricted chess expansion |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — psoriatic arthritis — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Seronegative arthritis that may accompany psoriasis; five patterns = **SODAS** (spondylitis/sacroiliac, oligoarticular, distal joint, arthritis mutilans, symmetric polyarthritis).
- Signature signs: dactylitis (sausage digit), enthesitis, nail dystrophy; imaging shows **pencil-in-cup**, floating syndesmophytes, osteolysis, periostitis.
- **CASPAR** = ≥3 of psoriasis, psoriatic nail dystrophy, negative RF, dactylitis, radiologic signs.
- Ladder: treat the skin, NSAIDs if mild, DMARDs (MTX, sulfasalazine, leflunomide) then biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17/12/23).

| Definition | Seronegative arthritic inflammation (primary hands, feed, spine) that may occur with psoriasis |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology | Unclear but many genetic, immunologic and some environmental factors |
| Presentation | • Dermatological: well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale in areas such as ears, hair line, umbilicus, gluteal cleft and nails<br>• Musculoskeletal: 5 types (mnemonic SODAS) →Spondylitis or Sacroiliac, Oligoarticular (~70% and usually of both DIP and PIP), Distal joint, Arthritis mutilans, Symmetric poylarthritis<br>• Rheumatological: enthesitis, tenosynovitis, dactylitis (sausage digit)<br>• Ophthalmic: conjunctivitis, irititis<br>• Cardiac and respiratory (late findings): aortic insufficiency, apical lung fibrosis, neurologic, cauda equina |
| Classification | • The ClASsification Criteria for Psoriatic ARthritis (CASPAR): ≥ 3 of: <br>• 1. Evidence of psoriasis, <br>• 2. Psoriatic nail dystrophy, <br>• 3. Negative rheumatoid factor (RF), <br>• 4. Dactylitis, <br>• 5. Radiologic signs |
| Investigations | Imaging: floating syndesmophytes, pencil-in-cup appearance at IP joints, osteolysis, periostitis |
| Management | • Treat skin lesions (steroid cream, salicylic acid, UV light)<br>• Mild disease: NSAIDS<br>• Moderate to severe disease: DMARDS (MTX, sulfasalazine, luflunomide), biologic therapies (anti-TNF agents, anti-IL-17/12/23) |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — reactive arthritis — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Seronegative spondyloarthropathy 1–4 weeks after GI or urinary infection; HLA-B27, typically young men.
- Triggers: chlamydia (post-urethritis); Shigella, Yersinia, Salmonella, Campylobacter, C. difficile (post-enteritis).
- "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" — conjunctivitis + urethritis + arthritis: **99% specific but only 51% sensitive**.
- Cultures are **sterile** (arthrocentesis is to exclude differentials); NSAIDs + physio, antibiotics only for the non-articular infection.

| Definition | Seronegative spondyloarthropathies  that occurs after a bacterial infection of the GI/urinary tract |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology | • Genetic predisposition and associated with HLA-B27, commonly affecting young men<br>• Post-urethritis: chlamydia<br>• Post-enteritis: Shigella, Yersinia, Salmonella, Campylobacter, C. difficile |
| Presentation | • Acute clinical course: 1-4 weeks post-infection, lasts weeks to years, often recurring, spinal involvement persists<br>• “Can’t See, Can’t Pee, Can’t Climb a Tree”: triad of conjunctivitis, urethritis, and arthritis is 99% specific (but 51% sensitive)<br>• Musculoskeletal: polyarthritis (asymmetric, migratory), sacroiliitis, insertional tendinopathy, dactylitis<br>• Ophthalmic: conjunctivitis, iritis<br>• Dermatological: keratoma blenorrhagicum on palms and soles, balanitis circinate<br>• Gastrointestinal: oral ulcers, diarrhoea<br>• Urethritis/cervicitis: sterile pyuria |
| Investigations | Bloods: ↑ESR/CRP, HLA-B27 positive serology, sterile culturesArthrocentesis: to rule out other differentials |
| Treatment | • Antibiotics for non-articular infections<br>• NSAIDs, physical therapy, exercise<br>• Local therapy: joint protection, IA steroid injection, topical steroid for ocular involvement<br>• Systemic: corticosteroids, sulfasalazine, MTX (peripheral joints), TNF-a inhibitors (spinal inflammation) |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — gout — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Monosodium urate deposition; hyperuricaemia is mostly **impaired renal excretion (90%)**, overproduction only 10% — and hyperuricaemia alone ≠ gout.
- Aspirate: **negatively birefringent, needle-shaped** crystals in >90%; podagra (first MTP) is the classic site.
- Acute: NSAIDs (indomethacin, naproxen), colchicine, or corticosteroids. Chronic: xanthine oxidase inhibitor titrated to **urate <0.36 mmol/L** ⚠ **SOURCE ERROR — the usual target is **<0.36**; **<0.30** is reserved for tophaceous or severe disease.**
- Risk mnemonics — food **SALT**, drugs **FACT** (furosemide, aspirin, cytotoxics, thiazides); beware allopurinol/febuxostat with 6MP or azathioprine.

| Definition | • Derangement in purine metabolism causing hyperuricemia<br>• Monosodium urate crystal deposits in tissue (tophi) and synovium (microtophi)<br>• Chronic gout is characterised with progressive joint degeneration due to recurring gout attacks |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology | • RISK FACTORS:<br>• Food (SALT): seafood, alcohol, liver/kidney (sugar), turkey (meat)<br>• Drugs (FACT): furosemide, aspirin, cytotoxic (cyclosporine), thiazide diuretics<br>• Co-morbidities: DM, HTN, hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, anaemia, renal failure (impacting on excretion)<br>• Non-modifiable: age, male gender, genetic mutations<br>• Hyperuricemia does not equal gout but ↑ risk of gout and is commonly caused by impaired renal excretion (90%), overproduction (10%) and over-consumption of purine-rich food<br>• PRIMARY HYPERURICEMIA: idiopathic<br>• SECONDARY HYPERURICEMIA: <br>• ↓ uric acid excretion (most common): <br>• Medications<br>• CRF<br>• Ketoacidosis/lactic acidosis<br>• ↑ uric acid production: <br>• High cell turnover (tumour lysis, haemolytic anaemia, psoriasis, myeloproliferative neoplasms, chemotherapy, radiation)<br>• ↑ protein diet and obesity<br>• Enzyme defects (Lesh-Nyhan) |
| Pathophysiology | • Uric acid is obtained exogenously (diet) or by xanthine oxidase (endogenously)<br>• Uric acid is excreted via intestinal (1/3) or urinary (2/3)<br>• Hyperuricemia → supersaturation of uric acid in ECF →intra-articular uric crystal precipitation (coated by IgGs) → phagocytised by polymorphonuclear cells → inflammatory mediators and enzymes →local joint inflammation<br>• Overtime, tophi causes destruction of cartilage and bone in joints → deformities and arthritis |
| Patterns | • Acute<br>• Intercritical (or interval) <br>• Chronic and recurrent |
| Presentation | • Acute gouty arthritis: severe pain, redness, joint swelling, impaired joint mobility<br>• Tophi: urate deposits on cartilage, tendons, bursae, soft tissues and synovial membranes<br>• Locations: podagra (usually first MTP), gonagra (knee), chiragra (finger joint), ear helix, olecranon bursae, tendon insertions (Achilles tendon)<br>• Kidney: gouty nephropathy, uric acid calculi |
| Investigations | • Joint aspirate: >90% show monosodium urate (negatively birefringent, needle-shaped)<br>• X-Ray: (in chronic gout): tophi shown as soft tissue swelling, punched-out-lesions, erosion with “over-hanging”<br>• Blood: hyperuricemia, ↑WBC, ↑ESR<br>• Clinical Diagnosis: ≥1 episodes of single joint inflammation, 72 hours, cannot mobilise joint, classic podagra involvement (can be other joints), visible/palpable lesions (tophus) |
| Management | • CONSERVATIVE: avoid high purine foods and drugs with hypouricemic effects, ice therapy, weight loss, management of diabetes and blood pressure<br>• ACUTE GOUT:<br>• NSAIDS (COX-2 inhibitors) e.g. indomethacin, naproxen, ibuprofen: discontinue 2-3 days after symptoms resolve<br>• Colchicine (interferes with neutrophil migration to the joint): 1 tablet (0.5mg) three/four times day; side effects are severe diarrhoea, nephrotoxicity and myelosuppresion<br>• Corticosteroid: IA/oral/IM; consider ACTH to prevent adrenal suppression<br>• CHRONIC GOUT<br>• Indications for anti-hyperuricemic: recurrent attacks (more than 2-3/year), tophi, bone erosions, urate kidney stones, renal dysfunction with very high urate load (controversial)<br>• Xanthine oxidase inhibitors starting at low dose then increasing over 3 months until urate < **0.36** mmol/L ⚠ **SOURCE ERROR — this source says 0.3; the standard target is **<0.36 mmol/L**, with **<0.30** only for tophaceous or severe disease.**  (e.g. allopurinol, febuxostat). Beware of interaction between xanthine oxidase inhibitors and 6MP/Azathioprine  <br>• Uricosuric medications (benzbromarone, probenecid, lesinurad): reabsorption inhibition of uric acid along renal tubules → increased renal elimination; very rarely used in combination<br>• Prophylactic NSAIDs or Colchicine |
| Complications | Nephrolithiasis, uric acid nephropathy |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — pseudogout (calcium pryophosphate dihydrate disease) — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- CPPD crystals in hyaline or fibrocartilage; M=F, onset >50 years, often asymptomatic.
- Crystals are **positively birefringent (blue) and rhomboid** — the direct contrast with gout; serum urate is normal.
- Chondrocalcinosis on X-ray in 75%; secondary causes to screen — hyperparathyroidism, haemochromatosis, hypophosphataemia, DM, trauma.
- Aspirate to exclude septic arthritis and gout, then NSAIDs, IA/oral steroids, ± prophylactic colchicine.

| Definition | Joint inflammation from calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystals in the hyaline or fibrocartilage |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | M=F, age of onset > 50 years old |
| Aetiology | • Non-modifiable: old age, advanced OA, neuropathic joints<br>• Secondary form: joint trauma, familial chondrocalcinosis, hyperparathyroidism, haemochromatosis, gout, hypophosphatemia, DM |
| Presentation | • Often asymptomatic<br>• ACUTE: monoarthritis, mostly affecting knees and other large joints<br>• PSEUDO-OA: progressive jt degeneration, sometimes with episodes of acute inflammatory arthritis<br>• PSEUDO-RA: symmetrical polyarticular pattern with morning stiffness and constitutional symptoms |
| Investigations | • Bloods: hyperCa2+, hyperPTH, serum uric acid normal<br>• Arthrocentesis: to rule out septic arthritis, gout<br>• CPPD crystals: ~ 60% of patients, often only a few crystals, positive birefringence (blue) and rhomboid shaped<br>• X-rays: chondrocalcinosis in 75% (radiodensities in fibrocartilaginous structures or linear radiodensities in hyaline articular cartilage) |
| Management | • Joint aspiration, rest, and protection<br>• NSAIDs: also used for maintenance therapy<br>• Prophylactic colchicine PO (controversial)<br>• IA or oral steroids to relieve inflammation |

Affect soft tissues or periarticular structures (bursitis, tendinitis, tenosynovitis, fibromyalgia, and PMR)

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — polymyalgia rheumatica — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Proximal girdle pain and stiffness with **no muscle weakness**; F>M, >50 years — the commonest inflammatory rheumatic disease of the elderly.
- ↑ESR, ↑CRP with **normal CK, negative RF, no autoantibodies** — that combination is the discriminator.
- GCA coexists in ~15% (HLA-DR4 association) — actively monitor for it.
- Prednisone 15–20 mg OD tapered on response; no improvement at 2 weeks or relapse → increase dose; osteoporosis prophylaxis.

| Definition | Characterised by pain and stiffness (no muscle weakness) in proximal extremities (girdle area) |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | F>M, age of onset > 50 years, most common inflammatory rheumatic disease in the elderly |
| Aetiology | Unknown, possibly contributed by genetic predisposition (HLA-DR4) and GCA (in ~15%) |
| Presentation | • Constitutional: fever, weight loss, night sweats, symptoms of anaemia<br>• Mood: depressed<br>• Pain and stiffness of symmetrical proximal muscles: neck, shoulder, hip girdles, thighs<br>• Gel phenomenon: stiffness after prolonged activity<br>• GCA symptoms |
| Investigations | Bloods: ↑ESR, ↑CRP, leucocytosis, normochromic anaemia, normal CK, neg RF, no autoantibodiesImaging: bursitis on ultrasound of affected joints |
| Diagnosis | ![](notes/images/rheumatology-and-immunology-anu/img008.jpg) |
| Management | • Low-dose oral glucocorticoids (prednisone 15-20 mg OD which is tapered with symptom resolution)<br>• No improvement after 2 weeks or relapse: increase dose<br>• Monitor for steroid side effects: osteoporosis prevention, symptoms of GCA |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — fibromyalgia — Additional Notes

**The short version**
- Chronic (>3 months) widespread non-articular pain at muscle/tendon insertions, with unrefreshing sleep, fatigue and "fibro fog"; F>M, 20–50.
- **Clinical diagnosis** — labs and imaging are normal and exist only to exclude other causes.
- 1990 ACR criteria: ≥11 of 18 tender points, all four body regions + axial skeleton, ≥3 months; 2010 criteria drop the tender-point exam.
- Exercise, sleep hygiene, CBT/PT plus low-dose amitriptyline, duloxetine/milnacipran, or pregabalin/gabapentin — **avoid narcotics**.

| Definition | Neurosensory disorder characterised by chronic (> 3 months), widespread, non-articular pain with characteristic tender points |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | F>M, age range 20-50 years |
| Aetiology | Incompletely understood but likely multifactorial (genetic, environmental e.g. stress, dysregulation of neuroendocrine and ANS) |
| Presentation | • Chronic, widespread pain, primarily where muscles and tendons attach to bones (tender points)<br>• Headache, fatigue, morning stiffness, unrefreshing sleep<br>• Cognitive dysfunction (“fibro fog”)<br>• Paraesthesia<br>• ANS dysfunction: digestion, weight fluctuations, palpitations, sexual dysfunction, night swefts |
