### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — Rheumatoid arthritis — Additional Notes

| Definition | Chronic inflammatory autoimmune disorder characterised by symmetric and erosive synovitis of peripheral joints and extra-articular features |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | F&gt;M, peak incidence ~ 20-40 years |
| Aetiology | Autoimmune disorder of unknown aetiology where there is immune tolerance breakdown → autoreactivity. Hypotheses suggest the following factors:Genetic predisposition: HLA-DR4/DR1 in ~93% of patientsEnvironmental: infections, tobaccoHormonal: improves during pregnancy, worsens after birth |
| Pathophysiology | Initially, non-specific inflammation affects the synovial tissue, which is later amplified by activation of T cells (autoimmune response)With time, inflammatory joint effusion, synovial hypertrophy, and progressive destruction and deterioration of cartilage and bone (with ↑TNF and upregulation of RANK-L) |
| Presentation | COURSES OF DISEASEVariable course of exacerbations and remissions Monocyclic=single episode; polycyclic=relapsing/remitting; progressive patternsARTICULAR MANIFESTATIONSPolyarthritis: symmetric joint involvement (tender, swollen), small joints affected, most commonly in hands and feet (MCP, PIP, MTP but not DIP)Morning stiffness &gt; 1 hour but impreoves with use, worsens with wrest“Rheumatoid hand”: swan neck, boutonniere, Z-line deformity, ulnar deviationHammer/mallet/claw toe: as a result of chornic synovitisAtlanto-axial subluxation: C-spine instability, neurological impingementEXTRA-ARTICULAR MANIFESTATIONSConstitutional: low-grade fever, myalgia, malaise, night sweatsRheumatoid nodules: non-tender, firm, subcutaneous lumps on the skinLungs: pulmonary fibrosis, pleural effusion, pleuritis, pulmonary nodulesEye: keratoconjunctivitis sicca, scleritis, episcleritisEndocrine/exocrine glands: secondary Sjogren syndrome, Xerostomia, Hashimoto’s thyroiditisHaematological: anaemia of chronic diseaseMusculoskeletal: tenosynovitis, bursitis, carpal tunnel syndromeHeart: pericarditis, myocarditis, myocardial infarction, stroke, CHFRenal: amyloidosisVascular: peripheral vasculitis, Raynaud’s, purpura, necrosing fingertips, rheumatoid neuropathy |
| 2010 ACR/EULAR Classification | ![](notes/images/rheumatology-and-immunology-anu/img006.jpg) |
| Investigations | Bloods: CRP, ESR, ferritin/leucocytosis/thrombocytosis (acute phase), anaemia of chronic diseaseSerology: RF (80% sensitivity but non-specific), anti-CCP (specificity &gt;90%), ANA (in 30%)Synovial fluid analysis from joint aspiration: ↑leukocytes/neutrophils/granulocytes/proteins, ↓viscosity, possibly RFX-ray (LESS): loss of joint space, erosions, soft tissue swelling, soft bones (osteopenia)MRI: if cervical spine involvementU/S: joint effusion and formation of a pannus |
| Differentials | Osteoarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, gout, pseudogout, AI-related (sarcoidosis, SLE, RF) |
| Management | “Window of opportunity”: early treatment within 3 months may allow better control/ remissionGeneral: PT, OT, physical activityAnalgesics NSAIDs Prednisone DMARDs: methotrexate (first line; folic acid recommended within 24-48 hours after taking MTX), Hydroxychloroquine, Leflunomide, Azathioprin/6MP Biologicals: TNF blockers (Infliximab, Golimumab, Adalimumab), Anakinra (IL-1 receptor blocker), Etanercept (TNF decoy receptor), Rituximab (CD20), Tofacitinib (JAK-2 inhibitor) |
| Complications | Deformities, Baker’s cyst, septic arthritis, fractures, vasculitis involving kdineys/skin |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — scleroderma — Additional Notes

| Definition | Abnormal growth of connective tissue → diffuse thickening, hardening of skin/inner organs |
| --- | --- |
| Pathophysiology | Non-inflammatory autoimmune disorder characterised by widespread small vessel vasculopathy, production of autoantibodies and fibroblast dysfunction causing fibrosis |
| Aetiology | Associated with HLA-DR1 |
| Presentation | CREST Syndrome: C- calcinosis cutis; R- Raynaud’s phenomenon; E- Esophageal hypomotility; S- Sclerodactyly; T- Telangiectasia |
| Investigations | ANA (90% of cases)Limited SSc: anticentromere antibodies (ACA)Diffuse SSc: Anti-Scl070, Anti-RNA polymerase IIISerum protein electrophoresis: ↑ γ-globulinsCXR: pulmonary involvement and other tests based on organ-specific symptoms |
| Treatment | General: PT, prevent dry skinImmunosuppressants e.g. MTXOrgan-specific e.g. PPIs in cases of GORD, ACE-I for renal HTN crisis |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — Introduction — Additional Notes

- Heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases, all characterized by inflammation of blood vessels (vasculitis) and subsequent ischemia and damage to the organs supplied by these vessels.
- PRIMARY: idiopathic
- SECONDARY: response to infection e.g. hepatitis B, hepatitis C

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — ankylosing spondylitis — Additional Notes

| Definition | Seronegative chronic inflammatory arthritis of sacroiliac joints and vertebrae with enthesitis |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | M&gt;F, age 15- 40 years old |
| Aetiology | 90-95% of people are HLA-B27 positive |
| Pathophysiology | Inflammation → osteopenia → erosion → ossification → osteoproliferation (syndesmophytes) |
| Classification |  |
| Presentation (6 “A”’s) | ![](notes/images/rheumatology-and-immunology-anu/img007.jpg) ATLANTO-AXIAL SUBLUXATION: back pain, morning stiffness &gt; 1 hour, night pain, tenderness over sacroiliac joints, limited mobility of spine ANTERIOR UVEITISAPICAL FIBROSISAORTIC REGURGITATIONAMYLOIDOSIS: renalACHILLES, ILIAC CREST AND TIBIAL TUBEROSITIES  INVOLVEMENT: enthesitis |
| Investigations | Clinical: Mennell sign (percussion tenderness on sacroiliac joints), FABER pain (flexion, abduction, external rotation)Bloods: ↑CRP, ↑ESR, auto-antibodies (RF, ANA) are negative, HLA-B27 positiveX-Ray: sacroiliac joints, spine, (syndesmophytes causing ‘bamboo spine’ in later stages)MRI: best method for early detection |
| Management | General: physical therapy, deep breathing exercises, smoking cessationMedical therapy: NSAIDs (first line e.g. indomethacin), TNF-α inhibitor (e.g. etanercept), DMARDs (e.g. sulfasalazine), temporary intra-articular glucocorticoids in severe casesSurgery: in severe cases to improve QoL e.g. hip replacement, vertebral osteotomy |
| Complications | Complete spine fusion (↓↓mobility), osteoporosis, pathological fractures, restricted chess expansion |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — psoriatic arthritis — Additional Notes

| Definition | Seronegative arthritic inflammation (primary hands, feed, spine) that may occur with psoriasis |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology | Unclear but many genetic, immunologic and some environmental factors |
| Presentation | Dermatological: well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale in areas such as ears, hair line, umbilicus, gluteal cleft and nailsMusculoskeletal: 5 types (mnemonic SODAS) →Spondylitis or Sacroiliac, Oligoarticular (~70% and usually of both DIP and PIP), Distal joint, Arthritis mutilans, Symmetric poylarthritisRheumatological: enthesitis, tenosynovitis, dactylitis (sausage digit)Ophthalmic: conjunctivitis, irititisCardiac and respiratory (late findings): aortic insufficiency, apical lung fibrosis, neurologic, cauda equina |
| Classification | The ClASsification Criteria for Psoriatic ARthritis (CASPAR): ≥ 3 of: 1. Evidence of psoriasis, 2. Psoriatic nail dystrophy, 3. Negative rheumatoid factor (RF), 4. Dactylitis, 5. Radiologic signs |
| Investigations | Imaging: floating syndesmophytes, pencil-in-cup appearance at IP joints, osteolysis, periostitis |
| Management | Treat skin lesions (steroid cream, salicylic acid, UV light)Mild disease: NSAIDSModerate to severe disease: DMARDS (MTX, sulfasalazine, luflunomide), biologic therapies (anti-TNF agents, anti-IL-17/12/23) |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — reactive arthritis — Additional Notes

| Definition | Seronegative spondyloarthropathies  that occurs after a bacterial infection of the GI/urinary tract |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology | Genetic predisposition and associated with HLA-B27, commonly affecting young menPost-urethritis: chlamydiaPost-enteritis: Shigella, Yersinia, Salmonella, Campylobacter, C. difficile |
| Presentation | Acute clinical course: 1-4 weeks post-infection, lasts weeks to years, often recurring, spinal involvement persists“Can’t See, Can’t Pee, Can’t Climb a Tree”: triad of conjunctivitis, urethritis, and arthritis is 99% specific (but 51% sensitive)Musculoskeletal: polyarthritis (asymmetric, migratory), sacroiliitis, insertional tendinopathy, dactylitisOphthalmic: conjunctivitis, iritisDermatological: keratoma blenorrhagicum on palms and soles, balanitis circinateGastrointestinal: oral ulcers, diarrhoeaUrethritis/cervicitis: sterile pyuria |
| Investigations | Bloods: ↑ESR/CRP, HLA-B27 positive serology, sterile culturesArthrocentesis: to rule out other differentials |
| Treatment | Antibiotics for non-articular infectionsNSAIDs, physical therapy, exerciseLocal therapy: joint protection, IA steroid injection, topical steroid for ocular involvementSystemic: corticosteroids, sulfasalazine, MTX (peripheral joints), TNF-a inhibitors (spinal inflammation) |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — gout — Additional Notes

| Definition | Derangement in purine metabolism causing hyperuricemiaMonosodium urate crystal deposits in tissue (tophi) and synovium (microtophi)Chronic gout is characterised with progressive joint degeneration due to recurring gout attacks |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology | RISK FACTORS:Food (SALT): seafood, alcohol, liver/kidney (sugar), turkey (meat)Drugs (FACT): furosemide, aspirin, cytotoxic (cyclosporine), thiazide diureticsCo-morbidities: DM, HTN, hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, anaemia, renal failure (impacting on excretion)Non-modifiable: age, male gender, genetic mutationsHyperuricemia does not equal gout but ↑ risk of gout and is commonly caused by impaired renal excretion (90%), overproduction (10%) and over-consumption of purine-rich foodPRIMARY HYPERURICEMIA: idiopathicSECONDARY HYPERURICEMIA: ↓ uric acid excretion (most common): MedicationsCRFKetoacidosis/lactic acidosis↑ uric acid production: High cell turnover (tumour lysis, haemolytic anaemia, psoriasis, myeloproliferative neoplasms, chemotherapy, radiation)↑ protein diet and obesityEnzyme defects (Lesh-Nyhan) |
| Pathophysiology | Uric acid is obtained exogenously (diet) or by xanthine oxidase (endogenously)Uric acid is excreted via intestinal (1/3) or urinary (2/3)Hyperuricemia → supersaturation of uric acid in ECF →intra-articular uric crystal precipitation (coated by IgGs) → phagocytised by polymorphonuclear cells → inflammatory mediators and enzymes →local joint inflammationOvertime, tophi causes destruction of cartilage and bone in joints → deformities and arthritis |
| Patterns | AcuteIntercritical (or interval) Chronic and recurrent |
| Presentation | Acute gouty arthritis: severe pain, redness, joint swelling, impaired joint mobilityTophi: urate deposits on cartilage, tendons, bursae, soft tissues and synovial membranesLocations: podagra (usually first MTP), gonagra (knee), chiragra (finger joint), ear helix, olecranon bursae, tendon insertions (Achilles tendon)Kidney: gouty nephropathy, uric acid calculi |
| Investigations | Joint aspirate: &gt;90% show monosodium urate (negatively birefringent, needle-shaped)X-Ray: (in chronic gout): tophi shown as soft tissue swelling, punched-out-lesions, erosion with “over-hanging”Blood: hyperuricemia, ↑WBC, ↑ESRClinical Diagnosis: ≥1 episodes of single joint inflammation, 72 hours, cannot mobilise joint, classic podagra involvement (can be other joints), visible/palpable lesions (tophus) |
| Management | CONSERVATIVE: avoid high purine foods and drugs with hypouricemic effects, ice therapy, weight loss, management of diabetes and blood pressureACUTE GOUT:NSAIDS (COX-2 inhibitors) e.g. indomethacin, naproxen, ibuprofen: discontinue 2-3 days after symptoms resolveColchicine (interferes with neutrophil migration to the joint): 1 tablet (0.5mg) three/four times day; side effects are severe diarrhoea, nephrotoxicity and myelosuppresionCorticosteroid: IA/oral/IM; consider ACTH to prevent adrenal suppressionCHRONIC GOUTIndications for anti-hyperuricemic: recurrent attacks (more than 2-3/year), tophi, bone erosions, urate kidney stones, renal dysfunction with very high urate load (controversial)Xanthine oxidase inhibitors starting at low dose then increasing over 3 months until urate &lt; 0.3 mmol/L  (e.g. allopurinol, febuxostat). Beware of interaction between xanthine oxidase inhibitors and 6MP/Azathioprine  Uricosuric medications (benzbromarone, probenecid, lesinurad): reabsorption inhibition of uric acid along renal tubules → increased renal elimination; very rarely used in combinationProphylactic NSAIDs or Colchicine |
| Complications | Nephrolithiasis, uric acid nephropathy |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — pseudogout (calcium pryophosphate dihydrate disease) — Additional Notes

| Definition | Joint inflammation from calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystals in the hyaline or fibrocartilage |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | M=F, age of onset &gt; 50 years old |
| Aetiology | Non-modifiable: old age, advanced OA, neuropathic jointsSecondary form: joint trauma, familial chondrocalcinosis, hyperparathyroidism, haemochromatosis, gout, hypophosphatemia, DM |
| Presentation | Often asymptomaticACUTE: monoarthritis, mostly affecting knees and other large jointsPSEUDO-OA: progressive jt degeneration, sometimes with episodes of acute inflammatory arthritisPSEUDO-RA: symmetrical polyarticular pattern with morning stiffness and constitutional symptoms |
| Investigations | Bloods: hyperCa2+, hyperPTH, serum uric acid normalArthrocentesis: to rule out septic arthritis, goutCPPD crystals: ~ 60% of patients, often only a few crystals, positive birefringence (blue) and rhomboid shapedX-rays: chondrocalcinosis in 75% (radiodensities in fibrocartilaginous structures or linear radiodensities in hyaline articular cartilage) |
| Management | Joint aspiration, rest, and protectionNSAIDs: also used for maintenance therapyProphylactic colchicine PO (controversial)IA or oral steroids to relieve inflammation |

non-articular rheumatism

Affect soft tissues or periarticular structures (bursitis, tendinitis, tenosynovitis, fibromyalgia, and PMR)

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — polymyalgia rheumatica — Additional Notes

| Definition | Characterised by pain and stiffness (no muscle weakness) in proximal extremities (girdle area) |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | F&gt;M, age of onset &gt; 50 years, most common inflammatory rheumatic disease in the elderly |
| Aetiology | Unknown, possibly contributed by genetic predisposition (HLA-DR4) and GCA (in ~15%) |
| Presentation | Constitutional: fever, weight loss, night sweats, symptoms of anaemiaMood: depressedPain and stiffness of symmetrical proximal muscles: neck, shoulder, hip girdles, thighsGel phenomenon: stiffness after prolonged activityGCA symptoms |
| Investigations | Bloods: ↑ESR, ↑CRP, leucocytosis, normochromic anaemia, normal CK, neg RF, no autoantibodiesImaging: bursitis on ultrasound of affected joints |
| Diagnosis | ![](notes/images/rheumatology-and-immunology-anu/img008.jpg) |
| Management | Low-dose oral glucocorticoids (prednisone 15-20 mg OD which is tapered with symptom resolution)No improvement after 2 weeks or relapse: increase doseMonitor for steroid side effects: osteoporosis prevention, symptoms of GCA |

### Rheumatology & Immunology (ANU) — fibromyalgia — Additional Notes

| Definition | Neurosensory disorder characterised by chronic (&gt; 3 months), widespread, non-articular pain with characteristic tender points |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | F&gt;M, age range 20-50 years |
| Aetiology | Incompletely understood but likely multifactorial (genetic, environmental e.g. stress, dysregulation of neuroendocrine and ANS) |
| Presentation | Chronic, widespread pain, primarily where muscles and tendons attach to bones (tender points)Headache, fatigue, morning stiffness, unrefreshing sleepCognitive dysfunction (“fibro fog”)ParaesthesiaANS dysfunction: digestion, weight fluctuations, palpitations, sexual dysfunction, night swefts |
| Differentials | Chronic fatigue syndrome, IBS, psychiatric disorders, sleep disorders, inflammatory rheumatic disease (SLE, RA) |
| Investigations | Fibromyalgia is a clinical diagnosis THE 1990 AMERICAN COLLEGE OF RHEUMATOLOGY (ACR) FIBROMYALGIA DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIATender points: ≥ 11 of 18 fibromyalgia-associated localized areas of painPain-affected areas: all four regions of the body (left and right halves of the body, as well as both the upper and lower parts) and the axial skeletonDuration: ≥ 3 monthsTHE 2010 ACR PRELIMINARY DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIAIntensity and extent of widespread painSeverity of cognitive and somatic symptoms Without tender point examination Laboratory values and imaging are normal (helpful for excluding other causes) |
| Management | GENERAL: dietary recommendations, sleep hygiene, regular exercise, stress reductionNON-PHARMACOLOGICAL: PT, CBTPHARMACOLOGICAL: low-dose TCA e.g. amitriptyline, SSNRIs e.g. duloxetine, milnacipran, anti-convulsant e.g. pregabalin, gabapentin, avoid narcotics Consider comorbidities in treatment planning e.g. sleep disorders |
