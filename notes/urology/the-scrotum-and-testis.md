## The Scrotum and Testis


### Urology Master Notes — The Scrotum and Testis

*Sources: EAU Testicular Cancer Guidelines (2024–25), Australian STI Management Guidelines (2025) & Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic), Melbourne Sexual Health Centre treatment guidelines (2024), Cancer Australia / Cancer Council Australia testicular cancer statistics & position statement (2025), USANZ and RACGP guidance. Scope: the acute scrotum, testicular torsion, epididymo-orchitis, Fournier's gangrene, testicular cancer, painless scrotal swellings, and a brief treatment of male infertility and erectile dysfunction, written for a written finals paper in Australia.*

---

#### 3.1 The acute scrotum

**The short version**

- Acute scrotal pain in a boy or young man is torsion until a surgeon says otherwise. The default disposition is theatre, not imaging.
- Age triages: neonate and 12–18 y → torsion; 7–12 y → torted appendage; sexually active adult → epididymo-orchitis; diabetic older man, pain out of proportion → Fournier's.
- Onset tells you the pathology: instantaneous or waking-from-sleep pain = vascular event; building over days with dysuria and fever = infection.
- Absent cremasteric reflex + high-riding, transversely lying testis is the strongest bedside combination for torsion. Its presence does *not* exclude torsion.
- ⚑ Prehn's sign (relief on elevation → epididymitis) is a historical curiosity with poor accuracy. Do not let it change management; naming it as the deciding factor in an exam answer is a trap.

**Discriminator table — the core of this section**

| Feature | Testicular torsion | Torted appendix testis | Epididymo-orchitis | Strangulated inguinoscrotal hernia | Idiopathic scrotal oedema | Fournier's gangrene |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical age | Neonatal peak, then 12–18 y (majority <25 y) | 7–12 y (prepubertal) | Sexually active adult, or older man with obstruction/instrumentation | Any; infants and elderly men | 4–10 y | >50 y, diabetic/immunosuppressed |
| Onset | Sudden, seconds to minutes; often wakes from sleep | Subacute, hours to 1–2 days | Gradual over days | Sudden if previously reducible lump becomes irreducible | Gradual over hours | Rapid — hours; pain precedes visible signs |
| Pain severity | Severe, constant | Moderate, localised to upper pole | Moderate, builds | Severe, colicky abdominal component | Minimal/painless | Severe, **out of proportion to findings** |
| Lie / position | High-riding, horizontal (transverse) lie, shortened cord | Normal lie | Normal lie | Testis normal, mass separate and superior | Normal | Normal until necrosis |
| Focal signs | Whole testis exquisitely tender | Tender 3–5 mm nodule upper pole; **blue dot sign** through thin scrotal skin | Tenderness maximal in epididymis initially, later whole testis | Cannot get above the swelling; ± cough impulse (absent once strangulated) | Erythema/oedema extends beyond scrotum to perineum/inguinal region; **testes non-tender** | Crepitus, dusky/black skin, malodour, skin anaesthesia |
| Cremasteric reflex | Usually **absent** | Preserved | Preserved | Preserved | Preserved | Variable |
| Prehn's sign | Classically no relief | Not useful | Classically relief | Not useful | Not useful | Not useful |
| Systemic features | Nausea/vomiting common; afebrile | Well, afebrile | Fever, rigors, urethral discharge | Vomiting, obstruction, peritonism | Systemically well | Sepsis, tachycardia, hypotension, delirium |
| Urinalysis | Normal | Normal | Pyuria ± nitrites (may be normal in chlamydial) | Normal | Normal | Normal or incidental |
| First action | **Theatre** | Analgesia, reassure, self-limiting 1–2 wk | Antibiotics + STI screen | Theatre | Reassure ± antihistamine | Resuscitate, IV antibiotics, **theatre** |

**Points that decide marks**

- *Nausea and vomiting* with scrotal pain shifts probability strongly towards torsion — visceral afferent activation, not gastroenteritis.
- Torsion may present as **abdominal or groin pain with no scrotal complaint**, particularly in an embarrassed adolescent. Every boy with acute lower abdominal pain gets his scrotum examined; missing this is the classic medicolegal case.
- Idiopathic scrotal oedema: a well, afebrile child with an inflamed *scrotal wall* but normal, non-tender testes.
- ⚑ TWIST score (hard testis 2, swelling 2, nausea/vomiting 1, absent cremasteric reflex 1, high-riding testis 1) stratifies risk in some Australian EDs. It is a rule-in tool, not a rule-out, and does not replace surgical judgement.

#### 3.2 Testicular torsion

**The short version**

- **Clinical diagnosis. Straight to theatre. Ultrasound must not delay exploration** — if suspicion is high, imaging is an unnecessary and dangerous detour.
- Salvage is time-dependent: near-universal within ~6 h of onset, falling substantially by 12–24 h, and rare beyond 24 h.
- The anatomical substrate is the **bell-clapper deformity** — failure of tunica vaginalis fixation to the posterior testis, allowing the testis to hang and rotate freely within it. It is frequently bilateral.
- A **normal Doppler does not exclude torsion** (intermittent, partial, or early torsion with preserved arterial signal). Do not use a reassuring scan to overrule a convincing history.
- Fix **both** testes at operation — bilateral orchidopexy, because the predisposing deformity is bilateral in a large proportion.

**Mechanism and types**

*Intravaginal* torsion is the adolescent/adult disease and rests on the bell-clapper deformity. *Extravaginal* torsion is perinatal — cord and tunica twist above the tunica attachment because dartos fixation has not yet occurred; it presents as a firm, non-tender, discoloured hemiscrotum at birth and is usually already non-viable.

Ischaemia is progressive: venous outflow obstructs first (congestion, swelling), then arterial inflow fails. Hence an early scan may still show arterial flow in a testis already in trouble, and the degree of twist (180°–720°+) determines speed of infarction as much as elapsed time does.

**Intermittent torsion.** Recurrent, self-limiting episodes of severe unilateral pain lasting minutes to hours, often with a horizontal lie between episodes. Not benign — it marks a testis at high risk of a completed, unwitnessed torsion. **Refer for elective bilateral orchidopexy.**

**Management sequence**

1. Nil by mouth, analgesia (opioid), IV access, bloods, consent.
2. Urgent urology/surgical referral — the clock starts at symptom onset, not at presentation, so quantify it.
3. Manual detorsion may be attempted while awaiting theatre — classically rotating the testis medial-to-lateral, "opening a book" — but it is a temporising manoeuvre only and does not substitute for exploration, since residual twist is common.
4. Scrotal exploration: assess viability after detorsion and warm packs. Viable → **bilateral orchidopexy** (three-point fixation). Non-viable → orchidectomy plus contralateral orchidopexy. Offer a prosthesis at a later date.
5. ⚑ Fix the contralateral side even when the affected testis was salvaged — the deformity, not the event, is what recurs.

**Working the past-paper stem: a 20-year-old with sudden severe testicular pain and swelling**

Make each inference explicit; this is what earns marks in a short-answer paper.

- *Age 20* — squarely in the peak incidence window for intravaginal torsion. Epididymo-orchitis is possible but the prior probability of a surgical emergency here is what dictates action.
- *Sudden onset* — vascular occlusive events start instantaneously; infection does not. A stem that says "sudden" or "woke him from sleep" is signalling torsion.
- *Severe pain with swelling* — consistent with venous congestion of an ischaemic testis.
- *What to say next*: examine for a high-riding, transversely lying, diffusely tender testis with an absent cremasteric reflex; ask about prior self-limiting episodes (intermittent torsion), trauma, and urethral discharge/dysuria (which would raise epididymo-orchitis but does not exclude torsion).
- *What to do*: this is a clinical diagnosis — nil by mouth, analgesia, and **immediate surgical exploration**. Do **not** order an ultrasound if it will delay theatre, and do **not** be reassured by preserved Doppler flow. Give the reason: irreversible ischaemic injury accrues from symptom onset, and salvage falls steeply after ~6 h.
- *Answer to "definitive management"*: scrotal exploration with detorsion and **bilateral orchidopexy** (orchidectomy if non-viable, with contralateral fixation regardless).
- Imaging earns its place only when the presentation is genuinely atypical or delayed and the surgeon judges torsion unlikely — never as a routine step to "confirm" a clinically obvious case.

#### 3.3 Epididymo-orchitis

**The short version**

- ⚑ Organism follows **sexual history, not age**: in anyone sexually active — of **any** age — *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* remain the most likely cause (Australian STI Management Guidelines). **Coliforms** (*E. coli*, *Proteus*) are suggested by **insertive anal sex, recent instrumentation or catheterisation, or outflow obstruction** — the old "over 35 = coliforms" split is wrong and misses chlamydia in a sexually active 40-year-old.
- Insertive anal intercourse at any age shifts the likely organism to enteric Gram-negatives — ask the question rather than assuming from age alone.
- Australian first-line for the sexually acquired picture: **ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat (in 1% lignocaine) plus doxycycline 100 mg PO BD for 14 days**.
- Enteric/urinary picture: a 14-day course of an agent with Gram-negative cover — e.g. **trimethoprim or cefalexin**, guided by urine culture; MSHC lists cefalexin 500 mg BD or amoxicillin+clavulanate 875/125 mg BD for 14 days.
- STI-related cases require **contact tracing of sexual partners from the preceding 6 months**; urinary-source cases do not.

**Assessment**

Gradual unilateral scrotal pain and swelling, maximal in the epididymis early, with fever, dysuria, discharge or LUTS. Scrotum warm and erythematous, testis lies normally, cremasteric reflex preserved. Investigations: first-pass urine NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhoea; urethral swab for gonococcal culture and sensitivities **before** antibiotics if discharge is present; MSU M/C/S; full STI screen including HIV and syphilis serology.

| Group | Likely organisms | Australian regimen | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sexually active, any age** | *C. trachomatis*, *N. gonorrhoeae*, *M. genitalium* | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat **+** doxycycline 100 mg PO BD × 14 d | • Azithromycin 1 g PO stat repeated at 1 week is the alternative where doxycycline is unsuitable<br>• Notifiable; contact tracing 6 months |
| **Insertive anal sex, instrumentation/catheter, or obstruction** (⚑ not age alone) | *E. coli*, *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas* | 14 d of Gram-negative cover — **cefalexin 500 mg BD** (⚑ trimethoprim is no longer a good empirical choice at ~20% *E. coli* resistance); amoxicillin+clavulanate 875/125 mg BD as an alternative | • Tailor to MSU sensitivities<br>• Investigate the underlying outflow problem |
| Insertive anal sex | Enteric organisms *and* STIs | Cover both — ceftriaxone + doxycycline, review with cultures | ⚑ Age alone misleads here |
| Post-vasectomy / amiodarone / vasculitis | Sterile inflammation | Supportive; consider drug cause | Amiodarone-induced epididymitis is dose-related and resolves on reduction |

**Adjuncts.** Rest, scrotal elevation and support, NSAIDs, abstinence until patient and partners complete treatment. ⚑ Fluoroquinolones appear in older texts as routine first line; in current Australian practice they are reserved, given resistance and tendon/neurological adverse effects.

**When to image.** Not needed in a straightforward case. Image if torsion remains possible (a surgical, not radiological, decision); symptoms fail to settle within 72 h of appropriate antibiotics; a mass is palpable or persists after treatment (**an underlying tumour can masquerade as epididymo-orchitis**); or abscess/pyocele is suspected.

**Mumps orchitis.** Post-pubertal viral orchitis, typically 4–8 days after parotitis but possible without it. Affects a substantial minority of post-pubertal cases, bilateral in a minority of those, causing atrophy in some; complete sterility is uncommon even after bilateral disease. Supportive management only — antibiotics are useless. Notifiable; the exam point is **MMR vaccination**, since cases cluster in the unvaccinated.

#### 3.4 Fournier's gangrene

**The short version**

- Necrotising fasciitis of the perineum, genitalia and perianal region — polymicrobial, synergistic, and rapidly fatal.
- **Pain grossly out of proportion to visible findings** is the earliest and most important sign. Crepitus, dusky skin, bullae, malodour and skin anaesthesia are late.
- Surgical emergency: resuscitation + broad-spectrum IV antibiotics + **urgent, aggressive debridement**, with planned re-look and repeat debridement.
- Antibiotics never substitute for the knife. Delay to theatre is the strongest modifiable determinant of death.
- Mortality remains high (roughly 20–40% in contemporary series) despite modern care.

**Risk factors.** Diabetes (commonest), obesity, alcohol misuse, immunosuppression including HIV and chemotherapy, chronic kidney or liver disease, malignancy, older age. Portals of entry: perianal/ischiorectal abscess and colorectal disease, urethral stricture or urinary extravasation, recent instrumentation, genital trauma, pressure injury. ⚑ SGLT2 inhibitors carry a rare labelled association with Fournier's — worth knowing given how widely they are now prescribed in Australia.

**Recognition and management.** Infection tracks along fascial planes far beyond the visible skin change, so cutaneous signs consistently understate the extent. Systemic toxicity — tachycardia, hypotension, fever, delirium, hyperglycaemia — is common. CT showing fascial gas or fluid may support the diagnosis but **must not delay theatre**.

Management: resuscitate, correct glycaemia, blood cultures; broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Gram-positives, Gram-negatives and anaerobes per Therapeutic Guidelines (typically meropenem or piperacillin–tazobactam **plus clindamycin** for exotoxin suppression, plus vancomycin if MRSA is a concern); urgent radical debridement to healthy bleeding tissue; ICU support; planned second look at 24–48 h. Faecal or urinary diversion may be needed; reconstruction follows source control.

#### 3.5 Testicular cancer

**The short version**

- The commonest solid malignancy of young men. In Australia around 1,000 new cases a year, peak incidence in the **30–34 year** age band, and **5-year relative survival about 98%** — this is a curable cancer, which is exactly why delay is unforgivable.
- Risk factors: **cryptorchidism (raising the risk in the undescended testis *and* in the normally descended contralateral testis)**, prior testicular germ cell tumour, first-degree family history, GCNIS on biopsy, testicular atrophy/infertility. ⚑ Orchidopexy reduces but does not abolish the risk.
- **A firm, non-tender intratesticular mass is cancer until proven otherwise.** Ultrasound is the first-line test.
- ⚑ **Do not biopsy transscrotally.** Diagnosis and treatment are by **radical inguinal orchidectomy** with high cord ligation — a scrotal approach risks local recurrence and aberrant lymphatic spread to inguinal nodes.
- **AFP is never raised in pure seminoma** — an elevated AFP means non-seminomatous elements regardless of what the histology report says. β-hCG may be raised in seminoma; LDH reflects tumour bulk.
- **Sperm banking is offered BEFORE orchidectomy or any chemo/radiotherapy.**

**Histology**

| | Seminoma | Non-seminomatous GCT (NSGCT) |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Later peak (~35–45 y) | Earlier peak (~20–35 y) |
| Subtypes | Classic, spermatocytic | • Embryonal carcinoma<br>• Yolk sac tumour<br>• Choriocarcinoma<br>• Teratoma<br>• Mixed (commonest) |
| AFP | **Never raised** (if raised → treat as NSGCT) | Raised in yolk sac and embryonal components |
| β-hCG | Raised in up to ~30% | Raised, markedly so in choriocarcinoma |
| Behaviour | Slower, orderly nodal spread; **radiosensitive** | Earlier haematogenous spread; **chemosensitive**, not radiosensitive |

Non-germ-cell: Leydig cell (may secrete androgens/oestrogens → gynaecomastia, precocious puberty), Sertoli cell, and **lymphoma** — commonest testicular malignancy in men over 60, more often bilateral.

**Tumour markers**

- **AFP** — half-life ~5–7 days. Raised by yolk sac and embryonal elements; also by hepatocellular disease. Never by pure seminoma.
- **β-hCG** — half-life ~24–36 hours. Highest in choriocarcinoma; modestly raised in some seminomas (syncytiotrophoblastic cells). Very high levels cause gynaecomastia and thyrotoxicosis via TSH-receptor cross-reactivity.
- **LDH** — non-specific; correlates with tumour burden, used prognostically.
- Measure **before and after** orchidectomy. Markers failing to fall along their half-life, or plateauing/rising, indicate residual or metastatic disease. Normal markers do not exclude cancer — a large minority are marker-negative.

**Diagnosis and staging**

Scrotal ultrasound → markers → **radical inguinal orchidectomy** for histology and local control → **CT chest, abdomen and pelvis** for staging. Brain imaging if very high hCG, multiple lung metastases, or poor-prognosis disease. Consider contralateral biopsy for GCNIS in high-risk men (small testicular volume, cryptorchidism); not routine in men over 40 without risk factors.

**Lymphatic drainage — why it matters.** The testis descends from the retroperitoneum dragging its vessels and lymphatics with it, so it drains to the **para-aortic (retroperitoneal) nodes at the renal vessels — not inguinal nodes**. The *scrotal skin* drains to inguinal nodes. Two consequences: staging CT must cover the retroperitoneum, and palpable inguinal nodes are not the expected finding; and a transscrotal biopsy breaches the barrier between these territories, seeding inguinal nodes and altering both spread pattern and adjuvant treatment fields. That is the reason for the inguinal approach.

**Treatment principles.** Radical inguinal orchidectomy underpins all stages. Stage I seminoma: surveillance preferred (adjuvant carboplatin or radiotherapy as alternatives). Stage I NSGCT: surveillance, or risk-adapted BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) where lymphovascular invasion is present. Metastatic: BEP by IGCCCG risk group, with retroperitoneal lymph node dissection for residual NSGCT masses. Cure exceeds 95% in early disease and remains good with metastases. ⚑ Australian protocols are published through **eviQ**.

**Late effects** (increasingly examinable, since most patients are cured and live decades): hypogonadism and infertility; second malignancies from radiotherapy and etoposide; cisplatin ototoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, nephrotoxicity and Raynaud's; **bleomycin pulmonary fibrosis** (lifelong caution with high-inspired oxygen); cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome.

⚑ **Screening:** Cancer Council Australia does not recommend population screening or routine testicular self-examination, given the low incidence and excellent prognosis. The message is prompt presentation with a new lump, not ritual self-screening.

#### 3.6 Painless scrotal swellings

**The short version**

- Two questions sort most of these: **can you get above it?** (no → inguinoscrotal hernia or a hydrocele extending into the cord) and **does it transilluminate?** (yes → fluid).
- Anything **intratesticular and solid is cancer until proven otherwise**; extratesticular and cystic is usually benign.
- ⚑ A hydrocele in a young man may be **secondary** — scan the testis to exclude an underlying tumour before calling it primary.
- ⚑ A **new right-sided varicocele, or one that does not decompress on lying flat**, mandates abdominal imaging for a retroperitoneal mass or renal tumour.
- Ultrasound is the investigation for essentially all of these.

| Lesion | Feel / signs | Transillumination | Get above it? | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrocele | Smooth, fluctuant, testis impalpable within | Yes | Yes (unless cord hydrocele) | US in young men to exclude tumour; surgery for symptoms |
| Varicocele | "Bag of worms", worse standing/Valsalva, decompresses supine | No | Yes | Left common and benign; **right-sided/new/non-decompressing → CT abdomen** |
| Epididymal cyst / spermatocele | Cystic nodule **separate from and above** the testis | Yes | Yes | Reassure; excise only if symptomatic |
| Inguinoscrotal hernia | Cough impulse, reducible, bowel sounds | No | **No** | Surgical repair |
| Tumour | Firm, craggy, **intratesticular**, non-tender | No | Yes | Urgent US + markers + urology |

**Hydrocele.** Fluid between the layers of the tunica vaginalis. In infants usually communicating (patent processus vaginalis); most resolve by 12–24 months. In adults usually idiopathic, but **secondary** hydrocele accompanies tumour, torsion, infection and trauma — hence imaging the young man rather than reassuring him. Repair by Jaboulay or Lord's procedure; aspiration alone recurs.

**Varicocele.** Dilated pampiniform plexus veins. **Left-sided predominance (~90%)** is anatomical: the left testicular vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle (higher pressure, longer column, valve incompetence), while the right drains obliquely into the IVC; "nutcracker" compression of the left renal vein between aorta and SMA contributes. Dull dragging ache, sometimes ipsilateral atrophy and impaired spermatogenesis — the commonest correctable cause of male infertility. Repair (embolisation or ligation) for pain, adolescent growth arrest, or abnormal semen parameters in an infertile couple. ⚑ Red flag: **right-sided, sudden onset, or failure to empty when supine** — suggests venous obstruction by a retroperitoneal or renal mass; image the abdomen.

**Epididymal cyst / spermatocele.** Extratesticular, transilluminate, at the epididymal head and palpably separate from the testis; a spermatocele contains spermatozoa (opalescent aspirate). Benign; excision risks epididymal obstruction, so avoid in men wanting fertility.

#### 3.7 Male infertility and erectile dysfunction

**The short version**

- Infertility work-up is of the **couple**: two semen analyses ≥2–3 weeks apart, morning testosterone with LH/FSH, and a scrotal examination looking for varicocele, absent vas or small testes.
- High FSH with small testes = primary testicular failure. Low/normal FSH with azoospermia = obstruction (consider CBAVD and cystic fibrosis testing).
- **ED is a marker of cardiovascular risk** — endothelial dysfunction in the small penile arteries typically precedes coronary events by a few years. Every man with new ED gets a cardiovascular risk assessment.
- ⚑ **PDE5 inhibitors are absolutely contraindicated with nitrates** — the combination causes profound hypotension. Standard separation is ≥24 h for sildenafil/vardenafil and ≥48 h for tadalafil.
- Reversible contributors: drugs (β-blockers, thiazides, SSRIs, antiandrogens), smoking, alcohol, diabetes, hypogonadism, depression, pelvic surgery/radiotherapy.

Retained early-morning erections and a situational pattern point to psychogenic ED; gradual global loss suggests organic disease. Check HbA1c, lipids, morning testosterone, and prolactin if testosterone is low. First line: risk-factor modification plus a PDE5 inhibitor; second line intracavernosal alprostadil or vacuum device; third line penile prosthesis. Warn about priapism (>4 h is an emergency requiring aspiration). In infertility, treat the treatable — varicocele, hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism, surgically correctable obstruction — and otherwise refer for ICSI with surgical sperm retrieval.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
|---|---|
| Sudden severe testicular pain, high-riding transverse testis, absent cremasteric reflex | Testicular torsion — theatre, no imaging |
| Normal Doppler flow in a boy with a convincing torsion history | Does not exclude torsion — still explore |
| Small tender blue nodule at the upper pole of a 9-year-old's testis | Torsion of the appendix testis (blue dot sign) — conservative |
| Anatomical defect underlying intravaginal torsion | Bell-clapper deformity — bilateral orchidopexy |
| Recurrent brief episodes of severe testicular pain that self-resolve | Intermittent torsion — elective bilateral orchidopexy |
| Epididymo-orchitis in a 24-year-old, Australian first-line | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM stat + doxycycline 100 mg BD × 14 days |
| Epididymo-orchitis in a 62-year-old with a catheter | Coliforms — 14 days Gram-negative cover per MSU sensitivities |
| Perineal pain out of proportion to signs in a diabetic man, crepitus | Fournier's gangrene — resuscitate, IV antibiotics, urgent debridement |
| AFP raised in a testicular tumour reported as "seminoma" | Manage as non-seminomatous GCT — pure seminoma never raises AFP |
| Marker of choriocarcinoma; causes gynaecomastia | β-hCG (LDH reflects bulk) |
| Why orchidectomy is inguinal, never transscrotal | Scrotal approach seeds inguinal lymphatics and raises local recurrence |
| Nodal basin for testicular cancer | Para-aortic/retroperitoneal at the renal vessels — not inguinal |
