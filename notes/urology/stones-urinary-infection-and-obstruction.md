## Stones, Urinary Infection and Obstruction


### Urology Master Notes — Stones, Urinary Infection and Obstruction

*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic), incl. the March 2025 UTI revision; EAU Urolithiasis and Urological Infections; RACGP/AJGP; WA KEMH obstetric UTI guideline; Vic Health UTI pharmacist protocol. Scope: urolithiasis, urinary infection, prostatitis and obstructive uropathy in adults, written-finals depth, Australian practice.*

#### 4.1 Urolithiasis — types, risk and presentation

**The short version**
- Calcium stones (oxalate ± phosphate) ≈80% of all stones; uric acid ~5–10%; struvite ~5–10%; cystine ~1%.
- **Uric acid is radiolucent on plain KUB; cystine is poorly radiopaque ("ground-glass"), not truly radiolucent.** The genuinely radiolucent group is uric acid, ammonium urate, xanthine, 2,8-dihydroxyadenine and drug stones — but almost all, including uric acid, are visible on non-contrast CT — ⚑ **indinavir stones are the exception and are radiolucent even on CT**. "Radiolucent" is an X-ray statement, not a CT one.
- Struvite = infection stone, formed only when a **urease-producing** organism alkalinises the urine: *Proteus mirabilis* (classic), *Klebsiella*, *Providencia*, *Morganella*, *Serratia*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*, *Ureaplasma urealyticum*. **Not** *E. coli* — it does not make urease.
- Pain is colicky loin-to-groin because the ureter and the testis/labium share T11–L2 afferents (embryological co-origin); the referred site migrates caudally as the stone descends.
- Three points of narrowing: **pelviureteric junction (PUJ) → crossing of the iliac vessels at the pelvic brim → vesicoureteric junction (VUJ)**. The VUJ is narrowest and the commonest lodging point.

**Composition and drivers**

| Stone | Share | Radiology | Typical driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate | ~70–75% | Radio-opaque, very dense | • Hypercalciuria (idiopathic, commonest) • Hyperoxaluria • Hypocitraturia • Low urine volume |
| Calcium phosphate | ~5–10% | Radio-opaque | • Alkaline urine • Distal (type 1) RTA • Primary hyperparathyroidism |
| Uric acid | ~5–10% | **Radiolucent** | • Persistently acid urine (pH <5.5) • Gout, high purine intake • Metabolic syndrome/T2DM (impaired ammoniagenesis) • Tumour lysis, chronic diarrhoea/ileostomy |
| Struvite (Mg-ammonium-phosphate, "triple phosphate") | ~5–10% | Radio-opaque, often **staghorn** | Chronic infection with urease producer; alkaline urine pH >7 |
| Cystine | ~1% | **Radiolucent** on plain film ("faint/ground-glass" at best) | Autosomal recessive cystinuria; young, recurrent, family history |

**Risk factors.** Low fluid intake and hot occupational exposure (the dominant Australian risk); high sodium, high animal protein, low dietary calcium; obesity, T2DM, gout; primary hyperparathyroidism; distal RTA; sarcoidosis; IBD, coeliac disease and bariatric surgery (**enteric hyperoxaluria** — unabsorbed fat binds gut calcium, freeing oxalate for absorption); medullary sponge kidney; horseshoe kidney and PUJ obstruction (stasis); drugs — topiramate, acetazolamide, indinavir, ceftriaxone, excess vitamin C.

**Presentation.** Sudden severe unilateral flank pain, waxing/waning, radiating to groin/testis/labium, patient writhing (contrast peritonitis, where the patient lies rigid). Nausea/vomiting near-universal. Haematuria ~85% — **its absence does not exclude a stone**. Distal/VUJ stones cause urgency, frequency and dysuria, mimicking cystitis. Fever is *not* part of uncomplicated colic — see 4.3.

⚑ In anyone >50 with new "renal colic", especially with hypotension or a pulsatile mass, **exclude a leaking AAA** before anchoring on stone — a recurring exam and coronial theme.

#### 4.2 Imaging and acute management

**The short version**
- **Non-contrast CT KUB is the investigation of choice** — sens ~95%, spec ~95%; low-dose protocols preserve accuracy at much lower dose.
- Ultrasound (± KUB film) is preferred first in **pregnancy, children, and young women** and where repeated imaging is anticipated — it detects hydronephrosis, not mid-ureteric stones.
- **NSAIDs are first-line analgesia, superior to opioids** for renal colic (Level 1 evidence). Opioids are the add-on, not the starter.
- Tamsulosin (medical expulsive therapy) is worth offering for **distal ureteric stones 5–10 mm**; benefit for stones <5 mm is negligible.
- Stone size predicts passage: <5 mm mostly pass; ≥10 mm rarely do.

**Imaging pathway.** CT KUB, ideally low-dose, within 24 h. It confirms the stone, gives size, position, Hounsfield density (predicts ESWL success) and degree of obstruction, and picks up alternatives (AAA, appendicitis, ovarian pathology). ⚑ IVP/IVU is obsolete. Plain KUB alone is only useful for follow-up of a stone already known to be opaque. Ultrasound first in pregnancy (note physiological right hydronephrosis of pregnancy is a confounder), in children, and reasonably in young non-pregnant women where gynaecological causes compete; if US is non-diagnostic, low-dose CT or MR urography can follow.

**Analgesia — the high-yield point.** NSAID unless contraindicated: parenteral or rectal routes act fastest, and rectal indomethacin/diclofenac is common Australian ED practice; oral ibuprofen or diclofenac suffices for milder pain. Add paracetamol (opioid-sparing). Reserve opioids (titrated IV fentanyl/morphine) for NSAID contraindication or breakthrough. ⚑ Avoid NSAIDs in AKI, significant CKD, hypovolaemia, active peptic ulcer; lowest dose/shortest course in cardiovascular disease. Add an antiemetic (metoclopramide or ondansetron) and rehydrate to euvolaemia — but **forced high-volume IV fluids do not flush stones out**.

**Medical expulsive therapy.** Tamsulosin 400 microgram daily relaxes distal ureteric smooth muscle. Large pragmatic RCTs (SUSPEND) found no overall benefit, but meta-analysis restricted to **distal stones 5–10 mm** shows faster expulsion with less analgesic use. EAU position: offer as one option for distal stones 5–10 mm; ⚑ **off-label in Australia** — warn about postural hypotension and intraoperative floppy iris if cataract surgery is planned.

**Passage vs intervention**

| Stone size | Spontaneous passage | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| <5 mm | High (~70–90%) | Trial of passage, oral analgesia, strain urine, review 2–4 weeks |
| 5–10 mm | Intermediate (~50%) | Trial of passage + MET if distal; image at 4 weeks |
| >10 mm | Low | Plan definitive intervention |

**Indications for urgent intervention (decompression by ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy):**
- **Infection above an obstruction** — see 4.3
- AKI from obstruction; obstructed solitary or transplanted kidney; bilateral obstruction/anuria
- Pain uncontrolled despite adequate analgesia
- Failure to progress after ~4 weeks (risk of irreversible functional loss beyond ~4–6 weeks of complete obstruction)

**Definitive options.** ESWL — renal stones <20 mm and selected proximal ureteric stones; avoid in pregnancy, coagulopathy, active UTI, distal obstruction. Ureteroscopy + laser (Ho:YAG or thulium fibre) — the workhorse for ureteric stones and increasingly renal stones (flexible URS/RIRS); best single-session stone-free rate. PCNL — renal stones >20 mm and staghorn calculi. ⚑ **Struvite/staghorn stones must be cleared completely** — residual fragments harbour organisms and the stone regrows.

#### 4.3 ⚑ THE INFECTED OBSTRUCTED KIDNEY — urological emergency

**The short version**
- **Fever (or any sepsis physiology) + an obstructed upper tract = emergency decompression, within hours, not days.**
- Antibiotics alone will not sterilise pus behind an obstruction — no drug delivery past the block. This is a closed-space abscess.
- Decompress by **retrograde ureteric stent OR percutaneous nephrostomy** — both are equally effective; choose on local expertise, patient stability and anatomy.
- **Do not attempt to treat the stone at the same sitting.** Definitive stone treatment is deferred until sepsis has resolved and the antimicrobial course is complete.
- Delay kills: mortality rises steeply with each hour of untreated pyonephrosis.

**Recognition.** Renal colic *plus* fever, rigors, tachycardia, hypotension, delirium or rising lactate. Neutrophilia, raised CRP, AKI. ⚑ Urinalysis may be bland if the obstructed side cannot drain into the bladder — clean urine does not exclude it. CT shows the obstructing stone with perinephric stranding; a dilated system with debris/pus is pyonephrosis.

**Immediate management (in parallel, not sequence):**
1. Resuscitate — IV access, fluids, sepsis bundle, blood and urine cultures **before** antibiotics.
2. Empirical IV antibiotics with Gram-negative cover per local antibiogram/TG for urinary-source sepsis; narrow to susceptibilities.
3. **Call urology immediately for decompression.** Nephrostomy is favoured if unstable, coagulopathic, or the stone is large/impacted; stenting if endoscopic access is straightforward.
4. ICU if shocked; monitor for post-obstructive diuresis after drainage (4.8).
5. Definitive stone treatment later, electively.

⚑ The rule extends beyond stones: fever + hydronephrosis from any cause (malignant compression, retroperitoneal fibrosis, blocked stent) is the same emergency.

#### 4.4 Prevention and metabolic workup

**The short version**
- Recurrence ~50% at 10 years — prevention is the point of the follow-up visit.
- Fluid is the single highest-yield intervention: drink to a **urine output of 2–2.5 L/day** (roughly 2.5–3 L intake, more in heat/manual work); urine should be pale.
- ⚑ **Do not restrict dietary calcium.** Normal calcium intake (~1000–1200 mg/day, i.e. 2–3 dairy serves) *reduces* calcium oxalate stone risk by binding oxalate in the gut. Low-calcium diets increase oxalate absorption and increase stones.
- Restrict **sodium** (**NaCl 4–5 g/day**, i.e. <100 mmol sodium) and **animal protein**; moderate oxalate only if hyperoxaluric.
- Send every first stone for **composition analysis**; full metabolic workup for high-risk and recurrent formers.

**Dietary detail.** Sodium restriction matters because natriuresis drags calcium into the urine — high salt generates hypercalciuria. Animal protein raises uric acid, lowers urinary citrate and lowers urine pH — all pro-lithogenic. Moderate high-oxalate foods (spinach, rhubarb, beetroot, nuts, chocolate, strong tea) only in documented hyperoxaluria, and take calcium **with** meals so it binds oxalate in the lumen — calcium *supplements* between meals do not confer the same protection and may raise risk. Avoid excess vitamin C (oxalate precursor); citrus juice raises urinary citrate.

**Drug therapy**

| Agent | Indication | Mechanism / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Thiazide (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide, indapamide) | Recurrent calcium stones with hypercalciuria | ↑ distal tubular Ca reabsorption → ↓ urinary Ca. Requires low-salt diet to work. Watch hypokalaemia (worsens hypocitraturia), hyponatraemia, gout |
| Potassium citrate | Hypocitraturia, uric acid stones, cystinuria, distal RTA | Alkalinises urine and raises citrate (a crystallisation inhibitor). Target urine pH **7.0–7.2 for oral chemolysis (dissolution)** of an existing uric acid stone; **6.2–6.8 for prevention** ⚑ — do not conflate the two/prevention. Caution in CKD/hyperkalaemia |
| Allopurinol | Uric acid stones with hyperuricosuria/hyperuricaemia; calcium oxalate with hyperuricosuria | ↓ uric acid production. Alkalinisation is the primary measure for uric acid stones — allopurinol is adjunct |
| Antibiotics ± complete stone clearance | Struvite | Sterilising the tract without clearing the stone fails |
| Tiopronin, high fluid, alkalinisation | Cystinuria | Aim urine volume **>3 L/day and pH >7.5** |

**Who gets a full metabolic workup** (fasting bloods — creatinine/eGFR, calcium ± ionised, phosphate, urate, electrolytes, PTH if hypercalcaemic — plus 1–2 24-hour urines for volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, urate, sodium, creatinine, pH, and cystine screen if suspected):
- Recurrent stone formers; bilateral or residual stone burden
- Children and young adults (<25 years)
- Non-calcium stones — **uric acid, struvite, cystine** — at first presentation
- Solitary kidney, CKD, nephrocalcinosis, family history
- Malabsorption/bariatric surgery, or a systemic driver (hyperparathyroidism, gout, sarcoid, RTA)

Everyone else: stone analysis, UEC/calcium/urate, urinalysis and culture, imaging follow-up.

#### 4.5 UTI in adults — definitions and Australian antibiotic therapy

**The short version**
- **Uncomplicated** = acute cystitis in a non-pregnant, non-immunocompromised adult with a structurally and functionally normal tract. Everything else — pregnancy, catheters, stones, obstruction, transplant, poorly-controlled diabetes, instrumentation — is **complicated**.
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** = significant bacteriuria without urinary symptoms. ⚑ **Treat only in pregnancy and before an invasive urological procedure breaching the mucosa. Otherwise do not treat** — including the elderly, diabetics, catheterised patients, and residents of aged care.
- Commonest organism *E. coli* (~75–85%); then *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus*, and *Staph. saprophyticus* in young sexually active women.
- ⚑ **Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic, March 2025) made nitrofurantoin first-line for acute cystitis and demoted trimethoprim** because of *E. coli* trimethoprim resistance (~20% in Australia). Older notes saying "trimethoprim 300 mg nocte for 3 days, first-line" are out of date.

**Regimens — non-pregnant adults**

| Situation | Therapy | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Acute cystitis, non-pregnant woman | **1st: nitrofurantoin 100 mg orally 6-hourly**; 2nd: fosfomycin 3 g orally single dose; 3rd: trimethoprim 300 mg orally nocte | Nitrofurantoin **5 days**; fosfomycin single dose; trimethoprim **3 days** |
| Acute cystitis, man | Same agent choices, guided by culture; men are by definition complicated — always culture, and consider prostatic involvement | Longer than in women (typically ~7 days; if prostatitis suspected, treat as 4.7) |
| Cystitis/asymptomatic bacteriuria in **pregnancy** | Choose by susceptibility: nitrofurantoin 100 mg 6-hourly, cefalexin 500 mg 12-hourly, amoxicillin 500 mg 8-hourly (if susceptible), or amoxicillin+clavulanate 875+125 mg 12-hourly | **5 days** (some protocols 5–7); ⚑ **send test-of-cure** |
| Acute pyelonephritis, non-pregnant adult, not septic, tolerating oral | ⚑ TG 2025 now permits **initial oral therapy**; amoxicillin+clavulanate **875+125 mg orally 8-hourly** is a listed option (⚑ 8-hourly for pyelonephritis, not the 12-hourly used for cystitis), otherwise culture-directed. ⚑ Ampicillin/amoxicillin are **no longer** recommended as empirical combination therapy in non-pregnant adults (E. coli resistance) | Total ~10–14 days (shorter courses acceptable with fluoroquinolone-class agents; follow current TG) |
| Acute pyelonephritis, septic / vomiting / pregnant | IV therapy: gentamicin-based regimen, or ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily; piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV 8-hourly for severe/resistant risk. In **pregnancy**, amoxicillin 2 g IV 6-hourly + gentamicin retains a role (group B strep cover) | IV until afebrile/improving, then oral switch; **total 10–14 days** |

⚑ Pregnancy-specific traps: **avoid trimethoprim in the first trimester** (folate antagonist — if unavoidable, give folic acid 5 mg daily); **avoid nitrofurantoin at term (≥37 weeks) or if delivery is imminent** (neonatal haemolysis); avoid fluoroquinolones. Screen **all** pregnant women for asymptomatic bacteriuria with an MSU at the first antenatal visit or 12–16 weeks — dipstick is not adequate — because untreated ASB progresses to pyelonephritis and is linked to preterm birth and low birth weight.

⚑ Nitrofurantoin caveats: no therapeutic tissue levels in renal parenchyma or prostate — **useless for pyelonephritis or prostatitis**, cystitis only. Avoid at low eGFR (commonly cited <45 mL/min/1.73 m²) and in G6PD deficiency.

**Diagnosis.** In a young woman with classic dysuria/frequency/urgency and no vaginal discharge, empirical treatment without culture is acceptable. MSU culture is mandatory in men, pregnancy, children, recurrent or failed treatment, pyelonephritis and catheterised patients. Nitrite is specific but insensitive (nitrate-reducing Enterobacterales only); leucocyte esterase sensitive, non-specific. ⚑ Sterile pyuria → chlamydia/urethritis, renal TB, stones, tumour, interstitial nephritis, partly-treated UTI.

**Recurrent UTI** (≥2 in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months). Confirm each episode by culture. Non-antibiotic measures first: fluids, avoid spermicide/diaphragm, post-coital voiding, treat constipation. **Vaginal oestrogen** post-menopause (strong recommendation). **Methenamine hippurate** is non-inferior to daily antibiotic prophylaxis (ALTAR) in a normal tract. Cranberry and D-mannose: weak/contradictory evidence. ⚑ TG 2025 prioritises **patient-initiated self-start treatment** and **post-coital single-dose prophylaxis** over continuous daily prophylaxis. Image ± cystoscopy for stones, incomplete emptying, fistula or tumour if haematuria between episodes, a persistent single organism (*Proteus* → stone), or failure to respond.

**Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI).** ⚑ **Do not send urine and do not treat an asymptomatic catheterised patient.** Every long-term catheter is colonised/biofilmed within days; culturing it yields a positive result that invites unnecessary antibiotics and drives resistance. Diagnose CAUTI only with systemic features (fever, rigors, delirium, suprapubic/loin pain, unexplained sepsis) *plus* no other source; sample from a freshly changed catheter, change or remove the catheter as part of treatment, treat per culture. Cloudy or malodorous urine alone is not an indication.

#### 4.6 Pyelonephritis and renal/perinephric abscess

**The short version**
- Fever + rigors + loin pain + **costovertebral angle tenderness**, usually with cystitis symptoms; nausea/vomiting common.
- Admit for: haemodynamic instability, vomiting/unable to tolerate oral, pregnancy, obstruction or single kidney, immunosuppression, failure of outpatient therapy at 48–72 h, significant comorbidity or social isolation.
- **Image (CT with contrast unless AKI, else US) if: septic, no improvement by 48–72 h, known/suspected stone, single kidney, diabetes, or recurrent same-side infection** — you are looking for obstruction, abscess or emphysematous change.
- Persistent fever beyond 72 h on appropriate antibiotics = complication until proven otherwise.

Renal abscess arises by ascending infection (Gram-negatives) or haematogenous seeding (*Staph. aureus* — IVDU, endocarditis, skin sepsis). Perinephric abscess sits between capsule and Gerota's fascia, presenting insidiously with prolonged fever, weight loss and a flank mass; may track to psoas. Antibiotics plus **drainage** — small abscesses (<3 cm) often resolve on antibiotics alone; larger need percutaneous drainage; surgery if refractory.

⚑ **Emphysematous pyelonephritis.** Necrotising gas-forming infection of the renal parenchyma, **>90% in diabetics**, usually poorly controlled; *E. coli* then *Klebsiella*. Often also obstructed. CT is diagnostic (gas in parenchyma/collecting system/perinephric tissue) and classifies severity (Huang–Tseng). Management has shifted ⚑ from immediate nephrectomy to **resuscitation + broad-spectrum IV antibiotics + glycaemic control + percutaneous drainage**, with nephrectomy reserved for failure of that approach; mortality has fallen from historical 40–90% to roughly 10–20%.

#### 4.7 Prostatitis

**The short version**
- **Acute bacterial prostatitis**: fever, rigors, perineal/low back pain, dysuria, obstructive voiding, exquisitely tender boggy prostate on PR.
- ⚑ **Do not perform prostatic massage in acute prostatitis** — it is painful and can precipitate bacteraemia. Do a gentle PR to assess tenderness only.
- Organisms are the uropathogens (*E. coli* dominant); in sexually active men <35 also consider *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *N. gonorrhoeae*.
- ⚑ **Nitrofurantoin does not penetrate the prostate** — never use it here. Choose an agent with prostatic penetration (trimethoprim; a fluoroquinolone where justified by susceptibility) after IV therapy if severely unwell.
- Duration is **long: 2–4 weeks for acute, 4–6 weeks (or longer) for chronic bacterial prostatitis** — short courses relapse.

Investigations: MSU culture, blood cultures if febrile, STI NAAT in younger men; PSA is unhelpful acutely (it rises). Complications: acute retention (⚑ prefer **suprapubic** catheterisation — urethral instrumentation through an inflamed prostate risks bacteraemia) and prostatic abscess (suspect if fever persists >36–48 h on appropriate antibiotics; image and drain).

**Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS, NIH category III)** is far commoner than chronic bacterial prostatitis: ≥3 months of pelvic/perineal pain with voiding and sexual dysfunction, **no positive cultures**, no fever. Repeated antibiotic courses are not the answer — manage multimodally (UPOINT domains): alpha-blocker, pelvic floor physiotherapy, neuromodulators (amitriptyline, pregabalin), and address anxiety/depression. Distinguishing category II (chronic *bacterial* — recurrent UTIs with the same organism, responds to a long antibiotic course) from category III is the examinable discrimination.

#### 4.8 Obstructive uropathy and hydronephrosis

**The short version**
- Obstruction is a **reversible** cause of AKI — always consider it, and the test is a **renal tract ultrasound**.
- Think obstruction when: anuria or fluctuating urine output, palpable bladder, known stones/pelvic malignancy/prior pelvic radiotherapy, single kidney, or an unexplained AKI with bland urinalysis.
- **Bilateral hydronephrosis (or unilateral in a single kidney) is needed to cause AKI**; unilateral obstruction with a normal contralateral kidney gives pain, not a rising creatinine.
- Below the bladder → catheterise. Above the bladder → stent or nephrostomy.
- After relieving obstruction, watch for **post-obstructive diuresis**.

**Causes**

| Level | Causes |
|---|---|
| Upper tract (kidney/ureter) | • Stone • PUJ obstruction • Transitional cell carcinoma • Extrinsic compression: cervical/ovarian/colorectal/prostate malignancy, lymphadenopathy • Retroperitoneal fibrosis • Ureteric injury post-pelvic surgery • Blocked/migrated stent |
| Bladder outlet and below | • BPH (commonest in men) • Prostate cancer • Urethral stricture • Phimosis/meatal stenosis • Blocked catheter • Neurogenic bladder • Constipation, pelvic mass, anticholinergic drugs |

**Consequences.** Acute complete obstruction causes a transient rise then fall in renal blood flow, tubular dysfunction and, beyond ~4–6 weeks, irreversible parenchymal loss with cortical thinning. Partial chronic obstruction may present with polyuria/nocturia (concentrating defect) and hyperkalaemic distal RTA rather than anuria. ⚑ Beware the false-negative ultrasound: obstruction can exist **without** hydronephrosis early, in volume depletion, and in retroperitoneal fibrosis or malignant encasement where the ureter cannot dilate — if suspicion is high, proceed to CT.

**Post-obstructive diuresis.** After relief of bilateral or solitary-kidney obstruction, urine output can exceed 200 mL/h — initially physiological (offloading retained solute and water), pathological if tubular dysfunction drives ongoing salt and water loss. Measure hourly urine output, weigh daily, check UEC and magnesium at least 12-hourly, and replace roughly 50–75% of losses with crystalloid — replacing 100% perpetuates the diuresis. Watch hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia and hypovolaemic collapse. ⚑ Transient **haematuria ex vacuo** after decompressing a chronically distended bladder does not require clamping the catheter (risks re-obstruction) — drain completely and monitor.
