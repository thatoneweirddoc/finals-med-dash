## Inflammatory Dermatoses

### Finals Tutor — Inflammatory Dermatoses

#### Atopic dermatitis (eczema)

Chronic, relapsing, itchy inflammatory dermatosis with impaired barrier function (**filaggrin** mutations) and immune dysregulation. Part of the atopic triad with asthma and allergic rhinitis.

Distribution changes with age: **face and extensors in infants**, **flexural in older children and adults** (antecubital, popliteal fossae, neck, wrists).

**Management — a stepped, explainable plan (commonly asked):**
1. **Emollients**, liberally and frequently, including when clear — the foundation
2. Avoid triggers: soap (use soap substitutes), overheating, wool, known allergens
3. **Topical corticosteroid** appropriate to site and severity — the potency question is the exam point: mild (hydrocortisone) on **face, flexures, genitals**; moderate to potent on trunk and limbs. Use enough, for long enough, then step down
4. Topical calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus, tacrolimus) — steroid-sparing, useful on face
5. Treat infection: **Staph aureus** commonly colonises and flares eczema
6. Severe/refractory: phototherapy, systemic immunosuppression, dupilumab

**Fingertip unit** — the amount from the distal crease to the fingertip, covers about two adult palms.

**Eczema herpeticum** — a dermatological **emergency**. Widespread monomorphic punched-out erosions and vesicles in a person with eczema, with fever and malaise, caused by **HSV**. Treat with **aciclovir urgently**; risk of ocular involvement and dissemination.

#### Psoriasis

Chronic immune-mediated disease with accelerated keratinocyte turnover. Classic lesion: **well-demarcated salmon-pink plaques with thick silvery scale on extensor surfaces**, scalp, and sacrum.

Signs: **Auspitz sign** (pinpoint bleeding on scale removal), **Koebner phenomenon** (lesions at sites of trauma), nail changes (**pitting, onycholysis, oil drop sign**).

Variants: chronic plaque (commonest), **guttate** (small "raindrop" papules, classically **2 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis**, in young people), flexural/inverse, pustular, erythrodermic.

**Associations**: psoriatic arthritis (up to 30%), metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, depression, inflammatory bowel disease. Screen for joint symptoms.

**Triggers**: streptococcal infection, stress, trauma, **drugs — beta-blockers, lithium, antimalarials, NSAIDs, and corticosteroid withdrawal** (which can precipitate pustular flare).

**Management**: emollients; **topical corticosteroid plus vitamin D analogue (calcipotriol)** is the standard first-line combination; coal tar; dithranol. Scalp preparations. Then phototherapy (NB-UVB), then systemic (methotrexate, ciclosporin, acitretin), then biologics (TNF, IL-17, IL-23 inhibitors).

**Avoid systemic corticosteroids in psoriasis** — withdrawal risks severe rebound and pustular disease.

#### Acne vulgaris

Pilosebaceous unit disease: follicular hyperkeratinisation, increased sebum (androgen-driven), *Cutibacterium acnes*, and inflammation. Lesions are **comedones (non-inflammatory)**, papules, pustules, nodules, cysts, with scarring in severe disease.

**Stepwise treatment**:
- Mild comedonal: **topical retinoid** (adapalene, tretinoin)
- Mild–moderate inflammatory: topical retinoid + **benzoyl peroxide** ± topical antibiotic (never antibiotic monotherapy — resistance)
- Moderate–severe: add **oral antibiotic** (doxycycline) for a limited course; **COC or spironolactone** in women
- Severe, nodulocystic, scarring, or failed therapy: **isotretinoin**

**Isotretinoin counselling** — highly **teratogenic**, requiring reliable contraception and pregnancy prevention; dryness of skin/lips/eyes, photosensitivity, myalgia, deranged lipids and LFTs, and discussion of mood effects. Monitor lipids and LFTs.

#### Rosacea

Central facial erythema, telangiectasia, papules and pustules **without comedones** (the discriminator from acne), flushing triggered by heat, alcohol, spicy food, sun. Ocular involvement is common and under-recognised. May progress to rhinophyma.

Treat with trigger avoidance, sun protection, **topical metronidazole or ivermectin**, brimonidine for erythema, oral doxycycline for papulopustular disease. **Topical corticosteroids make rosacea worse** — a frequent trap.

#### Urticaria

Transient itchy wheals, each lasting **<24 hours**, from mast cell degranulation. Acute (<6 weeks, often infection or drug) or chronic (>6 weeks, usually spontaneous/autoimmune).

Treatment: **non-sedating antihistamine, uptitrated to up to four times standard dose** before adding anything else. Omalizumab for refractory chronic urticaria.

**Red flags**: individual lesions lasting >24 hours, bruising or pigmentation on resolution, systemic symptoms → consider **urticarial vasculitis** and biopsy. Angioedema with airway involvement is an emergency; if without urticaria and unresponsive to adrenaline, consider **ACE inhibitor-induced or hereditary angioedema** (bradykinin-mediated).
