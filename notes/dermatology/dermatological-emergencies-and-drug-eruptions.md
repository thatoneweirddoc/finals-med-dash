## Dermatological Emergencies and Drug Eruptions

### Finals Tutor — Dermatological Emergencies and Drug Eruptions

#### Recognising the emergency

Features that should make you worry about any rash: **mucosal involvement**, **skin pain or tenderness**, **blistering or skin detachment**, **positive Nikolsky sign**, fever and systemic upset, **facial oedema**, lymphadenopathy, and rapid progression.

#### Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis

A spectrum of severe mucocutaneous reaction, almost always **drug-induced**, with keratinocyte apoptosis and full-thickness epidermal necrosis.

| | Body surface area detached |
|---|---|
| **SJS** | <10% |
| **SJS/TEN overlap** | 10–30% |
| **TEN** | >30% |

Prodrome of fever and malaise, then painful dusky macules/targetoid lesions progressing to blisters and sheet-like detachment, with **erosions of at least two mucosal surfaces** (oral, ocular, genital). **Nikolsky sign positive.**

**Common culprit drugs**: allopurinol, **anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin)**, sulfonamides (including sulfamethoxazole), NSAIDs (oxicams), nevirapine. Onset usually **1–3 weeks** after starting.

**Management**: **stop the culprit drug immediately** (the single most important intervention), transfer to a burns or ICU setting, meticulous fluid and electrolyte management, temperature control, analgesia, wound care, **early ophthalmology review** to prevent blindness, nutrition, and infection surveillance. **Prophylactic antibiotics are not given.** SCORTEN scores severity.

Note: **erythema multiforme is a different entity** — target lesions with a central blister, usually triggered by **HSV** rather than drugs, and generally self-limiting. Conflating the two is a common error.

#### DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)

Delayed — **2–8 weeks** after the drug, longer than most drug eruptions.

Features: widespread morphilliform rash, **facial oedema** (characteristic), fever, **lymphadenopathy**, **eosinophilia**, atypical lymphocytosis, and **internal organ involvement** — liver most commonly, also kidney, lung, heart.

Culprits overlap with SJS/TEN: anticonvulsants, allopurinol, sulfonamides, vancomycin, minocycline.

Management: stop the drug, systemic corticosteroids, monitor organ function. Can relapse over weeks and is associated with **HHV-6 reactivation** and later autoimmune sequelae (especially thyroid disease — follow up).

#### Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP)

Rapid onset (days) of numerous small non-follicular **sterile pustules** on oedematous erythema, starting in flexures, with fever and neutrophilia. Usually antibiotics (aminopenicillins, macrolides). Resolves with desquamation after drug withdrawal.

#### Erythroderma

**>90% body surface area erythema** from any cause — psoriasis, eczema, drug reaction, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, idiopathic.

Dangerous because the skin fails as an organ: **hypothermia, high-output cardiac failure, dehydration and electrolyte loss, hypoalbuminaemia, and secondary infection**. Requires admission, warming, fluid and electrolyte management, emollients, and treating the underlying cause.

#### Meningococcal septicaemia

Any **non-blanching purpuric rash with fever** is meningococcaemia until proven otherwise. Do not delay: **immediate parenteral antibiotics** (benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone) before investigations, blood cultures if no delay, resuscitation, and public health notification with contact prophylaxis. Do not wait for LP or CT.

#### Immunobullous disease — the two to distinguish

| | Pemphigus vulgaris | Bullous pemphigoid |
|---|---|---|
| Antibody target | **Desmoglein** (desmosome) | **Hemidesmosome** (BP180/BP230) |
| Split level | **Intraepidermal** | **Subepidermal** |
| Blisters | **Flaccid**, rupture easily | **Tense**, persist |
| Mucosa | **Commonly involved**, often first | Usually spared |
| Nikolsky | **Positive** | Negative |
| Age | Middle age | **Elderly** |

Pemphigus is more dangerous. Both diagnosed with biopsy plus **direct immunofluorescence** on perilesional skin. Treated with corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunosuppression.
