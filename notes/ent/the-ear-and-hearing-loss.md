## The Ear and Hearing Loss


### ENT Master Notes — The Ear and Hearing Loss

*Sources: Therapeutic Guidelines (Antibiotic), Otitis Media Guidelines for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Children 2020 (Menzies/CDU), RCH CPGs, Queensland Health/CHQ otitis externa guidelines, Australian Prescriber, AAO-HNS Sudden Hearing Loss guideline, Hearing Australia. Scope: ear anatomy and assessment, infective ear disease, the hearing-loss differential, sudden SNHL, tinnitus and trauma.*

#### 1.1 Applied anatomy and the examination

**The short version**
- Outer ear (pinna → canal → drum) and middle ear (drum, ossicles, ET) disease produces **conductive** loss; anything medial to the stapes footplate (cochlea, CN VIII, central) produces **sensorineural** loss.
- Weber lateralises **to** a conductive ear and **away from** a sensorineural ear. Rinne is "negative" (BC > AC) only in conductive loss.
- The **air–bone gap** is the whole game: gap = conductive component; both curves down with no gap = SNHL; both down *and* a gap = mixed.
- Tympanometry: **A** normal; **B** flat = effusion (normal canal volume) or perforation/grommet (large volume); **C** = negative middle-ear pressure from ET dysfunction.
- Describe a drum systematically: colour, translucency, position, light reflex, mobility, perforation (site/size), attic.

**Anatomy that earns its keep.** Outer third of canal cartilaginous; inner two-thirds bony and thin-skinned. The **bony–cartilaginous junction** collects debris and is where necrotising otitis externa granulation appears. The middle ear cleft connects forward to the nasopharynx via the Eustachian tube (shorter, wider, more horizontal in infants — hence paediatric otitis media) and backward to the mastoid air cells (hence mastoiditis). The facial nerve runs along the medial middle-ear wall over the oval window; dehiscent bone here explains facial palsy in middle-ear disease. Chorda tympani crosses the drum medially — ear surgery risks altered taste.

**The normal drum.** Pearly grey, translucent, slightly concave; handle of malleus down and back to the umbo, lateral process bulging anterosuperiorly, cone of light forward and down. Taut **pars tensa** (four quadrants) plus the small **pars flaccida** (attic) above the lateral process — where retraction pockets and cholesteatoma hide.

**Otoscopy.** Largest speculum that fits; pinna up-and-back in adults, down-and-back in infants; good ear first. Pneumatic otoscopy adds most — an immobile drum suggests effusion or perforation.

**Tuning fork tests.** Use **512 Hz** (256 Hz is felt as vibration; 1024 Hz decays too fast). **Weber**: fork on vertex/mid-forehead — ask *where* it is heard. **Rinne**: AC (beside meatus) vs BC (mastoid); AC > BC = "positive" (normal or SNHL); BC > AC = "negative" = conductive loss of roughly ≥20–25 dB.

| Pattern | Weber (forehead) | Rinne left | Rinne right |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Central, heard midline | Positive (AC > BC) | Positive (AC > BC) |
| **Left conductive** | Lateralises to **left** (the deaf ear) | **Negative (BC > AC)** | Positive |
| **Right conductive** | Lateralises to **right** (the deaf ear) | Positive | **Negative (BC > AC)** |
| **Left sensorineural** | Lateralises to **right** (the better ear) | Positive (both reduced) | Positive |
| **Right sensorineural** | Lateralises to **left** (the better ear) | Positive | Positive (both reduced) |

⚑ **The false-negative Rinne trap.** In severe/profound unilateral SNHL ("dead ear") the mastoid fork is heard by the *opposite* cochlea through the skull, so the patient reports BC > AC on the affected side and looks conductive. The giveaway: Weber lateralises to the *opposite* (good) ear. Mask the good ear if the tests disagree.

**Reading a pure tone audiogram.** Frequency (250–8000 Hz) left to right; threshold dB HL top (−10, best) to bottom (120, worst) — **lower on the page = worse hearing**. AC: **O = right, X = left**. BC: **< = right, > = left**. Normal adult ≤20–25 dB HL. Grading: mild 26–40, moderate 41–60, severe 61–90, profound >90 dB.

| Audiogram | Air conduction | Bone conduction | Air–bone gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | ≤20–25 dB | Same | Nil (<10 dB) |
| Conductive | Reduced | **Normal** | **Present (>10–15 dB)** |
| Sensorineural | Reduced | Reduced, tracks AC | **Nil** |
| Mixed | Reduced most | Reduced, but better than AC | Present, on a depressed BC line |

Shape cues: bilateral symmetrical **high-frequency downsloping** = presbycusis; **4 kHz notch with recovery at 8 kHz** = noise-induced; **fluctuating low-frequency SNHL** = Ménière's; **Carhart notch** (BC dip ~2 kHz) with a conductive gap = otosclerosis; **asymmetry >15 dB at two adjacent frequencies**, or speech discrimination worse than expected, = image the internal auditory meatus.

**Tympanometry** measures compliance against applied canal pressure. **Type A**: peak near 0 daPa, normal (*As* shallow = stiff — otosclerosis, tympanosclerosis; *Ad* tall = hypermobile — ossicular discontinuity, flaccid drum). **Type B**: flat — *normal* canal volume = middle ear effusion; *large* volume = perforation or patent grommet. **Type C**: peak shifted negative (beyond about −100 daPa) — Eustachian tube dysfunction.

#### 1.2 Otitis externa

**The short version**
- **Pain on tragal pressure or on pulling the pinna is the discriminator** from otitis media — the canal, not the middle ear, is inflamed.
- *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *S. aureus* dominate; **fungal** (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*) typically follows prolonged drop use.
- Treatment is **topical + aural toilet**. ⚑ Oral antibiotics are *not* indicated for uncomplicated diffuse otitis externa.
- ⚑ If the drum is perforated or a grommet is in situ, use **non-ototoxic ciprofloxacin drops**, never aminoglycoside-containing preparations.
- Diabetic/immunosuppressed + severe pain + **granulation at the bony–cartilaginous junction** = necrotising otitis externa until proven otherwise.

**Presentation.** Rapid onset (<48 h) otalgia — often out of proportion to the otoscopic picture — with itch, fullness, scant discharge, and conductive loss if the canal occludes. Signs: **tragal and/or pinna tenderness**, diffuse canal oedema and erythema, debris. Predisposing: swimming, humidity, cotton-bud trauma, hearing aids, eczema/psoriasis. If the drum cannot be seen, a discharging middle ear is not excluded.

**Organisms.** *P. aeruginosa* and *S. aureus*; polymicrobial common. Fungal disease is the classic **treatment-failure** picture after antibacterial drops — fluffy white hyphae with black spores (*Aspergillus niger*) or creamy curd (*Candida*), itch over pain.

**Management (Australian practice).**

| Situation | Topical agent | Regimen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uncomplicated, **intact drum** | Dexamethasone + framycetin + gramicidin | 3 drops tds, 7 days |
| **Perforation or grommet** ⚑ | Ciprofloxacin 0.3% (non-ototoxic) | 5 drops bd, 7 days |
| Severe, marked canal oedema, **intact drum** | Ciprofloxacin + hydrocortisone | 3 drops bd until 2 days after symptoms clear; max 2 weeks. ⚑ **Contraindicated with a perforated drum or a grommet** — use ciprofloxacin 0.3% alone (or cipro+dexamethasone) there |
| **Fungal** | Triamcinolone + neomycin + gramicidin + nystatin; alternatives flumetasone + clioquinol, or clotrimazole 1% cream | 3 drops tds, 7 days |

- **Aural toilet is therapeutic, not cosmetic** — drops cannot work through debris. Dry mopping or microsuction; microsuction preferred, effectively mandatory for fungal disease.
- **Wick** (ribbon gauze or sponge) if canal oedema prevents drop penetration; drops onto the wick, review at 48–72 h.
- Analgesia, keep the ear dry, stop cotton buds, treat any dermatitis.
- **Systemic antibiotics only** if infection extends beyond the canal (pinna/facial cellulitis), the patient is systemically unwell, or significant immunocompromise/diabetes — discuss with ENT.
- No response at 48–72 h: retained debris, occluded canal, fungal overgrowth, contact allergy (neomycin sensitises), or wrong diagnosis.

**Necrotising (malignant) otitis externa.** Skull-base osteomyelitis spreading from the canal — a *Pseudomonas* disease of the **elderly diabetic** or immunosuppressed patient. Suspect when pain is deep, nocturnal and disproportionate, and fails to settle on standard topicals.

- **Sign to quote: granulation tissue at the bony–cartilaginous junction of the canal floor.**
- Progressive **cranial nerve palsies** mark spread: **CN VII first** (poor prognostic sign), then jugular foramen nerves IX/X/XI, then XII; V and VI imply extensive disease.
- Investigate: swab/biopsy of granulation tissue (also excludes canal SCC), **ESR/CRP** to track response, **HRCT** for bony erosion, **MRI** for soft-tissue/intracranial extent; nuclear medicine (Tc-99m to diagnose, Ga-67 to monitor) where available.
- Treat: admit, ENT + infectious diseases, **prolonged systemic antipseudomonal therapy over weeks to months** (ciprofloxacin for bone penetration, or IV antipseudomonal beta-lactam, culture-guided), strict glycaemic control, repeated aural toilet. Duration follows clinical, inflammatory-marker and imaging response, not a fixed course; surgery is limited to debridement/abscess drainage.

#### 1.3 Acute otitis media and otitis media with effusion

**The short version**
- Peak 6–24 months; *S. pneumoniae*, non-typeable *H. influenzae*, *M. catarrhalis*, plus viruses.
- Diagnosis needs a **bulging, opaque, immobile drum** with acute symptoms — a red drum in a crying febrile child is not enough.
- ⚑ Any systemically well child **≥6 months** without risk features gets **analgesia and observation** (review/delayed script at 48–72 h), not immediate antibiotics. The under-2 rule below bites only for **bilateral** disease.
- ⚑ **Always give antibiotics**: <6 months; systemically unwell; **bilateral in <2 years**; **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children**; immunocompromised; cochlear implant; only-hearing ear; AOM with discharge.
- First-line **amoxicillin 15 mg/kg (max 500 mg) 8-hourly for 5 days**, or 30 mg/kg (max 1 g) 12-hourly for 5 days.

**Clinical picture.** Otalgia (ear-tugging or unexplained irritability in infants), fever, coryzal prodrome, conductive loss; sudden pain relief with purulent discharge = perforation. Distinguish from otitis externa (tragal tenderness, canal oedema) and referred pain (teeth, TMJ, tonsil, cervical spine).

**Antibiotics.**
- **Analgesia is the priority** — regular paracetamol ± ibuprofen. ⚑ Decongestants, antihistamines and corticosteroids do **not** help.
- Penicillin allergy: cefuroxime (non-severe) or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole/macrolide (severe immediate hypersensitivity) — check current TG.
- Failure at 48–72 h, or recent amoxicillin: amoxicillin–clavulanate, covering beta-lactamase-producing *H. influenzae*/*M. catarrhalis*.
- ⚑ **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children** (Menzies 2020): treat *all* episodes — **amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day for 7 days**; **azithromycin** where adherence is difficult or refrigeration unavailable; escalate to 90 mg/kg/day or amoxicillin–clavulanate if unresolved. AOM **with perforation**: antibiotics until discharge resolves, adding topical ciprofloxacin if larger than pinhole.

**Complications.** **Acute mastoiditis** is the commonest suppurative complication — postauricular swelling, erythema, tenderness, loss of the postauricular crease, pinna pushed **down and out**; admit for IV antibiotics (e.g. third-generation cephalosporin), ENT review, CT if abscess suspected, myringotomy ± cortical mastoidectomy. Also perforation, facial palsy, labyrinthitis, and intracranial spread (meningitis, extradural/brain abscess, lateral sinus thrombosis, Gradenigo syndrome — petrous apicitis with CN VI palsy and retro-orbital pain).

**Otitis media with effusion ("glue ear").** Non-infected middle-ear fluid after AOM or with ET dysfunction. Painless; presents as hearing loss, inattention, loud television, speech/language delay or behavioural concern. Otoscopy: dull, retracted or amber drum, air-fluid level or bubbles, reduced mobility; **Type B tympanogram, normal canal volume**; conductive loss typically 20–30 dB.

- **Natural history is the examinable point** — most resolve spontaneously within about 3 months, so the default is watchful waiting with hearing and language review.
- Consider **grommets** when effusion persists **>3 months** with documented bilateral conductive loss, or at any stage with speech/language delay, developmental concern or retraction pocket. ⚑ Australian prioritisation ranks **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children**, significant bilateral loss and confirmed speech delay as higher-urgency; Menzies sets the hearing-impairment threshold here at an average **>20 dB**.
- Autoinflation (Otovent) may help cooperative children; antibiotics, antihistamines and steroids are not routine.
- Grommets extrude over ~6–12 months; counsel about otorrhoea (topical ciprofloxacin, ⚑ not aminoglycoside). Routine water precautions are no longer mandated for most.

#### 1.4 Chronic suppurative otitis media and cholesteatoma

**The short version**
- CSOM = persistent otorrhoea through a perforation with conductive loss — ⚑ **≥6 weeks in the Australian OMA/Menzies definition** (typically with a perforation >2% of pars tensa). The **≥2-week** figure is WHO, not Menzies.
- ⚑ Treat with **aural toilet plus topical ciprofloxacin 5 drops bd** — topical quinolone beats systemic therapy and avoids ototoxicity.
- **Painless, foul-smelling discharge with hearing loss = cholesteatoma until excluded.**
- The dangerous perforations are **attic (pars flaccida)** and **marginal/posterosuperior**; central pars tensa perforations are "safe".
- Cholesteatoma is a **surgical** disease — drops may dry the ear but do not treat it.

**CSOM.** Recurrent or continuous mucopurulent discharge through a non-healing perforation; hearing loss proportional to perforation size and ossicular status. Aural toilet then topical ciprofloxacin; add oral trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole if topical therapy fails. Systemic antibiotics alone are inferior. The long-term cost is educational and developmental — hence audiology follow-up, particularly for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, in whom prevalence is high.

**Cholesteatoma.** Keratinising squamous epithelium trapped medial to the drum — usually from a retraction pocket (acquired), rarely congenital (white mass behind an intact drum in a child). Not a neoplasm, but locally destructive via pressure and osteolytic enzymes.

- **Cues:** scanty, offensive, *painless* discharge unresponsive to drops; progressive conductive loss; crust or granulation in the **attic**; a retraction pocket whose base you cannot see; drum polyp.
- **Why it matters:** ossicular erosion (usually long process of incus); **facial nerve palsy**; **labyrinthine fistula** (lateral SCC) — vertigo on tragal pressure or loud sound (fistula sign/Tullio); SNHL; intracranial sepsis (meningitis, extradural/temporal lobe/cerebellar abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis).
- **Imaging:** **HRCT temporal bones** for extent and bony erosion (scutum, ossicles, tegmen, fallopian canal, lateral SCC); MRI non-EPI DWI separates residual/recurrent cholesteatoma from granulation or fluid.
- **Management:** ENT surgery — atticotomy, canal-wall-up or -down mastoidectomy with tympanoplasty/ossiculoplasty, plus second-look or DWI surveillance. Drops are pre-operative adjuncts only.

#### 1.5 Hearing loss by category

**The short version**
- Sort the stem: **conductive vs sensorineural** (Weber/Rinne, air–bone gap), then unilateral vs bilateral, then sudden vs gradual.
- Conductive: wax, foreign body, otitis externa, effusion, perforation, ossicular discontinuity, **otosclerosis**.
- Sensorineural: presbycusis, noise, **ototoxicity**, Ménière's, **vestibular schwannoma**, congenital/infective.
- **Unilateral or asymmetric SNHL demands imaging of the internal auditory meatus.**

| Cause | Discriminating features |
| --- | --- |
| **Conductive** — wax / foreign body | Sudden, after cotton bud or water; obvious on otoscopy. **⚑ Do not irrigate** with perforation, grommet, previous ear surgery or only-hearing ear — cerumenolytic then microsuction |
| Otitis media with effusion | Painless, dull/retracted drum, **Type B tympanogram**, inattentive child |
| Perforation; ossicular discontinuity | Visible defect; or post-traumatic/post-infective large gap with **Ad tympanogram** |
| **Otosclerosis** | Young–middle-aged adult, often female, worse in pregnancy, family history, **paracusis Willisii**, normal drum ± Schwartze sign, **Carhart notch**; aid or stapedotomy |
| **Sensorineural** — presbycusis / noise | Bilateral, gradual; high-frequency slope vs **4 kHz notch** with exposure history |
| **Ototoxicity** | Aminoglycosides (gentamicin vestibulotoxic; amikacin/neomycin cochleotoxic), platinum chemotherapy (cisplatin > carboplatin), loop diuretics (often reversible), high-dose salicylates/quinine (reversible tinnitus), high-dose macrolides. ⚑ Also **aminoglycoside drops through a perforation or grommet** |
| **Ménière's disease** | Recurrent spontaneous vertigo ~20 min–12 h, **fluctuating low/mid-frequency SNHL**, tinnitus and fullness, same ear |
| **Vestibular schwannoma** | **Unilateral** SNHL/tinnitus, discrimination worse than the PTA predicts, unsteadiness; **trigeminal involvement only with larger tumours — but when it comes, a reduced corneal reflex is its earliest sign** (§2.8), facial nerve signs later still; **MRI IAM with gadolinium** |
| Congenital / infective | Congenital CMV (commonest non-genetic), GJB2/connexin-26, rubella; acquired — bacterial meningitis (⚑ urgent implant referral before cochlear ossification), mumps, measles. Universal Australian newborn screening detects most congenital cases |

#### 1.6 Sudden sensorineural hearing loss

**The short version**
- Definition: SNHL of **≥30 dB across ≥3 consecutive frequencies within 72 hours**, usually unilateral.
- ⚑ **Weber lateralises AWAY from the affected ear; Rinne stays positive bilaterally** — and the ear looks entirely normal.
- **An otological emergency**, not wait-and-see: audiology same/next day, ENT referral, **high-dose corticosteroids as soon as possible, ideally within 14 days** of onset.
- **MRI of the internal auditory meatus** in all cases to exclude vestibular schwannoma.
- Recovery falls sharply with delay; salvage **intratympanic steroids** if systemic therapy fails or beyond ~2 weeks.

**Why it is missed.** The ear looks normal, so the loss is mislabelled as wax, "fluid" or infection. Most notice it on waking or on lifting the phone to that ear; many report fullness and tinnitus, and up to a third have vertigo (worse prognosis). **Confirm with tuning forks and an urgent audiogram** — do not reassure and review in a fortnight.

**Management.**
- **Urgent PTA** to confirm and quantify; do not delay steroids awaiting imaging.
- **Systemic corticosteroids**: prednisolone approximately **1 mg/kg/day (max ~60 mg) for 7–14 days, then taper**. Benefit is greatest within days, falls markedly after ~2 weeks, occasionally reported to 6 weeks. Counsel re diabetes, dyspepsia, mood, sleep.
- **Intratympanic steroid** as salvage after failed systemic therapy, or first-line if systemic steroids are contraindicated.
- **MRI IAM with contrast** (or ABR/audiometric follow-up if MRI not possible) for retrocochlear pathology. ⚑ **CT head is not the test**; routine screening bloods are not recommended.
- ⚑ **Do not** routinely prescribe antivirals, vasodilators, thrombolytics or antioxidants.
- Repeat audiometry at end of treatment and within ~6 months; refer for rehabilitation if loss persists.

#### 1.7 Presbycusis, noise-induced loss, hearing aids and implants

**The short version**
- Presbycusis: bilateral, symmetrical, gradual, high-frequency-first SNHL of ageing; *"I hear but can't understand,"* worst in background noise.
- Noise-induced loss: cumulative, permanent, **preventable** — 4 kHz notch; protection and exposure limits are the intervention.
- Untreated hearing loss is linked with isolation, depression and cognitive decline — a reason to treat, not just document.
- Hearing aids first; **bone-conduction devices** for conductive/mixed loss or chronically discharging ears.
- ⚑ **60/60 rule** for adult cochlear implant referral: better-ear unaided PTA **≥60 dB HL** and best-ear monosyllabic word score **≤60%** — a far lower bar than the old "profound deafness" mindset.

Management = amplification plus communication strategy (face the person, reduce background noise, assistive listening devices). Australian access: the Hearing Services Program funds devices for pensioner concession card holders and DVA clients; Hearing Australia covers under-26s, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people **aged 50+** (or any age if in a Community Development Program). Profound bilateral SNHL found on newborn screening is implanted in infancy, and ⚑ **post-meningitic deafness needs immediate referral**, since cochlear ossification can preclude electrode insertion within weeks.

#### 1.8 Tinnitus

**The short version**
- Most tinnitus is **bilateral, non-pulsatile, subjective** and accompanies presbycusis or noise damage — explain, treat the hearing loss, address sleep and anxiety.
- ⚑ **Investigate: unilateral tinnitus; pulsatile tinnitus; tinnitus with asymmetric hearing loss; tinnitus with neurological signs; tinnitus with sudden hearing loss.**
- Unilateral/asymmetric → **audiogram + MRI IAM** (vestibular schwannoma).
- Pulsatile → vascular: glomus tumour (red retrotympanic mass), dural AV fistula, carotid stenosis/dissection, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, high-output states (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy). Examine the drum, auscultate neck and mastoid, check BP and fundi; CT/MR angiography.
- Benign subjective tinnitus: audiology, hearing aids (amplification alone often reduces awareness), sound enrichment, CBT for distress, sleep hygiene, review ototoxic and stimulant drugs. No drug reliably abolishes tinnitus — do not start benzodiazepines.

#### 1.9 Perforation, barotrauma, temporal bone fracture and otorrhoea

**The short version**
- Traumatic perforations (slap, blast, cotton bud, water) mostly **heal spontaneously** within weeks to ~3 months.
- ⚑ **Keep the ear dry**; no drops unless infected, and then non-ototoxic ones.
- **Myringoplasty** if the perforation persists beyond ~3 months, causes recurrent infection or significant hearing loss, or the patient needs water exposure.
- Barotrauma: descent/flying with a blocked ET → retraction, haemotympanum, rarely perforation; treat the nasal cause, analgesia, autoinflation.
- Post-trauma **clear otorrhoea = CSF until proven otherwise** — base-of-skull fracture, not an ear infection.

**Perforation.** Record site, size and hearing; larger and posterior perforations lose more. New **vertigo, marked SNHL or facial weakness** after ear trauma suggests ossicular disruption into the oval window or perilymph fistula — urgent ENT.

**Temporal bone fracture.** Suspect with head injury plus **Battle's sign**, haemotympanum, canal bleeding, CSF otorrhoea, facial palsy or hearing loss.

| Feature | Longitudinal (~80%) | Transverse (~20%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Lateral blow | Frontal/occipital blow |
| Hearing loss | **Conductive** (ossicular, haemotympanum) | **Sensorineural** (otic capsule) |
| Vertigo | Uncommon | Common, severe |
| Facial palsy | ~20%, often delayed | ~50%, often immediate |
| Otorrhagia | Common, canal laceration | Less common; drum often intact |

Management: do not syringe, pack or instil drops; head-injury care first; HRCT temporal bone; audiometry once stable. **Immediate complete facial palsy** needs urgent ENT (± decompression); delayed or incomplete palsy is managed conservatively with corticosteroids and eye protection. Most traumatic CSF leaks settle conservatively; persistent leaks need repair. ⚑ Prophylactic antibiotics are not routine.

#### Rapid-Recall Discriminators

| Cue | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Pain on pressing the tragus / pulling the pinna | Otitis externa (not otitis media) |
| Weber to the left, Rinne negative on the left | Left conductive hearing loss |
| Weber to the left, Rinne positive both ears | Right sensorineural hearing loss |
| Diabetic, nocturnal otalgia, granulation at the bony–cartilaginous junction | Necrotising otitis externa — *Pseudomonas* |
| Type B tympanogram, normal vs large canal volume | Effusion vs perforation/patent grommet |
| Bone conduction dip at 2 kHz with an air–bone gap | Carhart notch — otosclerosis |
| Painless foul scanty discharge + attic crust + conductive loss | Cholesteatoma |
| Unilateral loss over hours, normal drum, Weber to the *good* ear | Sudden SNHL — steroids urgently, MRI IAM |
| Steroid window in sudden SNHL | ASAP, ideally within 14 days of onset |
| First-line antibiotic for AOM (non-Indigenous child) | Amoxicillin 15 mg/kg (max 500 mg) 8-hourly, 5 days |
| AOM in an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander child | Always treat — amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day, 7 days |
| Discharging ear with perforation or grommet: which drops | Ciprofloxacin — avoid aminoglycosides |
| Postauricular swelling, pinna pushed down and out | Acute mastoiditis |
| Fluctuating low-frequency SNHL + vertigo 20 min–12 h + fullness | Ménière's disease |
| Pulsatile tinnitus with a red retrotympanic mass | Glomus tumour |
| Better-ear PTA ≥60 dB and word score ≤60% | Refer for implant assessment (60/60 rule) |
| Clear fluid from the ear after head injury | CSF otorrhoea — skull base fracture |
