## general practice models


### General Practice (ANU) — general practice models

#### 5 DOMAINS OF GENERAL PRACTICE (MNEUMONIC CAPPO)

- C- communication skills and patient-doctor relationship
- A- applied professional knowledge and skills
- P- population health and the context of general practice
- P- professional and ethical role
- O- organizational and legal dimensions

#### WHO four features of GP

- First contact access for each new need
- Long-term person focused care (not disease focused)
- Comprehensive care
- Co-ordinated care when it must be sought elsewhere

#### Six characteristics of GP

- Holistic and Comprehensive- physical, biopsychosocial, financial, event ethical/legal/political considerations
- Patient-centred
- Family-centred
- Community-based- GPs are usually the first line of contact + advocacy
- Continuity of care- strong formation of relationships
- Coordination of care- interdisciplinary, leadership, management, advocacy, referrals, common clinical record, system integration, health care home, share care pays

### Justice & Correctional Health — Justice and Correctional Health — general practice models

#### Population Characteristics of People in Custody

22.8% of adults in NSW prisons are Aboriginal (48% of young people in custody). 46% of young people entering custody have borderline or lower intellectual functioning; 14% have functioning consistent with an intellectual disability — roughly 10× the rate in the general youth population.

52% of male prisoners and 45% of female prisoners left school without a qualification; around half of male and two-thirds of female prisoners were unemployed; 60% are not functionally literate or numerate. 18% of prisoners had one or both parents also in prison; 22% of males, 28% of females, and 45% of Aboriginal people were placed in out-of-home care as a child.

**Substance use** — daily/near-daily alcohol use, illicit drug use (ever used and ever injected), and current smoking are all substantially higher than the general population, particularly among Aboriginal people in custody.

#### Disease Characteristics of the Custodial Population

**Mental health** — 15% of men and 20% of women in custody have had a psychiatric inpatient admission; roughly a third of men and half of women carry a diagnosis of depression or an affective disorder; rates of suicide attempt are very high.

**Physical health** — kidney disease, heart disease, and asthma are all more prevalent than in the general population, especially among Aboriginal people in custody.

**Blood-borne viruses and STIs** — hepatitis B and hepatitis C prevalence are substantially elevated compared with the general population, particularly in Aboriginal custodial populations.

#### Specific Patient Characteristics to Consider

Poor literacy; poor decision-making skills and reasoning; often poor self-care/compliance; high burden of disease; advanced pathology at presentation (illness often goes unaddressed until it is severe); multiple problems across several domains simultaneously; high prevalence of substance abuse.

#### The Custodial Environment

Correctional facilities are run for **security, not health** — by Corrective Services NSW / Youth Justice, not by a health service. This means security precautions (gates, escorts, biometric scanning, metal detectors, duress alarms, no mobile phones) shape every consultation. Counter-intuitively, correctional health work is statistically **safer** than working in general hospitals.

**Effects on care delivery:**
- Security processes slow access to patients and throughput.
- The area is not politically popular, so funding is often limited.
- Patients move between facilities frequently, undermining continuity of care.
- Multiple small, scattered clinics limit access to equipment and specialist services, and are inefficient for small numbers of patients in remote/secure locations.

**Patient pathway:** arrest → police cell (basic assessment; unstable patients go to hospital ED; medications can usually be commenced; liaison with community GP if needed) → reception centre (full nursing clinical assessment plus Corrective Services processing — offence, risk, classification/segregation) → custodial centre, where patients are triaged into primary/women's health, mental health, population health, drug & alcohol, adolescent health, Aboriginal health, aged care, and chronic care programs, with pathways out to community services, external hospitals, and the Long Bay Hospital medical/surgical and aged-care/rehabilitation units.

#### Why Treat People in Custody

Framed around four points: it's **safer** (a functioning health service is part of what allows the justice system to keep people in custody appropriately); it's **cost-effective** (untreated disease in a concentrated, high-burden population is expensive and late-diagnosed); it's **beneficial to all of society** (poor prisoner health has whole-of-society costs — family, community, child health, downstream burden on other agencies); and it's simply a **right** — prisoners and detainees have the same right to access, equity, and quality of health care as the general population (Australian Medical Association position statement).

#### Practitioner Approach to Consulting in Custody

1. **Mindset** — treat people in custody because it's safer, cheaper, beneficial to all of us, and right.
2. **Know your objective** — whole-patient care (WHO definition: physical, mental, and social wellbeing, not merely absence of disease), for people who often have multiple problems and may not care well for themselves.
3. **Safety** — always follow Corrective Services officer directions; officer positioning; sit nearest the door; leave the room at any warning sign; extra caution with psychosis or severe mental illness.
4. **General approach** — be respectful, patient, calm; listen; maintain firm boundaries; be concrete, avoid humour/sarcasm; allow extra consultation time; a thorough clinical assessment matters more here since tests and specialist referral are harder to arrange.
5. **Treat the individual** — tailor to the patient's expectations, social context, education level, and personality; irrational or difficult behaviour can be part of the presentation itself (analogous to managing a distressed child) rather than something to react to personally.
6. **Realism** — drugs don't solve social problems and may worsen them; benzodiazepines and opiates are frequently misused in this setting; special favours (even well-intentioned ones like a shoe certificate) can create real safety risk; patients may not always be truthful; health is often not the patient's top priority — survival is; aim for the best achievable outcome given the circumstances.
7. **Special skills** — chronic disease management; mental health (mental state exams, self-harm risk, trauma-informed counselling); addiction medicine (substance use, withdrawal, opioid substitution); blood-borne virus/STI management; IV access; trauma management.
8. **Team work** — multidisciplinary, spanning medical officers, nursing, allied health, Aboriginal health workers, and administrative/executive staff, coordinated with NSW Health and Commonwealth structures.
9. **Legal awareness** — practise meticulous documentation. All deaths in custody are Coroner's cases; patients typically have legal representation and may request reports or make complaints hoping to influence release decisions. "Good documentation, good defence; poor documentation, poor defence; no documentation, no defence."

#### Systemic Challenges

Acceptance (discrimination against both patients and staff working in the field); staffing (attracting clinicians interested in high-quality correctional care, teaching, and research); funding (a politically unattractive area to resource); and setting standards (defining models of care for a unique system funded through agencies more used to dealing with secondary/tertiary care).
