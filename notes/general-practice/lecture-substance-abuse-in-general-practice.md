## Lecture: substance abuse in general practice


### General Practice (ANU) — Lecture: substance abuse in general practice

**Alcohol most common (41%),** then Cannabis (24%), Amphetamines (14%), Heroin (8%)

#### Alcohol

- Identify hazardous or harmful
- drinking and can use structured questionnaires e.g. AUDIT-C
- HIGH RISK GROUPS: adolescents, young adults, pregnant women/planning pregnancy, illicit drug users, FHx, medical conditions made worse by EtOH (HT, chronic liver disease, depression/anxiety)
- BRIEF INTERVENTIONS: helpful for people at risk or as a prelude to engage dependent drinkers in treatment; can reduce harmful consumption by ~1/3
- ALCOHOL DEPENDENCE: ask about cravings, consequences and interference with life, tolerance
- ALCOHOL USE DISORDERS FIT CHRONIC DISEASE MODEL: intensive intervention, referral, medication (acamprosate, disulfiram, naltrexone), manage comorbidities and psychosocial aspects, adherence to treatment

#### Opioid dependence

- Chronic disorder, relapse and remission over many years
- HIGH MORTALITY: 15 X that expected in population same age/sex who do not inject drugs
- HEALTH EVENTS IVDU: non-fatal overdoses, arrested, mental health problems

#### Prescription drug misuse

- FORMS: injecting oral drug, selling on street, over-using prescribed amount, used for non-intended purpose
- MOST COMMONLY MISUSED: opioids, benzodiazepines, quetiapine
- APPROACH: say no, discharge patients and provide non-SM related, engage patient in treatment plan
- Initial assessment: reasons for codeine, benefits/harms, degree of dependence
- Treatment options: patient preference, practicalities (cost and availability)
- Management: opioid replacement therapy (methadone, buprenorphine)
- Shown in over 30 RCTs to mitigate risk
- Stop IVDU/use of non-prescribed drugs, and/or reduce injecting and drug risk behaviour
- 33,988 clients, 1,472 prescribers, 2011 dosing points on snapshot day, June 2014
- Prescribing authority: authorised GP, support from drug treatment services
- CONTROLLED PRESCRIBING STRATEGIES
- Controlled quantities (needed and safe)
- Controlled dispensing
- Private scripts/authority scripts for ↑ quantities
- Request patients obtain their prescriptions from one pharmacy
- Obtain a fuller picture of perceptions
- Inform that they will need to see same GP for their prescription

#### Medicare information access

- Prescription shopping info service 24h hotline (don’t need patient consent)
- PATIENT COMES UP IF IN ANY 3-MONTH PERIOD: prescriptions from 6 or more prescribers, obtains 50 or more PBS drugs, or obtains 25 or more drugs targeted by Medicare

#### Key Principles

- NON-JUDGEMENTAL, EMPATHIC APPROACH
- IDENTIFY AND ACKNOWLEDGE SUBSTANCE MISUSE
- PROVIDE EVIDENCE BASED MANAGEMENT
- SUBSTANCE ABUSE IS A CHRONIC DISEASE: expect relapse

### Pre-Travel Health Assessment — Pre-Travel Health Assessment — Diabetes and VTE Risk — Lecture: substance abuse in general practice

#### Case Framing

A 63-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes (metformin + biphasic insulin, HbA1c 7.4%), hypertension, and a **previous post-operative DVT** presents for pre-travel advice ahead of a month-long trip to Vietnam. This combination — known VTE history plus a long-haul flight — is the classic trigger for a pre-travel thromboprophylaxis and diabetes-management consultation.

#### Planning Stage (4–6 Weeks Before Departure)

See the GP early enough to assess current diabetic control, determine vaccination needs, and adjust the medication regimen well in advance. Dietary planning (e.g. lower-carbohydrate meals) and general advice on preventing acute diabetic complications while travelling.

#### Insulin Adjustment for Time-Zone Travel

- **Travelling east** (shorter day) → lower the insulin dose.
- **Travelling west** (longer day) → increase the insulin dose.
- Patients on an insulin pump may need to switch to a basal-bolus regimen, allowing for slightly higher blood glucose tolerance during transit.
- Split doses across the flight so total daily insulin is unchanged over 24 hours; resume the normal regimen after arrival.

#### In-Flight and Trip Considerations

Travel raises hypoglycaemia risk through disrupted routine, unpredictable meal timing, and increased physical activity. Preventive strategies: carry snacks plus glucose tablets/gel, monitor BGL regularly (note that extreme temperatures affect glucometer strip accuracy), and identify an English-speaking physician at the destination in advance.

**What to carry (as hand luggage, in original manufacturer-labelled packaging):** glucometer; all medications and insulin; hypoglycaemia management supplies including a glucagon emergency kit; fast-acting carbohydrate; medical identification for emergencies. Skin pricks and X-ray screening of diabetic supplies are both fine at security. A doctor's letter is good practice but is not guaranteed to be accepted at every airport.

#### The VTE Risk Layer

Long-haul flight plus a prior personal history of DVT is a recognised risk stack for travel-associated VTE, independent of the diabetes management above. This is the context in which pre-travel thromboprophylaxis (mechanical measures, hydration and mobility advice, and consideration of pharmacological prophylaxis for higher-risk patients) should be discussed alongside the glycaemic planning.
